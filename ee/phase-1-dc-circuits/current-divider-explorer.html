Current Divider Explorer

Two (or more) resistors in parallel, one current going in, multiple currents coming out. The current splits inversely with resistance — the smaller R gets the bigger share. This visual is the parallel-circuit counterpart to the voltage divider, plus everything that makes parallel networks subtle: the conductance form, the tolerance trap that ruins parallel-LED designs, and the shunt-resistor measurement trick.

Three things to take away: (1) the two-resistor formula flips relative to the voltage divider — the current through R1 is proportional to R2, not R1, and that single sign-error sinks more homework problems than any other DC-circuit mistake; (2) the conductance form I_k = I_total · G_k / ΣG is the same shape as the voltage divider, so once you see currents as "voltage drops across conductances," parallel networks stop feeling foreign; (3) real parallel circuits don't share current the way the math says — resistor tolerance, copper trace resistance, and LED Vf spread all add up, and the divider formula tells you exactly how bad it will be.

How the formula falls out (KCL + Ohm)

N resistors in parallel between two nodes; the same voltage V appears across every one. The total current is the sum of branch currents (KCL at the top node).

1. All branches share the same V (definition of parallel): V = I_k · R_k = I_j · R_j for any j, k 2. KCL at the source node: I_total = ΣI_k 3. Substitute V = I_total · R_parallel (the parallel equivalent): V = I_total · (1 / ΣG), where G_k = 1/R_k 4. Branch current = V / R_k = V · G_k: → I_k = I_total · G_k / ΣG (the always-true form) 5. For exactly two branches: G_1 / (G_1 + G_2) = (1/R_1) / (1/R_1 + 1/R_2) = R_2 / (R_1 + R_2) 6. So the two-resistor shortcut is: → I_R1 = I_total · R_2 / (R_1 + R_2) (note: R_2 in the numerator, not R_1)

The numerator is the other resistor. That's the entire reason students mess this up: the voltage-divider uses the resistor you're tapping across; the current-divider uses the resistor on the other branch. Both are correct — they have to be, because a small R sees a big voltage drop (voltage divider) and hogs the current (current divider).

Duality with the voltage divider

The two formulas are mirror images of each other. Voltage divider has a series loop with one current; current divider has a parallel network with one voltage. Swap the roles, swap the formulas:

The conductance form is the unifying picture. If you write voltage-divider in resistance and current-divider in conductance, the two formulas are literally identical: X_k / X_total = element_k / Σelement. That's worth a minute of staring.

The tolerance trap — why parallel LEDs almost always need individual resistors

Real resistors carry a tolerance (1 %, 5 %, 10 %). Two parallel branches with the same nominal resistance can have very different actual resistances, and the current-divider formula tells you exactly how the imbalance lands.

For two equal-nominal resistors at tolerance ±t, the worst case puts one at R(1−t) and the other at R(1+t). The branch currents become:

I_low / I_total = (1 + t) / 2  ·  I_high / I_total = (1 − t) / 2 Current ratio I_low : I_high = (1 + t) : (1 − t) At t = 5 %: ratio = 1.05 : 0.95 → 10.5 % imbalance between the two LEDs At t = 1 %: ratio = 1.01 : 0.99 → ~2 % imbalance — usually invisible to the eye

Now add the LED Vf spread. Two "identical" 20 mA white LEDs from the same reel can differ by 100 mV in Vf at 20 mA. If you drive two LEDs from a single series resistor — i.e., the LEDs are the parallel branches and the resistor is upstream — the LED with the lower Vf takes much more than half the current, can heat up, drop further in Vf (negative tempco), take even more current, and thermally run away. This is why parallel-LED-with-one-resistor circuits are forbidden in any production design. Each LED gets its own current-limiting resistor; the parallel-current-divider math only describes resistors, and even there it punishes you for being lazy.

Worked example 1 — Shunt resistor for current measurement

The problem

You want to measure the current flowing into your robot's motor driver — call it 0–5 A. You don't have a Hall-effect sensor, but you do have an INA-style differential amp with a ±20 mV input range and a 12-bit ADC. The classic answer is a shunt resistor: a very small R in series with the load. The current flows through both the load and the shunt (they're in series, not parallel) — but the divider concept still applies because the shunt is in parallel with the ADC's measurement node.

Why it's a parallel problem in disguise

Putting an ADC across a shunt looks like two paths from V+ to V−: through the shunt (carries the real motor current) and through the ADC's input impedance (typically MΩ–GΩ, carries almost nothing). The current-divider math tells you how much the ADC "steals":

I_through_shunt = I_total · R_ADC / (R_shunt + R_ADC) With R_shunt = 5 mΩ and R_ADC = 10 MΩ: ratio = 10·10⁶ / (10·10⁶ + 0.005) ≈ 1.0000000005(ADC steals 5 pA of every amp)

The shunt sizing math

Full-scale current: 5 A · 5 mΩ = 25 mV (over the INA's 20 mV range — too high, scale down) Pick 3 mΩ shunt: 5 A · 3 mΩ = 15 mV at full scale (75 % of range — comfortable headroom) Power dissipated in shunt at 5 A: P = I² · R = 25 A² · 0.003 Ω = 75 mW (0805 chip resistor handles > 100 mW with airflow) ADC resolution: 15 mV / 4095 = 3.66 µV/count → 3.66 µV / 3 mΩ = 1.22 mA/count (usable down to ~5 mA with averaging)

Why the divider trick matters here

If you instead used a 1 Ω shunt for "easy" 5 V/A scaling, the shunt would dissipate 25 W at 5 A — a power resistor with a heat-sink — and the shunt would also drop the motor's supply voltage by 5 V, which usually exceeds your headroom. The shunt resistor's smallness is what keeps the parallel-divider error and the power loss both negligible. The divider math justifies why "1 mΩ" isn't a typo.

Worked example 2 — Three parallel power resistors sharing 200 W

The problem

You're building a dynamic brake for a 48 V BLDC motor — when the controller switches the motor into regen, you dump the energy into a resistor bank. Total power budget: 200 W continuous, voltage at the bank: 48 V. You have a drawer full of 50 W, 33 Ω wirewound resistors. How many in parallel, and what's the real current per resistor?

The arithmetic (N equal resistors)

R_required ≤ V² / P = 48² / 200 = 11.5 Ω N = 33 / 11.5 ≈ 2.9 → 3 in parallel: R_par = 33 / 3 = 11.0 Ω (rounds up so we don't exceed 200 W) Total current at 48 V: I_total = 48 / 11 = 4.36 A Per-resistor current (ideal split): 4.36 / 3 = 1.45 A → P = 1.45² · 33 = 69.5 W per resistor ⚠ exceeds 50 W rating

The trap and the fix

69.5 W per resistor in a "50 W" package = thermal runaway and char marks. Per-resistor power rating, not bank total, is what matters. The wrong instinct: "add more in parallel." That doesn't help — each resistor still sees 48 V across it, so P = V²/R = 48²/33 = 69.8 W per resistor regardless of how many you put in parallel. The fix that does work: a series-parallel arrangement. Two 33 Ω in series across 48 V → each sees 24 V, I = 0.727 A, P = I²R = 17.5 W per resistor ✓. To handle 200 W total, you need 200 / (2·17.5) ≈ 6 such pairs → 12 resistors total. Or just pick 100 W rated parts and keep 3 in parallel. The divider insight is that the bank's resistance sets total current; the per-element dissipation is what kills you, and it's set by V across each element — not by the parallel count.

Tolerance check

±5 % wirewound in the 2-series-by-6-parallel arrangement: a low-R branch carries ~5 % more current → ~10 % more power → 19 W per resistor (still under 50 W). 100 W rated parts in the simple 3-parallel arrangement: worst-case branch gets ~5 % more share = 73 W per resistor — safe. Either works; the series-parallel uses cheap parts at the cost of resistor count.

Worked example 3 — Reading a current divider out of a schematic

The setup

A 12 V supply feeds a 2 A load. In parallel with the load sits a 1 kΩ "bleeder" resistor (used to discharge the supply's bulk capacitor when the load is removed). What's the current in each branch?

The arithmetic

R_load = V / I = 12 / 2 = 6 Ω (the load's equivalent resistance at this operating point) G_load = 1 / 6 = 0.1667 S  ·  G_bleed = 1 / 1000 = 0.001 S I_load = I_total · G_load / (G_load + G_bleed) — but here you're told I_load directly I_bleed at 12 V: 12 / 1000 = 12 mA (0.6 % of the 2 A load — negligible during normal operation) Bleeder power: 12² / 1000 = 144 mW (a 1/4 W resistor is the minimum, 1/2 W gives margin)

The insight

The 1 kΩ bleeder is a parallel-current divider, but its job isn't to "share" current during normal operation — it's to be there when the load disappears. With the load gone, the bulk capacitor (say 1000 µF) discharges through the bleeder with τ = R · C = 1000 · 1·10⁻³ = 1 s, so within 5 s the cap is safe to touch. Without the bleeder, the cap can sit at 12 V for hours. The divider math confirms the bleeder is harmless when the load is connected; the time-constant math justifies why it's there at all.

What to notice

  • Tiny R hogs current. Drag R1 to its minimum; almost all the source current goes through it. This is the parallel dual of "tiny R sees tiny voltage drop in a series loop." Both are statements about conductance dominance.
  • The conductance form generalizes; the resistance shortcut only works for two branches. Try increasing N past 2 — the "R₂ / (R1+R2)" shortcut from the box above stops applying, and only the G_k / ΣG form keeps making sense. Memorize the conductance form first; the two-resistor case is a corollary.
  • Equal-R branches give equal current. Set R1 = R2 = R3; each branch carries 1/N of the total. This is the only configuration where parallel-LED-with-one-resistor "looks like it works" — and even then, only on the bench, not in production.
  • R_parallel is always less than the smallest branch. Adding more paths can only increase total conductance, so total resistance only goes down. Adding one more 100 Ω in parallel with an existing 1 Ω drops R_par from 1 Ω to 0.99 Ω — barely. Adding 1 Ω in parallel with 1 Ω drops it to 0.5 Ω. Adding more small resistors matters; piling on big ones doesn't.
  • The tolerance band sets the worst-case imbalance. Try the tolerance dropdown: ±10 % branches can be 22 % apart in current; ±0.1 % branches are tighter than your meter can measure. If your design needs precise current sharing (parallel power transistors, current mirrors, ladder DACs), use better resistors or trim with an op-amp.
  • Power doesn't divide proportionally to current — it divides as I²R, which is steeper. A branch carrying 2× the current dissipates 4× the power if R is the same, or 2× if R is half. The branch with the smallest R hogs both the current and the heat — bad if that resistor is also the smallest physical part. Size for the worst-case branch, not the bank average.

Where you'll see this circuit

Real uses

  • Shunt resistor + ADC — measure motor current by sensing voltage across a very small R. Worked example 1.
  • Parallel power resistors — distribute heat across multiple smaller packages. Worked example 2.
  • Bleeder across a bulk capacitor — drains the cap when the load is removed. Worked example 3.
  • Parallel MOSFETs sharing current — same trap as parallel LEDs, but worse: MOSFETs have negative R_DS(on) tempco below saturation, so the hot one takes more current and runs hotter. Solution: source/emitter ballast resistors (small R in series with each device's source) to enforce sharing via local feedback.
  • Op-amp summing junction — currents from N input branches sum at a virtual ground; the feedback resistor converts the summed current back to voltage. The "divider" here is current-into-the-summing-node from each input, and the analysis uses KCL identically.
  • Ladder DAC / R-2R — the R-2R ladder is a beautifully recursive current divider, where each rung halves the current relative to the one above it.
  • PCB power-plane copper — multiple via paths in parallel from a plane to a pin form a current divider; minimum vias for 1 A through 0.5 oz copper is ~4, calculated by the same conductance-sum.
  • Decoupling capacitor stacks — multiple caps of different values (10 µF + 100 nF + 1 nF) in parallel divide the load's high-frequency current pulse across paths, with each cap dominating in its own frequency band. See EE 7 — Decoupling Caps.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

2-branch shortcut
I_R1 = I_total · R_2 / (R_1 + R_2)
N-branch (always)
I_k = I_total · G_k / ΣG
Parallel R
1 / R_par = Σ 1 / R_k
Product over sum (2)
R_par = R_1 · R_2 / (R_1 + R_2)
N equal R
R_par = R / N
Shared voltage
V = I_total · R_par = I_k · R_k
Branch power
P_k = I_k² · R_k = V² / R_k

SHORTCUTS

  • Two equal R → R/2, current splits 50/50.
  • N equal R → R/N, current = I_total/N each.
  • Big R in parallel with small R → R_par ≈ small R; the big R is almost invisible.
  • Numerator (2-branch) is the other resistor — opposite convention from voltage divider.
  • Adding paths only lowers R_par; never raises it.
  • For LED current sharing: each LED gets its own resistor, always.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Numerator-resistor swap — using R1 in the numerator for I_R1. The 2-branch formula is I_R1 = I·R2/(R1+R2). When in doubt, fall back to conductance: I_k ∝ G_k.
  • Parallel LEDs from one resistor — works on the bench, fails in production. Vf spread + negative tempco causes thermal runaway. Use one resistor per LED, or a constant-current driver.
  • Parallel MOSFETs without ballast — same failure mode below saturation. Use source resistors or driver IC with current-balance feedback.
  • Sizing per bank instead of per element — "200 W bank" with 4× 50 W resistors only works if each branch stays under 50 W. Worst-case branch sets the rating.
  • Ignoring trace/wire resistance — for very small load R, the milli-ohms of copper trace are part of the divider. A 5 mΩ shunt with 2 mΩ of trace inline is now a 7 mΩ shunt, 40 % calibration error.
  • Forgetting the ADC isn't an open — ADC input impedance during sample is finite (10 kΩ–100 MΩ depending on architecture). Usually negligible for voltage measurement, but check the datasheet.
