Regime & assumptions

Regime
linear-resistive DC steady-state Ohmic R
Assumes
R is independent of V and I (no junction effects, no temperature drift); R is linear over the operating range; the resistor can dissipate the calculated P without thermal damage
Breaks down when
The "resistor" is actually a junction device (diodes, transistors, LEDs are non-Ohmic — V/I is nonlinear); self-heating shifts R via temperature coefficient (positive TC for most metals, negative for NTC thermistors); calculated P exceeds the resistor wattage rating (open-circuit failure); breakdown voltage exceeded (resistor arcs or burns)

First time seeing this? Think water: V (voltage, in volts) is the pressure that pushes charge along the wire. I (current, in amps) is the flow rate of charge. R (resistance, in ohms) is the pipe's narrowness — how hard it is for the flow to get through. P (power, in watts) is how much heat the resistor dumps as the charge squeezes through. Ohm's law says I = V / R; power follows as P = V·I.

This visual teaches the three-and-four variable game: given any two of {V, I, R, P}, the other two follow from algebra. The water analogy below shows why pressure (V) and flow (I) trade off against pipe narrowing (R). The unit table cures the "is that 4.7 kΩ or 4700 Ω?" hesitation that slows down circuit reading.

What to notice

  • All three P forms agree — pick the one matching the variables you have. The three cards always show the same value.
  • R on log scale — that's because real circuits span 10 Ω to 10 MΩ. A linear slider would waste 99% of its travel on values above 10 kΩ.
  • E12 snap — the resistor jumps to the nearest E12 value (10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82 ×10ⁿ). Real parts live on this grid.
  • Water analogy breaks where — capacitors and inductors. Water has no "phase-shifted flow." Use V/I/R intuition only for resistive DC.
  • Power scales with V² — double V at fixed R → 4× the power. If a microcontroller pin accidentally connects to ground (R becomes ~0.05 Ω), P = V²/R explodes: 3.3² / 0.05 ≈ 220 W in a chip rated for tens of milliwatts. That's why a shorted GPIO burns out in seconds.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

Ohm
V = I · R
solve I
I = V / R
solve R
R = V / I
Power
P = V · I = I² · R = V² / R

SCALE BY HEART

  • mA / µA — 1 mA = 10⁻³ A; 1 µA = 10⁻⁶ A.
  • kΩ / MΩ — 1 kΩ = 10³ Ω; 1 MΩ = 10⁶ Ω.
  • LED — ≈10–20 mA typical; never connect bare to a rail.
  • 3.3 V / 1 kΩ = 3.3 mA — anchor for sanity checks.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Unit mix — V/Ω gives A, not mA. Convert before computing.
  • P = I²R and P = V²/R hold only for resistors (where V = IR). For LEDs, diodes, and ICs the V across them doesn't follow Ohm's law, so use P = V·I directly with the actual measured V across the device.
  • R on log scale — real parts span 10 Ω to 10 MΩ; don't trust a linear feel.
