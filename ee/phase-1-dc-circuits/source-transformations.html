Source Transformations

A practical voltage source (Vth in series with Rth) and a practical current source (In in parallel with Rn) are indistinguishable from outside as long as you set Vth = In · R and Rth = Rn. Source transformation is the trick of swapping one for the other to simplify analysis: convert all voltage sources to current sources to apply nodal, or all current sources to voltage sources to apply mesh; or chain transformations to collapse a multi-source network into a single Thévenin equivalent. This is also the bridge between Thévenin and Norton.

Three things to take away: (1) the math is just one equation — V = I · R relates the two equivalent representations, with R the same on both sides; (2) the inside is different — only the terminals match, so the power dissipated in the internal resistor changes (Vth²/R vs In²·R, dramatically different at no-load vs short-circuit), so don't use transformations to reason about internal heating; (3) chained transformations collapse complex networks — turn voltage sources with series R into current sources with parallel R, combine parallels, turn back into voltage sources with series, repeat until you have one source — this is how Thévenin-equivalence is taught.

The transformation in one equation

Practical voltage source: V_th in series with R. At open-circuit V = V_th; at short-circuit I = V_th / R. Practical current source: I_n in parallel with R. At open-circuit V = I_n · R; at short-circuit I = I_n. Set them equal at the terminals: → V_th = I_n · R, R_th = R_n. Then they behave identically on the outside. Both V_open · I_short = V_th · (V_th/R) = V_th²/R. The maximum power transferable is V_th² / (4·R) (at R_load = R), same number for either form.

Why "equivalent" is only an outside view

The two networks deliver identical voltage and current at the terminals for any external load — that's the definition of equivalence. But internally, they're profoundly different. Consider a 12 V / 1 Ω battery at no load:

Thévenin form (12 V in series with 1 Ω): no current → 0 W dissipated internally. Norton form (12 A current source in parallel with 1 Ω): the source still pumps 12 A; with no external load, all 12 A goes through the internal R → 1 Ω · 12² = 144 W dissipated internally! ⚠ The Norton form is an analytical fiction. A real 12 V battery has zero internal current at no-load. Use whichever form matches the physical device; use the other form for analysis only.

Worked example — chained transformation to find V_load

The setup

Two practical voltage sources, V1 = 12 V with R1 = 4 Ω, V2 = 9 V with R2 = 6 Ω, both feeding a common load R_L = 3 Ω. Find V across R_L the long way (mesh), then the short way (transform + combine).

Step 1 — Transform both V sources to I sources

Source 1: 12 V / 4 Ω = 3 A in parallel with 4 Ω Source 2: 9 V / 6 Ω = 1.5 A in parallel with 6 Ω

Step 2 — Now all three (3 A, 4 Ω, 1.5 A, 6 Ω, 3 Ω) are in parallel at the load node

Total current source: 3 + 1.5 = 4.5 A (KCL at the node) Total parallel resistance: 4 ‖ 6 ‖ 3 = 1/(1/4 + 1/6 + 1/3) = 1/0.75 = 1.333 Ω V_load = 4.5 A · 1.333 Ω = 6.00 V

Step 3 — Sanity-check with mesh analysis

Two-mesh circuit (one loop through V1, R1, R_L; one through V2, R2, R_L). Solving the matrix gives V_load = 6.00 V exactly. The transformation route was three lines vs nine — that's the whole point.

What to notice

  • R doesn't change. Vth/Rth and In/Rn share the same internal resistance. The transformation rotates the source, not the resistor.
  • Short circuit current is identical. Short the terminals of either form → both deliver V_th/R = I_n. That's the defining test.
  • Open-circuit voltage is identical. Disconnect any load → V_th in the Thévenin form, I_n · R in the Norton. Same number by definition.
  • Use Thévenin when V sources dominate (most circuits) and Norton when I sources dominate (transistor models, photodiodes).
  • Chaining works when sources can be combined. Voltage sources in series add (with sign). Current sources in parallel add (with sign). Use transformation to get to the topology where addition works.
  • Power inside the source is form-dependent. A real battery is closer to Thévenin form; a real photodiode is closer to Norton. Pick the form that matches reality if you care about internal heating.

Where you'll see this technique

Real uses

  • Mesh ↔ Nodal switching — current sources prefer nodal; voltage sources prefer mesh. Transform first, then pick the technique.
  • Thévenin extraction — reduce a complex sub-circuit to one V_th + R_th by repeated transform-and-combine.
  • Transistor small-signal — collector models naturally as Norton (g_m · v_be in parallel with r_o); transform to Thévenin for output-stage analysis.
  • Battery modeling — V_oc(SOC) in series with R_int(temperature, SOC, age). Internal R has a wider tolerance than V_oc.
  • Op-amp inverting input — incoming signals (each via R_in) Norton-transform into the summing junction (virtual ground); the feedback resistor converts the summed I back to V.
  • Cross-link: see Thévenin / Norton Explorer for the equivalent-circuit framing.

WHEN TO TRANSFORM

  • Want to apply nodal — convert all V sources to I sources first.
  • Want to apply mesh — convert all I sources to V sources first.
  • Chasing a Thévenin — chain transforms + parallel-combine.
  • Sources in series with same R → combine V's, sum stays a V source.
  • Sources in parallel with same R → transform first, then sum I's.

PITFALLS

  • Internal power isn't preserved. Vth²/R ≠ I_n²·R in general dissipation accounting — they're equivalent only at the terminals.
  • Ideal sources can't be transformed. An ideal V source has R = 0 → I_n = V/0 = ∞. An ideal I source has R = ∞ → V_th = ∞. Add a tiny series or shunt R first.
  • Sign of the source. The arrow direction (current source) and the + terminal (voltage source) must match across the transformation.
  • Internal node disappears. The series-R node in Thévenin form vanishes when you transform to Norton — sometimes that node is the one you wanted to measure.
  • Real batteries aren't quite Thévenin. R_int varies with SOC, T, age. Use a lookup table or refresh R_th periodically in firmware.
