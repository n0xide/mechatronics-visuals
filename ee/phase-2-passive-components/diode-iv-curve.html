Diode I-V Curve Explorer

A diode is a one-way valve for current — but the "one-way" is gentle, exponential, and family-dependent. The Shockley equation I = Is·(exp(V/(nVT)) − 1) compresses every flavor — Si, Schottky, LED, Ge — into one shape with three knobs: Is (leakage), n (ideality), and Vbr (reverse breakdown). Slide V_D, watch the current explode; compare Si vs Schottky vs LED; pick an operating point and read off the small-signal resistance rd = nVT / ID.

The Shockley equation, term by term

One equation, three constants, exponential explosion. Memorize the shape, not the numbers — the numbers come from the datasheet.

I = Is · ( exp(V_D / (n·V_T)) − 1 ) Is — saturation (reverse leakage) current. Si: ~1–10 nA. Schottky: ~1–10 µA. Ge: ~1 µA. LEDs: vanishingly small (10⁻²⁰ – 10⁻³⁰ A) because of the wide bandgap. n — ideality factor, 1 ≤ n ≤ 2 for Si junctions, n ≈ 1.0–1.1 for Schottky, n ≈ 2–3 for LEDs because recombination dominates in the wide bandgap. V_T = kT/q — thermal voltage. 25.7 mV at 25 °C, ~30 mV at 75 °C, ~40 mV at 200 °C. Always temperature-dependent. → The "−1" matters only near zero. Once V_D ≳ 4·V_T, the equation is just I ≈ Is·exp(V_D/(nVT)).

Why the curve looks the same shape every time

Pure exponentials look identical on a log-Y plot — they're just straight lines with different intercepts. That's why Si, Schottky, LED, Ge all look like the same curve shifted left or right. The shift is the difference in Is (and a little in n).

Take log of both sides: ln(I) ≈ ln(Is) + V_D / (n·V_T) Slope on log-Y: 1 / (n·V_T) — same for any diode with the same n, regardless of Is or material. Intercept: ln(Is) — sets where the line crosses 0 V. Decades per volt: 1 / (n·V_T · ln 10). At 25 °C and n=1: 16.9 decades/V; n=2: 8.4 decades/V. → Equivalently: n·V_T · ln(10) ≈ 60 mV/decade at 25 °C for n=1. The famous "60 mV per decade" of BJT transconductance.

Reverse breakdown — the cliff at the back

The exponential is only one side of the truth. Reverse-bias a diode hard enough and avalanche/Zener breakdown takes over, slamming current up at a fixed Vbr. For ordinary rectifiers, Vbr is a destructive limit (50 V, 100 V, 600 V). For Zener and TVS diodes, Vbr is the working voltage.

Worked example — 5 V supply, red LED at 10 mA

The setup

Drive a red LED (Vf ≈ 1.9 V at 10 mA from Lite-On LTL-307 datasheet, IF_max = 25 mA) from a 5 V Pico GPIO via a series resistor. Pick the resistor.

The arithmetic

KVL: 5 V − I·R − V_LED = 0 R = (5 − 1.9) / 0.010 = 310 Ω (exact) E12 pick: R = 330 Ω (closest, gives I = 9.4 mA, brightness loss ≈ −0.3 dB → eye can't tell) Power in R: I²·R = (0.0094)² · 330 = 29 mW (any 0805 SMD or 1/8 W through-hole is fine) Power in LED: V·I = 1.9 · 0.0094 = 18 mW (well under 60 mW spec)

Now the gotcha

If the GPIO drops by 0.3 V (sourcing 10 mA from a 3.3 V Pico through internal pull-up — not the same as a real 5 V supply): I drops to ~9.1 mA. If V_LED also rises 50 mV at this slightly-higher current: I drops further. Always read the diode at the actual operating point on its curve, not at the datasheet's typical.

Worked example — small-signal rd in a clamp

The setup

Two Si diodes anti-parallel across an op-amp input as ESD clamps. Forward current under fault is 5 mA. What is the dynamic (small-signal) resistance the noise current sees?

rd = n·V_T / I = (1.9 · 25.7 mV) / 5 mA = 9.8 Ω (at this bias) At 1 mA forward: rd = 48.8 Ω (soft clamp) At 100 µA forward: rd = 488 Ω (barely clamping)

This is why ESD clamps work hard only once a real fault current flows. For sub-100-µA leakage, they look high-Z — so they don't load the signal in normal operation.

What to notice

  • Vf is not a constant. "0.7 V" is shorthand for "between 0.55 and 0.85 depending on the current." A 1 mA Si diode is at ~0.58 V; a 1 A diode is at ~0.95 V. The same diode, different operating point.
  • Schottky drops half as much — perfect for low-headroom rectification (battery OR-ing, polarity protection on a 3.3 V rail). The penalty: higher leakage (Is in microamps) and lower Vbr (often 20–60 V).
  • LED Vf is set primarily by bandgap — IR ≈ 1.2 V, red ≈ 1.9 V, green ≈ 2.2 V, blue/white ≈ 3.2 V. The bandgap sets the photon energy AND the forward-voltage floor. Secondary effect: Vf rises ~80–150 mV per decade of forward current within a single LED, but that's small compared to the bandgap-set base.
  • Temperature shifts Vf by ≈ −2 mV/°C for Si — a hot diode passes more current at the same V. This is the basis of every "diode thermometer" circuit, and also the runaway risk for paralleled diodes without ballast resistors.
  • Below the knee, current is "zero" only on linear scales. Log-scale plots show that current at 0.4 V is genuinely 0.4 µA on a Si diode — small, but not zero. This matters for leaky CMOS gate protection and pico-amp instrumentation.
  • The decade slope is universal for n=1, T=25 °C: ~60 mV per decade. Memorize this. Every Si junction. Every BJT V_BE. Every Schottky.

Where this curve shows up

Real uses

  • Rectification — bridge rectifiers, freewheel diodes (1N4007); Schottky for low-loss rectification on switchers.
  • Voltage references — Zener regulators, bandgap references stack diodes to cancel Vbe drift.
  • ESD / surge protection — TVS arrays clamp transient overvoltage; Schottky steering diodes route to power rail.
  • Logarithmic amplifiers — diode in op-amp feedback gives V_out ∝ log(I_in). Used in audio dB meters.
  • Temperature sensing — Vbe of a forward-biased junction drops by ~2 mV/°C; the "PTAT" reference (proportional-to-absolute-temperature).
  • LEDs — every status indicator, every IR remote, every photodiode is the same physics in reverse.

TYPICAL Vf

  • Si small-sig (1N4148) ~0.65 V @ 10 mA
  • Si rectifier (1N400x) ~0.95 V @ 1 A
  • Schottky (1N5817) ~0.32 V @ 1 A
  • Ge (1N34A) ~0.30 V @ 1 mA
  • IR LED ~1.2 V; red ~1.9 V; green ~2.2 V; blue/white ~3.2 V
  • Zener BZX84C5V1 ~5.1 V reverse

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Paralleling diodes without ballast — TC of −2 mV/°C means the hotter diode hogs the current. Use ballast Rs (~0.1 Ω) or pick from same reel.
  • Reverse-stress on small-signal diodes — 1N4148 dies at 100 V reverse. Use TVS or rectifier for surge paths.
  • Treating Vf as exact — it's a curve. A "0.7 V" assumption mis-estimates I by ±30 % near the knee.
  • Forgetting LED self-heating — at IF_max, junction can rise 30 °C; Vf drops 60 mV → I rises further → thermal runaway in unballasted strings.
  • Schottky leakage — Schottky's Is is 1000× a Si's. At 85 °C, reverse leakage can be milliamps. Don't use Schottky for high-impedance reverse blocking.
