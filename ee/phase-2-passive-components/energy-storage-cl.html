Energy Storage in C and L

A capacitor stores energy in the electric field between its plates; an inductor stores energy in the magnetic field around its coil. The two formulas — E_C = ½·C·V² and E_L = ½·L·I² — are duals of each other, and the factor of ½ is the same factor you see in kinetic energy (½·m·v²) and spring PE (½·k·x²). This visual lets you compare a capacitor and an inductor side-by-side, dial in real-world C / L values and voltages / currents, and watch energy transfer between the two during an LC charge-discharge cycle.

Three things to take away: (1) energy scales with the square of voltage (for C) or current (for L) — double the V, four times the energy; (2) the two devices are perfect duals — V ↔ I, C ↔ L, ε ↔ µ, and the storage formulas swap accordingly; (3) real components fall short of ideal — capacitors lose energy as ESR · I² dielectric loss, inductors lose energy as DCR · I² and core hysteresis loss, and the energy ratings on datasheets are nominal at low frequency and 25 °C — derate aggressively for the real world.

Where the formula comes from (capacitor)

Definition: C = Q/V (capacitance = charge per volt). Differential form: i = C · dV/dt. Instantaneous power into the capacitor: P(t) = V(t) · i(t) = V(t) · C · dV/dt. Energy stored = ∫₀ᵗ P dτ = C · ∫₀ᵛ V dV = ½ · C · V². → the factor of ½ comes from integrating V from 0 to V. In terms of charge: E = ½ · Q · V = ½ · Q² / C. All three forms are equivalent.

Where the formula comes from (inductor)

Definition: L = Φ/I (inductance = flux linkage per ampere). Differential form: v = L · di/dt. Instantaneous power into the inductor: P(t) = v(t) · i(t) = L · di/dt · i(t). Energy stored = ∫₀ᵗ P dτ = L · ∫₀ⁱ I dI = ½ · L · I². → same ½ factor, same kinetic-energy mathematical shape. In terms of flux: E = ½ · Φ · I = ½ · Φ² / L. All three forms equivalent.

Worked example — sizing a bulk cap for a microcontroller brownout

The problem

An ESP32 draws 200 mA peak during a WiFi TX burst. The 3.3 V regulator can supply average current but not the burst. A bulk capacitor between regulator output and ESP32 V_DD must hold V above the 3.0 V brownout for 10 ms (the TX burst duration). Size the cap.

The arithmetic — energy view

Allowed voltage droop: ΔV = 3.3 − 3.0 = 0.3 V Energy needed during burst: E = V · I · t = 3.15 (average) · 0.2 · 0.01 = 6.3 mJ Cap energy difference: E = ½ · C · (V_high² − V_low²) = ½ · C · (3.3² − 3.0²) = ½ · C · 1.89 Solve: C = 6.3·10⁻³ / 0.945 = 6.67 mF = 6700 µF (too big — wait)

The arithmetic — current view (more honest)

From i = C · dV/dt: C = I · Δt / ΔV = 0.2 · 0.01 / 0.3 = 6.67 mF

Same number, because the energy view assumed constant power; the current view assumes constant current. They agree. But: 6700 µF is a huge cap — physically larger than an ESP32 module. The real fix is one of: a smaller burst current (better antenna), a lower brownout threshold (configurable on ESP32), an LDO with a fast dynamic response, or a 10 µF + 100 µF combo that handles the high-frequency edge and lets the regulator handle the low-frequency average.

The lesson

Energy storage is fundamental, but in real circuits, bandwidth matters more than steady energy. A 100 µF cap with 0.01 Ω ESR drops 10 mV per amp of high-frequency current (V = I·ESR) — that's the cap doing what it's actually for. Picking caps for energy alone is rare; picking caps for ESR + Z(f) + ripple capability is the norm.

Worked example — ignition coil (inductor) flyback

The setup

Old-school automotive ignition coil: primary inductance L ≈ 5 mH, primary current I = 5 A at the moment of breakthrough. Calculate stored energy, predict the back-EMF when the points open in 1 µs, and see why a snubber cap is mandatory.

The arithmetic

Stored energy: E = ½ · L · I² = ½ · 0.005 · 25 = 62.5 mJ v = L · di/dt — opening 5 A in 1 µs: v = 0.005 · 5/10⁻⁶ = 25,000 V back-EMF spike Stored energy must go somewhere; without a path, it dumps into the dielectric of the switch (sparks across the points, arcs across the FET in a modern system). Snubber: C = 0.5 µF parallel across the switch → spike clamps to roughly ±√(L/C) · I = √(0.005 / 0.5×10⁻⁶) · 5 = √(10⁴) · 5 = 500 V

The insight

An inductor with 5 A of current is "loaded" with 62.5 mJ. Switching that current off in microseconds produces hundreds or thousands of volts because v = L · di/dt scales linearly with how fast you cut the current. Inductive kickback is exactly conservation of energy expressed at high speed. Every relay, every motor, every solenoid needs either a flyback diode (recirculates the current until L dissipates the energy as DCR·I²) or a snubber (slows down the transition). This is why a bare BJT switching a relay coil will fail within seconds.

Worked example — LC tank stores energy back and forth

The setup

L = 10 µH, C = 100 nF. Initially V_C = 10 V, I_L = 0. The tank rings; total energy is conserved (ideal case), oscillating between C-stored (electric) and L-stored (magnetic).

The arithmetic

Resonant ω: ω = 1/√(LC) = 1/√(10⁻⁵ · 10⁻⁷) = 1/√10⁻¹² = 10⁶ rad/s Frequency f = ω/2π = 159.2 kHz Initial energy: E = ½ · C · V² = ½ · 10⁻⁷ · 100 = 5 µJ (all in C) Peak current at zero V: I = √(2E/L) = √(10⁻⁵ / 10⁻⁵) = 1 A (reaches L when V_C swings through zero) Check: ½·L·I² = ½ · 10⁻⁵ · 1 = 5 µJ ✓ — same energy now in L

The picture

Two energy buckets, alternately full. V_C = V_max·cos(ωt), I_L = I_max·sin(ωt). E_C oscillates between 5 µJ and 0; E_L oscillates between 0 and 5 µJ; E_total = 5 µJ always. In a real tank, ESR + DCR drains the energy gradually as R·I² heat; this is the Q-factor of the tank, and the basis of every LC oscillator, every PLL, every radio.

What to notice

  • Energy scales with the square. Double the voltage on a cap → 4× the energy. Double the current in an inductor → 4× the energy. Bigger sources don't just store more, they store quadratically more.
  • The factor of ½ is universal. ½·C·V², ½·L·I², ½·m·v², ½·k·x² — all four come from integrating linear "force × displacement" with one end at zero. Not a coincidence.
  • Capacitor energy is in the field, not the charge. Move the plates apart at constant Q → V rises (because C drops) → stored energy increases. You did work against the electric field; the cap stored that work.
  • Inductor energy is in the magnetic field. Same flux, smaller core → field intensity rises → energy rises. Saturating the core ruins the formula: L collapses near saturation, energy falls off, and ringing changes.
  • Real components have parasitic energy losses. Cap: ESR · I_rms² + dielectric loss tangent tanδ × jωCV². Inductor: DCR · I² (copper) + core hysteresis (∫H·dB per cycle) + eddy-current loss. Datasheets show these as ratings; the ideal formula is only the upper bound on what gets stored.
  • An LC tank conserves energy on the ideal axis, ESR/DCR drains it on the time axis. Q-factor = ω·L/R for an inductor's losses, or 1/(ω·C·ESR) for the cap's. Higher Q = more cycles before energy decays e-fold.

Where you'll see this

Real uses

  • Power supply bulk caps — holds energy through the brownout when load steps; sized by ΔV vs Δt vs I.
  • Switch-mode converter inductors — store energy each cycle, release into the load. See EE 6 — Sync Rectification.
  • Photo flash — charge a 1000 µF cap to 300 V → 45 J → dumps through a xenon tube in 1 ms = 45 kW peak.
  • Defibrillator — 32 µF cap charged to 5 kV → 400 J pulse into the heart. Same physics, life-saving instead of camera-flashing.
  • Hybrid car regen braking — kinetic energy → battery/supercap storage → drive train. The supercap form lets you accept high-current bursts the battery alone can't.
  • Magnetic levitation / MRI coils — superconducting coils carry tens of kA at zero DCR; stored energy stays for years.
  • Cross-link: RLC Step Response — what happens when you combine C, L, and resistance to dissipate.

PITFALLS

  • Forgetting the ½. E ≠ C·V² or L·I². The half comes from integrating.
  • Inductive kickback. Cutting current → v = L·di/dt → huge spike. Always add a flyback diode or snubber.
  • Cap derating. Datasheet C is at low DC bias. Class-2 MLCC (X7R) loses 30–80 % of C at rated voltage. Use class-1 (NP0/C0G) or oversize.
  • Core saturation. Inductor's L collapses at saturation current; stored energy hits a ceiling.
  • Energy vs power confusion. A 1 J cap dumping in 1 µs delivers 1 MW. The energy is small; the power is enormous.
  • Stored energy ≠ delivered energy. ESR/DCR turns some into heat during transfer. Real-world efficiency is below 100 %.
