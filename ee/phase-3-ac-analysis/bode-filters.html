Bode Plots — RC & RLC Filters

A Bode plot turns a frequency-response curve into two straight-line asymptotes that meet at a corner frequency. The true curve deviates from the asymptote by exactly 3 dB at the corner — that's where the "−3 dB point" name comes from. Switch between low-pass, high-pass, and bandpass tabs and watch the slopes flip and the phase swing.

What this teaches

Every first-order pole gives a −20 dB/decade slope and a 90° phase shift, smeared over about a decade on each side of the corner. A second-order resonant peak gives −40 dB/decade rolloff (or in the bandpass case, ±20 dB/decade on each side of f₀) and up to 180° of total phase shift. The straight-line approximations let you sketch any filter response in seconds; the live curve overlay shows exactly when the approximation is "close enough" and when it isn't.

What to notice

  • The corner frequency is a "knee" — below f_c the asymptote is one slope, above it another. The true curve eases through the knee, sitting exactly 3 dB below where the two straight lines intersect. That's not coincidence: at f=f_c the magnitude is 1/√2 of the passband, and 20·log₁₀(1/√2) = −3.01 dB.
  • 20 dB per decade = 6 dB per octave — a decade is ×10 in frequency, an octave is ×2. log₁₀(2) ≈ 0.301, so 20×0.301 ≈ 6 dB per octave. Audio engineers prefer octaves; controls engineers prefer decades. Same slope, two units.
  • Phase is "smeared" over two decades — the full 90° shift of a first-order pole happens approximately between f_c/10 (≈−5.7°) and 10·f_c (≈−84°). At f_c itself, phase is exactly halfway: ±45°. This is why a fast slider sweep makes the phase plot look gentle but the magnitude knee look sharp.
  • Sliding R and C trades for the same f_c — if you halve R and double C, f_c = 1/(2πRC) is unchanged. Same Bode plot. The current draw and impedance change, but the frequency response doesn't. This is why filter design has a degree of freedom called impedance scaling.
  • RLC bandpass: Q sharpens the peak, doesn't move it — f₀ depends only on √(LC). Q = (1/R)·√(L/C) controls the height and narrowness. Drop R and the peak gets pointier and taller (more amplification at f₀, narrower BW). Raise R and the peak melts into a broad bump.
  • Second-order rolloff is twice as steep — at f much greater than f₀ the bandpass falls at −20 dB/decade (only one reactive element dominates), but a low-pass second-order rolls at −40 dB/decade because both L and C contribute. More reactive elements = steeper slope = sharper filter.
  • The asymptote breaks down near the corner — the straight-line Bode is a sketcher's tool. For exact specs (anti-aliasing margins, EMI compliance) you compute the true curve. The visual shows the gap clearly: it's largest right at f_c.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

dB
dB = 20·log₁₀|H|
f_c (RC)
f_c = 1 / (2πRC)
slope
−20 dB/dec per pole
phase
−90° per pole (total)

MEMORIZE

  • −3 dB ≈ 0.707× amplitude — the half-power corner.
  • Corner = asymptote intersection — true curve sits 3 dB below.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Asymptotes lie at the knee — true mag is off by ±3 dB at f_c.
  • Phase smears ~1 decade either side of corner — don't read it as a step.
