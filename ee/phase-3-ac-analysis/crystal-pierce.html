Crystal Pierce Oscillator

Quartz is the silent timing engine inside every MCU. The Pierce topology — inverter + crystal + two load caps — is so universal that 95 % of microcontroller XTAL pins use it without you ever drawing a schematic. Slide CL1 and CL2 to see how off-spec capacitors detune the resonance (parts per million matter for RTCs, ppb for GNSS). Why two caps and a feedback resistor make an oscillator

An oscillator needs positive feedback at exactly one frequency. The Pierce topology gives this for free by exploiting the fact that any RC tank shifts phase by 180° at its resonance, and an inverter shifts another 180° on its own — total 360° = positive feedback. The crystal is the high-Q resonator that picks the one frequency where both phase and gain conditions are satisfied (the Barkhausen criterion).

Barkhausen criterion: |loop gain| ≥ 1 AND total phase shift = 0° (or 360°) Inverter NOT gate: 180° phase shift by definition (V_out = −V_in) CL1 + Crystal + CL2 forms a π-network: another 180° at the crystal's series resonance f_s Sum: 360° = 0° → positive feedback at f_s The internal feedback resistor Rf (~1 MΩ) sets the inverter into its linear region (V_in ≈ V_out ≈ VCC/2), where it has enough gain to start oscillating. → The crystal does the precision; the inverter does the amplification.

The crystal equivalent circuit

A quartz blank is mechanically resonant, but electrically it behaves like an LCR series branch — wrapped inside a small shunt capacitor (the parasitic of its package). Four parameters fully describe it.

The 32.768 kHz watch crystal has an enormous L1 because the resonance equation f = 1/(2π√LC) needs something to be big when f is small — and L is set in the manufacturing for the chosen frequency.

The load capacitor is the trim knob

Crystal datasheets quote a "specified load capacitance C_L" — e.g., 12 pF, 18 pF, or 20 pF. This is the external capacitance the manufacturer assumed when calibrating the crystal at the factory. Deliver the right C_L and your oscillator hits f₀ exactly. Deliver more or less, and the parallel-resonance frequency shifts by:

Pulling sensitivity needs the motional capacitance C₁ (typ 10-30 fF for HC-49 crystals to ~30 MHz). Full formula: f_actual / f_spec − 1 = C₁ · (C_Lspec − C_Lactual) / (2·(C0 + C_Lspec)·(C0 + C_Lactual)) For small mismatches, the linearized trim sensitivity (ppm per pF of C_L change): S_L = C₁ · 10⁶ / (2·(C0 + C_Lspec)²) [ppm/pF] For 16 MHz, C₁ = 18 fF, C0 = 3 pF, C_Lspec = 18 pF: S_L = 0.018·10⁶ / (2·21²) = 18 000 / 882 ≈ 20 ppm/pF A 2 pF error → Δf ≈ 41 ppm. At 16 MHz that's 656 Hz — fine for an MCU clock. For a precision RTC (32.768 kHz, ±10 ppm budget), already 4× the budget — so RTC designs use trim caps and aging compensation. ⚠ Without the C₁ factor the formula gives 1/(2·21) ≈ 24 000 ppm/pF, off by 1000×; C₁ is what makes pulling small enough to be useful.

Capacitors are spec'd at ±5 % typical. Two 22 pF caps in series = 11 pF effective, ±0.55 pF. Add stray (PCB trace + pin) of 3–6 pF and you can easily be off by 15 % from the target C_L. This is why watch crystals are paired with an internal trim capacitance in modern MCUs.

Worked example — picking caps for a 16 MHz Arduino

The constraints

Crystal: 16 MHz, C_Lspec = 18 pF, C0 = 3 pF, Rs = 40 Ω. PCB stray capacitance per pin (FR4, short trace): Cs = 3 pF. ATmega328P XTAL1/2 internal C: 3 pF.

The arithmetic

Total Cs per side: 3 (PCB) + 3 (pin) = 6 pF Required CL1 = CL2: solve (CL1·CL2)/(CL1+CL2) + Cs = C_Lspec With CL1 = CL2 = X: X/2 + 6 = 18 → X = 24 pF (closest E12: 22 pF) With 22 pF actual: C_L_eff = 22/2 + 6 = 17 pF; mismatch = +1 pF S_L = C₁·10⁶/(2·(C0 + C_Lspec)²) = 0.018·10⁶/(2·21²) ≈ 20.4 ppm/pF detune ≈ +20.4 · 1 ≈ +20 ppm (oscillator runs slightly fast because C_L came in low) For 9600 baud UART, 20 ppm = 0.19 Hz error per second → negligible. For 1 PPS GPS sync: 20 ppm = 20 µs/s drift → fine for second-level alignment.

If you pick wrong

Putting 100 pF "for safety" makes C_L_eff = 56 pF — wildly off-spec (+38 pF). Detune ≈ −20·38 ≈ −770 ppm. f drops by ~770 ppm, or ~12 kHz at 16 MHz. UART eventually drifts out of bit timing.

Worked example — RTC needs ±5 ppm for a watch

The constraints

32.768 kHz watch crystal, C_Lspec = 12.5 pF, C0 = 1.2 pF. Used in a digital wristwatch — required accuracy 1 second/day = 11.6 ppm.

The arithmetic

Frequency budget: ±11.6 ppm Crystal own aging + temperature: typ ±5 ppm — leaves ±6 ppm budget for cap mismatch. Watch crystal C₁ ≈ 3 fF, C0 ≈ 1.2 pF. Using S_L = C₁·10⁶/(2·(C0+C_Lspec)²) = 0.003·10⁶/(2·13.7²) ≈ 8 ppm/pF Δf for 1 pF cap mismatch ≈ 8 ppm — already eats the ±6 ppm budget. ±5% on a 12.5 pF target = ±0.6 pF → ±5 ppm — borderline. Solution: integrated MCU XTAL drivers (like CC2540, STM32L0) have internally calibrated trim caps stepping 0.5 pF — they can fine-tune to ±3 ppm.

For DIY: use 0805 NP0 (C0G) capacitors. 5 % through-hole Y5V will drift 100 ppm with temperature alone.

What to notice

  • Caps trim the parallel resonance, not the series. The crystal can oscillate at two frequencies (fs and fp); load caps pull the oscillation to a point between them. More C_L → lower frequency.
  • The 1 MΩ feedback resistor (Rf) is critical and easy to forget. It biases the CMOS inverter into the linear region. Without it, the inverter rails out and doesn't oscillate.
  • Two equal load caps, not one — the Pierce topology needs symmetric loading. CL1 ≠ CL2 changes the gain balance and can prevent startup.
  • Series resistor Rs (not the crystal's ESR) is a startup tool, not a cap. Adding a few hundred Ω in series with the crystal limits inverter drive at startup, reduces overdriving (which damages the crystal mechanically), and helps suppress spurious modes.
  • Drive level matters. Overdriving a small crystal (especially 32.768 kHz) causes long-term frequency drift and eventual crystal failure. Datasheet drive level (DL) is typically 1–10 µW; respect it.
  • Negative resistance is the right metaphor for "gain margin." The inverter+caps presents a negative resistance to the crystal; if |R_neg| > 5·Rs (datasheet ESR), oscillation starts reliably. Many MCU app notes give you a recommended R_neg test procedure.

Where this circuit shows up

Real uses

  • Every MCU XTAL pin — ATmega, STM32, ESP32, Pico, RP2350. The Pierce is hidden inside the chip's XO peripheral.
  • USB devices — 12 MHz or 24 MHz crystal feeds an internal PLL → 480 MHz USB clock.
  • Ethernet PHY — 25 MHz or 50 MHz, ±50 ppm spec for 100BASE-TX.
  • RTC — 32.768 kHz, ±20 ppm typical; ±5 ppm for medical devices.
  • RF reference clocks — VCXO/TCXO/OCXO for GPS, LoRa, BLE. TCXO is a Pierce with internal trim cap controlled by temperature sensor; OCXO heats the crystal to a constant temperature.

RULES OF THUMB

  • R_neg ≥ 5·Rs (ESR) for reliable startup.
  • Drive level ≤ datasheet — typically 1–10 µW.
  • NP0/C0G caps for tight ppm — never X5R, X7R, Y5V.
  • Equal load caps; CL1 = CL2 = 2·(C_Lspec − Cs).
  • Always add Rs ≈ 0–1 kΩ for current limit on small crystals.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Ignoring stray capacitance — PCB traces + pins add 3–10 pF. Calculate C_L from MEASURED, not nominal.
  • Using ceramic Y5V caps — capacitance drops 70 % with voltage and temperature. Crystal stops oscillating in cold weather.
  • Routing crystal traces near switching nodes — coupling kills jitter spec. Keep XO pins isolated.
  • Wrong C_L spec — buying a 12 pF crystal and using 22 pF caps gives ~100 ppm error. Match the C_L spec.
  • Forgetting Rf — buying a "raw" inverter (74HC04) and skipping the 1 MΩ feedback resistor → no oscillation.
  • Overdriving — large CL or low Rs lets the inverter pump too much current through the crystal; mechanical damage over months.
