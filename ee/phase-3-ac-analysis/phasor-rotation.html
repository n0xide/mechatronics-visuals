Phasor Rotation

A sinusoid is the shadow of a spinning arrow. Watch a phasor rotate counter-clockwise at angular speed ω, and the x-projection (real part) traces out cos(ωt). For reactive components, V and I are out of phase by exactly 90°: inductor voltage leads current (V_L = jωL · I) and capacitor voltage lags current (V_C = I / jωC). From spinning arrow to sinusoid

Every steady-state AC signal at frequency f can be represented by a complex number, called a phasor: amplitude (length) and phase (angle). The instantaneous time-domain value is the real part of phasor · exp(jωt).

Sinusoid: v(t) = V_m · cos(ωt + φ) Phasor form: V = V_m · e^(jφ) = V_m·(cos φ + j sin φ) Recover time: v(t) = Re{ V · e^(jωt) } The exponential e^(jωt) is the spinning unit vector at speed ω; multiplication by V scales it (V_m) and rotates it (φ). → Phasors live in frequency-domain; all instantaneous time information is "stripped" — only amplitude and phase remain.

Why the 90° offsets aren't arbitrary

Inductors and capacitors are dual: one stores energy in magnetic flux, the other in electric charge. Differentiating cos(ωt) gives −sin(ωt) = cos(ωt + 90°), so any time-derivative of a sinusoid is itself a sinusoid rotated by 90°. That's where the 90° phase shift comes from.

Inductor: v_L = L·di/dt; if i(t) = I_m·cos(ωt), then di/dt = −ωI_m·sin(ωt) = ωI_m·cos(ωt + 90°) → V_L leads I by 90° Capacitor: i_C = C·dv/dt; if i(t) = I_m·cos(ωt), then v(t) = (I_m/ωC)·sin(ωt) = (I_m/ωC)·cos(ωt − 90°) → V_C lags I by 90° In phasor form: V_L = jωL·I, V_C = I/(jωC) — the j multiplies-and-rotates-by-90°.

Worked example — series RL at 60 Hz

The setup

R = 100 Ω, L = 100 mH, source V_m = 10 V at f = 60 Hz.

The arithmetic

ω = 2π·60 = 377 rad/s X_L = ωL = 377 · 0.1 = 37.7 Ω (small compared to R at line freq) Z = √(100² + 37.7²) = 106.9 Ω θ = atan(37.7/100) = +20.7° (V leads I) I_m = 10 / 106.9 = 93.5 mA peak; I_rms = 66.1 mA V_R_m = I_m · R = 9.35 V (in phase with I) V_L_m = I_m · X_L = 3.53 V (leads I by 90°)

Sanity check

|V_R + jV_L| = √(9.35² + 3.53²) = 10.0 V ✓ (equals source amplitude)

Worked example — RC high-pass corner

The setup

R = 1 kΩ, C = 100 nF. Where is the corner frequency, and what's the phase shift there?

The arithmetic

f_c = 1 / (2π·R·C) = 1 / (2π · 1000 · 100e-9) = 1592 Hz At f = f_c: X_C = R = 1000 Ω (by definition of corner) θ = atan(X_C / R) = atan(1) = +45° (half of the maximum +90° lead) |H(f_c)| = 1/√2 = 0.707 = −3 dB At f = 10·f_c: X_C = 100 Ω → θ = atan(0.1) → +5.7°; |H| → 0.995 At f = f_c/10: X_C = 10 kΩ → θ = atan(10) → +84.3°; |H| → 0.0995

The takeaway

The phase shift through a reactive element is gradual, not abrupt. It crosses +45° exactly at the corner. Well above f_c: phase → 0° (resistor dominates, signal passes); well below f_c: phase → +90° (capacitor dominates, signal blocked).

What to notice

  • The phasor diagram is the picture; the sinusoid is the projection. Looking only at the time waveform, you'd never guess they're all rotating at the same speed.
  • V_L and V_C are 180° apart — they always partially cancel each other. At resonance (X_L = X_C), they cancel exactly, leaving only R in the impedance.
  • Phase tells you what's leading what, but only in steady state. Transients (after a step) are a different beast — that's the territory of Laplace transforms.
  • "V leads I" / "I leads V" — the leading one is the one whose maximum comes first in time. Mnemonics: ELI the ICE man. (E_L leads I; I leads E in C.)
  • Phasor magnitudes are usually peak (V_m), not RMS, but the math doesn't care which — same arithmetic with √2 multiplier. Just be consistent across a problem.
  • Real waveform = Re{V · e^(jωt)}. The "phasor" is a frozen snapshot at t = 0; the e^(jωt) makes it spin. Engineers write V (no time) understanding the e^(jωt) is implicit.

Where phasors show up

Real uses

  • Filter analysis — Bode plots, group delay, transfer functions are all phasor math.
  • Power factor correction — measure V·I phase angle to know how much reactive power you're moving (and paying for).
  • Motor control — three-phase motors driven with phasors 120° apart; vector control uses Clarke/Park transforms (rotating reference frame).
  • RF impedance matching — Smith chart is a graphical phasor calculator. Z = R + jX everywhere.
  • Vibration / control — bode plots of mechanical systems are the same math (mass = inductor, spring = capacitor).
  • Audio EQ — every parametric EQ band is a 2nd-order RLC analog; phase response matters for transient fidelity.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

Phasor
V = V_m·e^(jφ)
Time-domain
v(t) = Re{V·e^(jωt)}
L impedance
Z_L = jωL (V leads I)
C impedance
Z_C = 1/(jωC) (V lags I)
R impedance
Z_R = R (no phase)
Series
Z = Z_R + Z_L + Z_C
|Z|, θ
|Z| = √(R²+X²); θ = atan(X/R)

MNEMONICS

  • ELI the ICE man — In L, E leads I; In C, I leads E.
  • Inductor = magnetic flywheel — resists change in current.
  • Capacitor = electric flywheel — resists change in voltage.
  • At resonance: X_L = X_C, so reactances cancel; Z = R, θ = 0.
  • Phasor rotation: CCW = positive frequency by convention.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Sign of X_C — Z_C = 1/(jωC) = −j/(ωC), so X_C is negative when written as reactance. Always keep track of the sign.
  • RMS vs peak — wall-outlet "120 V_RMS" means V_m = 170 V. Use one or the other consistently.
  • Mixing rad and degrees — calculators in degrees, tables in radians. Convert before adding.
  • Treating a transient as a phasor — phasors only describe steady state. After a step, you need the natural-response term too.
  • Wrong direction of rotation — engineering convention is CCW (positive ω); physics sometimes uses CW. Lab equipment uses CCW; honor that.
