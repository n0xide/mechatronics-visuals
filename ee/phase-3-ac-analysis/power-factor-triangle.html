Power Factor & The Power Triangle

When current lags (or leads) voltage, the supply has to push more amps than the load actually "uses." Real power P, reactive power Q, and apparent power S form a right triangle whose angle is the phase difference θ between V and I. Power factor PF = cos(θ) is the cosine of that angle — and the lever every utility engineer reaches for.

What this teaches

Hold V_rms·I_rms constant and rotate θ. Real power P = VI·cos(θ) shrinks, reactive power Q = VI·sin(θ) grows, but apparent power S = VI stays put. That's why an inductive motor on a 120 V / 10 A line might deliver only 700 W of useful work while the supply still bleeds 1200 VA. Drop a capacitor in parallel and its leading Q cancels the motor's lagging Q — the triangle collapses toward a flat horizontal line and PF marches toward 1.

Panel 1 — The triangle

PF = cos(θ) = 0.707 P = 848 W Q = 848 VAR S = 1200 VA
P — real power (W) Q — reactive power (VAR) S — apparent power (VA)

Why supplies care

An ideal resistive load at 120 V · 10 A needs 1200 W and pulls 10 A. At PF=0.707 the same wires must still pass 10.00 A to deliver only 848 W of useful power.

To deliver the same 1200 W of real power at this PF, the supply would need 14.14 A — 41.4% more current, which costs 100% more I²R loss in the distribution wiring. Utilities bill industrial customers on apparent power (kVA-h) precisely to penalize this.

Panel 2 — Power factor correction

An inductive load (a motor) lags by θ_L. Adding a capacitor in parallel injects a leading reactive current that subtracts from the inductor's lag. Walk the capacitor up and the net Q falls; the triangle's vertical leg shrinks; PF climbs toward 1.0.

P_load = 2500 W Q_load = 4330 VAR Q_net = 4330 VAR PF_before = 0.500 → PF_after = 0.500 I_line: 20.83 → 20.83 A
P (unchanged) Q_load (inductive, lag) Q_c (capacitor, lead, subtracts) S_before (faded) → S_after (solid)

What to notice

  • The triangle is just trig — fix the hypotenuse S=VI; sweep θ and you trace a quarter-circle of (P, Q) points. At θ=0 the triangle collapses to a horizontal line (all real). At θ=90° it stands straight up (all reactive — no useful power despite the same current draw).
  • PF=cos(θ) is brutal near 90° — at θ=60°, PF is already only 0.5. Cosine falls slowly near 0° and fast near 90°, so a "small" 30° phase shift costs you 13% of your real power; a 60° shift costs 50%.
  • Capacitor correction is Q subtraction — a capacitor on AC produces Q_c = V²·ωC (leading). It doesn't generate real power; it just cancels the inductor's reactive demand so the supply sees a smaller triangle.
  • The "perfect" capacitor — set Q_c = Q_load and net Q goes to zero. The triangle flattens to a horizontal line, PF=1, and the line current drops from S/V down to P/V. That's the savings.
  • Over-correction flips the sign — push C past the match point and Q_net goes negative (the load now looks capacitive to the grid). PF re-degrades; the triangle re-grows pointing the other way. Utilities don't love this either — overcorrection causes voltage rise on long lines.
  • Resistors don't have a power triangle — for a pure resistor θ=0, Q=0, S=P. The triangle degenerates. PF correction only matters when there's reactance in the load.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

P (real, W)
P = V_rms·I_rms·cos θ
Q (reac, VAR)
Q = V·I·sin θ
S (app, VA)
S = V·I = √(P² + Q²)
PF
PF = P/S = cos θ

MEMORIZE

  • PF = 1 — purely resistive (θ=0).
  • PF = 0 — purely reactive (θ=±90°, no real power).
  • Lag = inductive, lead = capacitive.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Low PF wastes capacity — wires/transformer must carry S amps to deliver only P watts. Utilities bill kVA-h.
