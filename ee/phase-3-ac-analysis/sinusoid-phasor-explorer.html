Sinusoid ↔ Phasor Explorer

A sinusoid in the time domain is the projection of a rotating vector — the phasor — onto the real axis. Drag the sliders and watch how the dot on the circle and the dot on the wave move in lockstep. Once that picture is in your head, AC analysis stops being algebra and starts being geometry.

What this teaches

The Euler-formula correspondence Vm·cos(ωt+φ) = Re{Vm·e^j(ωt+φ)}. The phasor magnitude is the peak; its angle at t=0 is the phase. Frequency is hidden — it's the rotation rate. Two signals at the same ω have a fixed angle between their phasors: that's lead/lag.

What to notice

  • The dot on the circle and the dot on the wave move together — the wave dot's height is the x-projection (real part) of the rotating phasor. We use cosine convention here, so the wave traces Re{phasor·e^(jωt)} = V_m·cos(ωt+φ). The y-projection (imaginary part) would give sin instead.
  • Changing φ rotates the phasor — and shifts the wave horizontally. Same information, two views.
  • Frequency doesn't change the phasor's shape — only how fast it spins. The phasor is the *steady-state* picture; ω is implicit.
  • Two phasors at the same ω keep a constant angle — that fixed angle is the phase difference. Positive = lead, negative = lag (cosine convention).
  • RMS is just the radius scaled by 1/√2 ≈ 0.707 — the dashed band on the time plot is where a DC voltage delivering the same average power would sit.
  • Freeze a few snapshots while spinning — the snapshots make it obvious that "phasor" is just a stop-action photo of a rotating vector.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

time form
v(t) = V_m·cos(ωt + φ)
angular ω
ω = 2πf
RMS
V_rms = V_m / √2

MEMORIZE

  • RMS ÷ peak = 0.707 — same heat as DC of V_rms.
  • AVG(|sin|) = 0.637·V_m — full-wave rectified mean.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • RMS for power — peak for breakdown ratings (insulation, cap V_max).
