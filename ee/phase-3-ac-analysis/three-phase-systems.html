Three-Phase Systems — Y, Δ, line vs phase, and why √3 keeps showing up

Three-phase power transmits ~3× more power than single-phase with the same conductor cross-section + insulation. Every industrial motor, every grid-tied inverter, every utility transformer is three-phase. The math has a single recurring constant — √3 ≈ 1.732 — that ties line-to-line voltage to line-to-neutral voltage, and total power to per-phase power. This visual shows the three sinusoids in time, the three phasors as vectors, computes V_LL / I_L / P / Q / S, draws balanced vs unbalanced neutral currents, and shows the rectified DC output of a 6-pulse bridge.

The math behind 3-phase

Three sinusoids 120° apart (positive sequence A-B-C): v_A(t) = V_m · sin(ωt) v_B(t) = V_m · sin(ωt − 120°) v_C(t) = V_m · sin(ωt − 240°) = V_m · sin(ωt + 120°) Sum at any instant: v_A + v_B + v_C = 0 ⇒ If load is balanced, neutral current is also zero. Y (wye) connection: V_phase = V_LN (per-phase voltage to neutral) V_line = V_LL = √3 · V_LN ⇐ 3-phase magic I_phase = I_line = I_L Δ (delta) connection: V_phase = V_LL (across the delta winding) V_line = V_LL (same as winding) I_phase = I_LL / √3 I_line = √3 · I_phase Power in either connection: P_3φ = √3 · V_LL · I_L · cos(φ) [W] Q_3φ = √3 · V_LL · I_L · sin(φ) [VAR] S_3φ = √3 · V_LL · I_L = √(P² + Q²) [VA] Note: at any instant, p_3φ(t) is constant (for balanced sinusoidal). No 100/120 Hz ripple as in single-phase. ⇒ smaller bulk capacitor needed in 3-phase rectifiers; motors get smoother torque. 6-pulse full-bridge rectifier (B6 / Graetz): V_DC_avg = (3√2 / π) · V_LL ≈ 1.35 · V_LL Ripple frequency = 6 · f (six commutations per fundamental cycle) Ripple amplitude (peak-peak before filter) ≈ V_LL_peak · (1 − cos(30°)) = √2·V_LL · (1 − cos(30°)) ≈ 13.4% of V_LL_peak

3-phase rectification — why DC drives love it

A 6-pulse diode bridge (B6, also called Graetz bridge) feeds DC from 3-phase. Twelve commutation points per cycle (six on positive half, six on negative half) make the unfiltered DC much smoother than single-phase. This is the input stage of every industrial VFD (variable frequency drive).

Key numbers (continuous-conduction mode)

V_DC_avg = (3·√2 / π) · V_LL ≈ 1.35 · V_LL V_DC_peak = √2 · V_LL (the actual line-line peak) Ripple frequency = 6·f (360 Hz for 60 Hz grid; 300 Hz for 50 Hz) Ripple peak-to-peak (unfiltered) ≈ V_DC_peak · (1 − cos 30°) ≈ 13.4 % of V_DC_peak (≈ 14 % of V_DC_avg p-p; RMS ripple ≈ 4.2 % of V_DC_avg) For 480 V_LL grid: V_DC ≈ 648 V (avg), 679 V (peak)

Compare to single-phase: V_DC_avg = (2√2/π)·V_AC ≈ 0.90·V_AC, ripple at 2f, ~100% peak-to-peak unfiltered. Three-phase needs only ~10% of the bulk capacitance of single-phase for the same ripple voltage.

Unbalanced loads + the neutral

A balanced 3-phase Y load draws equal current on each phase, with phases 120° apart — they vector-sum to zero, so the neutral conductor carries no current. The moment loads become unbalanced (one phase has more load than the others, or a single-phase load is added between one phase and neutral), the neutral conductor carries the residual.

Worst cases

  • Single-phase load only: I_N = I_phase (full current returns through neutral)
  • Two phases loaded: I_N = phasor sum; up to ~I_phase if loads are equal
  • Non-linear loads (rectifiers, lighting ballasts): 3rd-harmonic currents add in the neutral (don't cancel like fundamental). A neutral that's sized for fundamental balance can overheat from triplen harmonics.
  • Open phase (B disconnected): A and C carry the entire load; appliance motors see severely distorted voltage and may stall.

Practical rule

For modern data-center and office wiring (lots of non-linear loads), code requires the neutral to be sized 200% of the phase conductor in extreme cases. NEC 220.61(C)(2) and similar.

Worked example — 480V industrial chiller motor

Spec

Three-phase 480 VLL, 60 Hz grid. Motor nameplate: 50 hp, 0.85 PF lagging, 92% efficiency, Y-connected stator.

Run the math

P_out = 50 hp × 746 W/hp = 37,300 W (mechanical) P_in = P_out / η = 37,300 / 0.92 = 40,540 W (electrical real power) S = P / cos(φ) = 40,540 / 0.85 = 47,700 VA I_L = S / (√3 · V_LL) = 47,700 / (1.732 · 480) = 57.4 A Q = √(S² − P²) = √(47700² − 40540²) = 25,180 VAR V_LN = V_LL / √3 = 480 / 1.732 = 277 V (each phase to neutral)

For VFD with 6-pulse rectifier on 480V input: DC bus ≈ 1.35 · 480 = 648 V — exactly why standard IGBTs in industrial drives are 1200 V rated (~2× margin over operating).

Common pitfalls

  • Confusing line and phase quantities — for Y: V_LL = √3·V_LN, I_L = I_phase. For Δ: V_LL = V_phase, I_L = √3·I_phase. Halving these the wrong way gives 3× current overload predictions.
  • Forgetting cos(φ) — apparent power S in VA tells you wire size; real power P in W tells you the bill. Industrial loads with PF = 0.7 draw 43% more current than the W rating suggests.
  • Phase rotation — A-B-C (positive sequence) vs A-C-B (negative). Motors run backwards if rotation is reversed; some test equipment requires positive only. Always verify rotation before commissioning.
  • Neutral oversized for harmonics — non-linear loads (rectifiers, LED drivers) produce triplen harmonics that add in the neutral. A B-rated panel may need a doubled neutral.
  • Single-phase load on a 3-phase circuit — fine on a 4-wire Y system, but on a 3-wire Δ you must use line-to-line (V_LL voltage), not line-to-fake-neutral.
  • Resonance with PFC capacitors — adding power-factor correction caps can resonate with the source impedance + transformer leakage at a low harmonic (often 5th, 7th, 11th). Always model before installing > 100 kVAR.
  • "Half-bridge inverter" needs full DC bus — when designing the inverter side, remember V_DC ≥ √2·V_LL (peak) is needed to reproduce line-line voltage at the output. Below that, you can't fully modulate.
  • 240 V split-phase ≠ 2-phase — US residential is single-phase center-tapped (180° between L1 and L2, not 90°). Don't confuse with real 2-phase systems (now obsolete).
