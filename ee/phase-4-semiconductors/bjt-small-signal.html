BJT Small-Signal Analysis

Bias a BJT in the middle of its active region (Q-point) and the diode-like exponential becomes — for small wiggles around that point — a linear amplifier. Three numbers do all the work: transconductance gm = Ic / VT, base resistance rπ = β / gm, voltage gain Av = −gm · R_C. Slide the bias resistor, watch Q migrate on the load line, see voltage gain change. The hybrid-π model is the small-signal soul of every audio preamp, transistor switch, and current source ever built.

The DC Q-point: pick the bias before anything else

A BJT is a current amplifier (I_C ≈ β·I_B) only when biased into the active region: V_BE ≈ 0.65 V, V_CE > V_BE. The bias point — "quiescent" or Q-point — sets I_C and V_CE in the absence of any signal. Everything else (gm, rπ, gain) depends on that I_C.

Base bias network: V_B = (V_CC · R_B2)/(R_B1+R_B2) (or single R_B + base, for simplified case) V_E = V_B − V_BE ≈ V_B − 0.65 V I_E = V_E / R_E → I_C ≈ I_E (since β > 100) V_C = V_CC − I_C·R_C V_CE = V_C − V_E → design target: V_CE ≈ V_CC/2 (mid-rail, max swing)

The load line on the I_C vs V_CE plot

Pick any V_CC and R_C. Plot two extreme operating points: when V_CE = 0 (transistor "saturated"), I_C = V_CC / R_C; when I_C = 0 (transistor "cut off"), V_CE = V_CC. Connect them with a line: that's the load line. The Q-point sits somewhere on it. The base current dictates which I_C curve the transistor follows; where it crosses the load line is the actual operating point.

Small-signal: linearize the diode

The Ebers-Moll equation I_C = Is·exp(V_BE / V_T) is exponential, brutally so. But for a tiny wiggle around the Q-point, only the local slope matters — and that slope is gm.

I_C = Is · exp(V_BE / V_T) Differentiate w.r.t. V_BE: dI_C/dV_BE = Is·exp(V_BE/V_T)/V_T = I_C / V_T → gm = I_C / V_T — the transconductance, the heart of small-signal BJT analysis. At I_C = 1 mA, V_T = 25.7 mV: gm = 0.0389 A/V = 38.9 mA/V. Memorize: ~40 mA/V per mA. Base resistance: rπ = β / gm — the small-signal V_BE/I_B ratio. β=200 at I_C=1 mA: rπ = 5.14 kΩ. Emitter resistance: r_e ≈ V_T / I_E ≈ 1/gm. Often more useful than rπ for circuit analysis.

Voltage gain Av = −gm · R_C (common-emitter)

Small-signal input wiggle V_be drives a collector-current wiggle: i_c = gm · V_be That current goes through R_C, producing output: V_out = −i_c · R_C (minus because higher I_C = lower V_C) → Av = V_out / V_be = −gm · R_C For I_C = 2 mA and R_C = 5 kΩ: gm = 77 mA/V, Av = −385. If R_E is NOT bypassed by a capacitor (degenerated): Av = −R_C / (R_E + r_e) ≈ −R_C / R_E for R_E ≫ r_e. With R_E bypass (small Cap in parallel with R_E): Av_AC = −gm·R_C (full gain at signal freq, but stable DC bias). This is the standard CE amp.

Worked example — discrete CE amplifier, 1 kHz audio

The setup

V_CC = 12 V, β = 200. Want voltage gain ≈ 100. Bias for I_C = 2 mA. Pick R_C, R_E, R_B.

The arithmetic

Target Av = −100, gm at 2 mA = 2/0.0257 = 78 mA/V R_C = 100 / 78 mA = 1.28 kΩ → pick 1.3 kΩ (E12: 1.2 kΩ) For Q-point at V_CE = V_CC/2 = 6 V: V_C = 6 + V_E = 6 + 1 = 7 V (if V_E = 1 V) I_C · R_C = 12 − 7 = 5 V → I_C = 5/1200 = 4.17 mA — too high; adjust R_C up or accept Vce ≠ 6 V Recompute with R_C = 2.7 kΩ: I_C·R_C = 5.4 V at 2 mA → V_C = 6.6 V → V_CE = 5.6 V (OK) Resulting A_v = −gm·R_C = −78·2.7 = −210 (overshot −100; the V_CE=6 V "ideal" headroom forced R_C up. If A_v ≈ −100 is the hard requirement, drop back to R_C = 100/gm ≈ 1.3 kΩ and accept off-center bias V_CE ≈ 12 − 2·1.3 − 1 = 8.4 V — still active, with reduced positive-swing headroom.) R_E for V_E = 1 V at 2 mA: R_E = 500 Ω → pick 470 Ω R_E bypass cap C_E = 1/(2π·f_low·R_E/10) = 1/(2π·20·47) = 169 µF → use 220 µF electrolytic Base voltage = V_E + V_BE = 1.65 V; bias network R1/R2 sets this R_B equivalent ≈ 10·R_E for solid bias: 4.7 kΩ — pick R1=33k, R2=5.6k from V_CC

The achieved gain

gm at I_C = 2 mA: 78 mA/V; Av = −78 · 2700 = −210 (with R_E bypassed) If R_E not bypassed (degenerated): Av = −R_C/R_E = −2700/500 = −5.4 (low but stable)

Worked example — bias stability with temperature

The setup

Common-emitter amp warms up; V_BE drops 2 mV/°C, β rises 1 %/°C. By how much does I_C drift over a 60 °C swing?

Without R_E (no degeneration)

I_C ≈ (V_BB − V_BE)/(R_B/β) — heavily dependent on V_BE ΔV_BE = 60·2 = 120 mV; if V_BB = 2 V, R_B = 100 kΩ, β = 200: I_C drift = β·ΔV_BE / R_B = 200·0.12/100k = 240 µA on top of, say, 2 mA — 12 % drift!

With R_E = 1 kΩ (degenerated)

I_C ≈ (V_BB − V_BE)/R_E (since R_E ≫ r_e) I_C drift = ΔV_BE/R_E = 0.12/1000 = 120 µA — half, and independent of β

The emitter resistor is a feedback element: if I_C tries to rise, V_E rises with it, lowering V_BE, throttling I_C back. R_E gives bias stability at the cost of voltage gain (Av drops by ~R_E/r_e factor unless bypassed).

What to notice

  • gm = I_C / V_T — once you know I_C, gm is fixed by physics (V_T ≈ 25.85 mV at 300 K; 25.69 mV at 25 °C). You can't make gm depend on β or material; only on current.
  • The "ideal" CE gain has a maximum at V_CE = V_CC/2 and I_C such that R_C·I_C = V_CC/2. Beyond that, headroom limits swing.
  • R_E is the bias stabilizer. Without it, β variation (5×!) and V_BE temperature drift will move the Q-point dramatically. With it, the operating point is set by R_E alone (β-independent for β > 100).
  • Bypass R_E with a cap if you want AC gain. The cap shorts R_E to ground at signal frequencies, so the gain is −gm·R_C at AC and stable at DC.
  • Input impedance into a CE amp is approximately rπ, parallel with the base bias network. β=200, I_C=1 mA: rπ ≈ 5 kΩ. This is low — bipolar amps load source heavily.
  • The hybrid-π model uses 4 parameters: rπ (base-emitter input resistance), gm (transconductance), r_o (Early effect, output resistance), Cπ + Cµ (junction capacitances at high freq). For audio/DC, just gm and rπ are enough.
  • "Small" means < V_T (~25 mV). If V_be wiggles approach V_T, the linear model breaks down and you get distortion (the exponential exposes itself).

Where the hybrid-π model shows up

Real uses

  • Audio preamps — guitar pedals, microphone amps; CE topology with R_E bypass.
  • Logic / switches — same transistor, used in saturation/cutoff instead of active region.
  • Differential amplifiers — the front end of every op-amp; long-tailed pair is two CE amps sharing a tail current source.
  • Current mirrors — bias-distribution networks in IC design rely on matched gm of paired BJTs.
  • RF amplifiers — common-base topology has high f_T, used in LNAs and oscillator buffers.
  • Voltage references — ΔV_BE generator (PTAT) is two BJTs at different currents, exploiting gm.

RULES OF THUMB

  • gm ≈ 40·I_C(mA) — at 1 mA, gm = 40 mA/V.
  • r_e (Ω) ≈ 26 / I_E(mA) — at 1 mA, r_e = 26 Ω.
  • V_BE drops 2 mV per °C rise.
  • β doubles for −40 °C → +85 °C; use feedback bias.
  • Bias voltage at base ≈ 1/10 V_CC as a starting design point.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Using "fixed-bias" (R_B from V_CC to base alone) — extremely β-sensitive. Always add R_E.
  • Forgetting r_e in gain formula — for low I_C (sub-mA), r_e becomes significant compared to R_E.
  • Operating outside active region — saturation (V_CE ≈ 0.2 V) means β collapses, no amplification.
  • Treating β as a constant — β varies 2:1 between same-part-number BJTs.
  • Direct-coupling without level-shifting — output bias of one stage doesn't match input bias of the next; use coupling caps or differential pairs.
  • Ignoring r_o (Early effect) — at very high R_C, r_o limits Av to the intrinsic gain V_A/V_T ≈ 500–5000 (V_A ≈ 15–150 V ÷ 26 mV).
