Op-amp Integrator & Differentiator

Swap a resistor and a capacitor in the feedback loop and an op-amp becomes either a calculus engine or its inverse. Integrator: C in feedback, R at input → V_out = −(1/RC)·∫V_in dt. Differentiator: R in feedback, C at input → V_out = −RC·dV_in/dt. Slide RC and watch the input/output traces; flip the input waveform from step → sine → square and see ramp/cosine/spike. Then turn on the real-world fix (the bias resistor for the integrator, the series-R for the differentiator) and watch DC drift / HF gain peaking disappear.

How the equations fall out (Ohm + KCL at the inverting input)

For an ideal op-amp with negative feedback, the inverting input is a virtual ground: V_- = V_+ = 0, and no current flows in. KCL says the current through R must equal the current through the feedback element (charges have to go somewhere).

Integrator (C in feedback)

Current through R: i_R = V_in / R Current through C: i_C = C · d(V_- − V_out)/dt = −C·dV_out/dt KCL at virtual ground: i_R = i_C → V_in / R = −C · dV_out/dt → V_out = −(1/RC) · ∫V_in dt

Differentiator (R in feedback)

Current through C: i_C = C · d(V_in − V_-)/dt = C·dV_in/dt Current through R: i_R = (V_- − V_out) / R = −V_out / R KCL: i_C = i_R → C·dV_in/dt = −V_out / R → V_out = −RC · dV_in/dt

The gotchas: DC drift & HF noise gain

Integrator drift

The integrator integrates everything, including DC offset of the op-amp (~1 mV) and bias-current charge (~50 nA). Over seconds, the capacitor charges to the rail and the integrator saturates. The fix: parallel a large resistor R_f (10×–100× R) across C. This sets a DC gain limit at R_f / R and a low-frequency rolloff at 1/(2π·R_f·C). Below that frequency it's an inverting amplifier; above, an integrator.

Differentiator noise gain

The differentiator gain rises with frequency: gain ∝ f. High-frequency noise gets amplified by the gain at that frequency — potentially huge. The fix: add a small series resistor R_s with C (typically R/10). This sets a corner frequency at 1/(2π·R_s·C) above which gain stops rising. Below: differentiator; above: flat (or rolled off if you add a feedback cap).

Frequency response intuition

Both circuits implement a frequency-dependent transfer function. Plot |H| vs frequency on log-log axes and they're straight lines:

Integrator: |H(f)| = 1/(2π·f·RC) → −20 dB/decade. Gain = 1 at f = 1/(2πRC). Differentiator: |H(f)| = 2π·f·RC → +20 dB/decade. Gain = 1 at the same f. → They're reflections of each other across the unity-gain crossover frequency 1/(2πRC). One slope falls −1, the other rises +1. Real op-amp adds finite GBW (say 1 MHz for a 741). Above GBW: the op-amp can't follow, gain rolls off naturally.

Worked example — analog PID controller (integral term)

The setup

Feedback loop for a heater: error signal goes to a PID network. Integral term has time constant Ti = 100 ms. Pick R, C.

Choose R = 100 kΩ (easy E96 value) C = Ti / R = 0.1 / 100 000 = 1 µF (use NP0 or film for stability — electrolytic drifts) Anti-windup R_f = 10 MΩ (100× R) → DC gain = 100, low-freq corner = 0.016 Hz Op-amp choice: TLC2272 (rail-to-rail, low Ib = 1 pA) → DC drift < 1 mV over hours.

The math during a 10°C error

Error V_e = 10 mV (after sensor scaling) After 1 s of constant error: V_int = −(1/0.1)·∫(10 mV) dt = −100 mV After 10 s: V_int = −1 V (and growing, until external clamp engages)

Worked example — square-wave to pulse train (differentiator)

The setup

5 V CMOS digital signal (1 kHz square) into a differentiator with R = 10 kΩ, C = 1 nF. What's V_out?

τ = RC = 10 µs; f_corner = 15.9 kHz (input is well below) dV/dt during rising edge: 5 V in (say) 20 ns transition → 250 V/µs V_out at the spike = −RC·dV/dt = −10e-6 · 250e6 = −2500 V (but rail-clamped to ±15 V) Pulse width ≈ transition time × (R_s + R)·C ratio — wider than the input transition

With the fix

Add R_s = 1 kΩ in series with C. New corner at 1/(2π·1k·1n) = 159 kHz. Below 159 kHz it differentiates; above, it stops amplifying noise and just buffers. The output pulse is now finite, useful for edge detection in counters.

What to notice

  • Step in → ramp out (integrator). The slope of the ramp is −V_in / RC. This is exactly how a sawtooth generator works.
  • Sine in → cosine out: integrator(sin ωt) = −cos(ωt)/(ωRC); differentiator(sin ωt) = +ωRC·cos(ωt). Same shape, different sign and frequency-dependent gain. The integrator delays by 90°; the differentiator advances by 90°.
  • Square in → spike out (differentiator). dV/dt is huge during transitions, zero between them, so the output is a positive spike + negative spike train.
  • Triangle in → square out (differentiator). dV/dt is constant during each ramp, sign-flipped between them.
  • The corner frequency 1/(2πRC) is the same for both — but on opposite sides: integrator works above the corner, differentiator below.
  • Ideal integrator has infinite DC gain. Any DC offset on the input — even microvolts of op-amp offset — eventually saturates the output. Always add an anti-windup resistor in production designs.
  • Ideal differentiator has infinite HF gain. Any HF noise — even thermal noise of the input resistor — becomes huge at the output. Always add a series resistor in real designs.

Where these circuits show up

Real uses

  • PID controllers — integral (I) term uses an integrator; derivative (D) term uses a differentiator.
  • Sawtooth generators — integrate a constant current to ramp a voltage; reset with a switch.
  • Charge amplifiers — pyroelectric IR motion sensors, piezo accelerometers; integrate a current pulse into a voltage.
  • Edge detectors — high-pass differentiator picks transitions out of slowly-varying signals.
  • Active filters — Butterworth, Chebyshev, etc. are cascades of integrators (in state-variable topology).
  • ΣΔ ADCs — modulator is an integrator inside a feedback loop.
  • Crowbar / overvoltage protection — differentiate the input to catch sudden voltage spikes that aren't seen by an over-voltage threshold detector alone.

WAVEFORM RULES

  • Integrator: step → ramp; sine → −cosine; square → triangle; triangle → parabola.
  • Differentiator: ramp → step; cosine → −sine; triangle → square; square → spikes.
  • Both: phase shift ±90° (integ −90°, diff +90°).
  • Slope: integ −20 dB/dec; diff +20 dB/dec; cross at 1/(2πRC).
  • Always inverting; add a unity-gain stage if you want positive sign.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • No R_f on integrator → DC offset of op-amp drifts to rail in seconds.
  • No R_s on differentiator → HF noise dominates; output is unusable.
  • Electrolytic C in integrator → leakage current adds to drift; use NP0/C0G ceramic or film.
  • Sloppy reset in pulsed integrators — switch must short C cleanly between cycles. Use FET (CD4066) or SPDT switch.
  • Forgetting bias current for differential bias matching — add R = R1‖R2 in series with non-inverting input.
  • Treating an OPA at HF as ideal — finite GBW puts a hard ceiling on the bandwidth of the differentiator.
