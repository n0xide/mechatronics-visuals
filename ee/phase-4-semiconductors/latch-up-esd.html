Latch-up & ESD

Every CMOS chip has a parasitic SCR built into the silicon — a hidden PNP+NPN pair that, if triggered, shorts V_dd to V_ss until the supply is cut. Three things wake it up: I/O pin driven below V_ss or above V_dd, a supply transient that flips the substrate bias, or an ESD strike. This visual shows the parasitic structure, the trigger conditions, and the on-chip clamps and external networks that keep the SCR asleep.

The parasitic SCR (why CMOS has a hidden latch)

A bulk-CMOS inverter has a PMOS in an n-well sitting on a p-substrate next to an NMOS in the substrate. Four alternating doping layers (P+/N-well/P-sub/N+) form two cross-coupled BJTs: a vertical PNP (PMOS source = emitter, n-well = base, p-sub = collector) and a lateral NPN (NMOS source = emitter, p-sub = base, n-well = collector). The PNP's collector drives the NPN's base; the NPN's collector drives the PNP's base. That's an SCR.

Quiet state: n-well tied to V_dd, p-sub tied to V_ss → both BJT base–emitter junctions reverse-biased → leakage only (nA). Trigger: a current dump into the p-sub raises V_sub above V_ss by ~0.6 V → NPN turns on → its collector current pulls down the n-well → PNP turns on → its collector current pushes up the p-sub. Lock: loop gain > 1 once both transistors conduct. Both turn each other on harder. → SCR latches. Hold: V_dd → V_ss now sourced through the saturated PNP+NPN at ~1.5 V drop. Current ramps until package or bond wire opens (or you cycle V_dd).

ESD models — three different waveshapes

Datasheet "ESD: ±2 kV HBM" means the chip survives the Human Body Model test at 2 kV. Each model captures a different real-world failure mode; passing one is not passing the others.

CDM is the meanest: peak current as high as HBM but the rise time is so fast that on-chip clamps can lag. Modern parts often spec CDM lower (±500 V) than HBM (±2 kV) and rely on careful pad-cell layout for the rest.

Clamp diodes — the SCR's bouncer

Every modern I/O pad has two clamp diodes: one from pin → V_dd (catches overshoot), one from V_ss → pin (catches undershoot). They are not for normal current — they are reverse-biased in operation, forward-biased only when the pin tries to exceed the rail. The datasheet spec usually allows ±10 mA through these clamps for short durations.

Clamp current: 0 mA

Adding an external Schottky (V_f ≈ 0.3 V) in parallel with the internal clamp diverts most of the surge — the internal silicon diode (V_f ≈ 0.7 V) never reaches the conduction threshold. A series resistor (typically 100–1000 Ω) between the external connector and the pin limits even a clamp-diode-protected pin to a few mA peak.

The hard rule

Never exceed V_ss − 0.3 V or V_dd + 0.3 V on any pin, ever — including transients. The 0.3 V budget is the conduction threshold of the on-chip clamp diodes. Above that, current flows through the clamp; sustained currents above ~10 mA can trigger latch-up.

The three lines of defense

  1. Clamp the connector. TVS diode (rated for the line's ESD environment) between every external connector pin and ground.
  2. Limit the series current. Series resistor 100 Ω – 10 kΩ between connector and chip pin. Pick the upper bound by signal bandwidth (RC formed with pin capacitance).
  3. Backstop with the chip's own clamps. The internal diodes catch what the external network missed. They are a backstop, not a primary defense.

Worked example — RP2350 GPIO sees a 6 kV ESD strike at 1 m cable end

The constraints

External I/O on the fold-p4-pico cyberdeck: GPIO routed through a header to an off-board sensor PCB. RP2350 GPIO abs-max = V_ss − 0.5 V to V_dd + 0.5 V (5 ns transient, datasheet 5.1). Sustained clamp current ≤ 10 mA.

The arithmetic — sized for HBM 2 kV at the chip pad

V_HBM = 2 kV, R_HBM_source = 1500 Ω (model) I_peak (no protection) = 2000 / 1500 = 1.33 A (~130× the 10 mA budget — well into latch territory) Add TVS at connector (clamp 6 V): TVS clamps to ~5.5 V above V_dd during strike Residual into chip after TVS = ~30–50 mA (TVS dynamic R + lead inductance) Series R_s = 220 Ω limits clamp-diode current to 50 mA × (clamp / (clamp + 220 / I_load)) ≈ 6–8 mA (within abs-max) Signal-bandwidth check: R_s × C_pin = 220 × 5 pF = 1.1 ns → −3 dB at ~145 MHz. Fine for I²C/SPI/UART.

The pad-cell side

For CDM (sub-nanosecond rise), the external network can't help — the strike fires before the TVS turns on. CDM survival depends on the chip's pad-cell layout: ESD diode placement (close to the bond pad), ESD bus routing (low-inductance V_dd-to-V_ss strap), and ballast resistance in the output driver. This is silicon design, not your problem unless you're spinning a chip — but it's why "datasheet says CDM ±500 V" is a real number you need to respect.

What to notice

  • The 0.3 V budget is hard. Above V_dd + 0.3 V or below V_ss − 0.3 V the clamps conduct. The number isn't a margin — it's the silicon diode V_f. Don't design assuming you have more.
  • Latch-up survives power-on. Once the SCR fires, only cycling V_dd resets it. Holding the offending pin away from the rail does not clear it. This is what makes latch-up worse than a normal abs-max violation.
  • CDM is the modern killer. Hand assembly and ESD-safe stations handle HBM fine. CDM happens in automated handlers, robotic pick-and-place, or when a charged board is plugged into a grounded socket. Modern chips spec CDM separately because the protection strategies differ.
  • Series R + TVS > series R alone. A TVS at the connector handles the huge currents; the series resistor handles the residual. Either one alone is not enough above a few hundred volts.
  • Schottky clamps beat silicon clamps for overshoot. External Schottky V_f ≈ 0.3 V conducts before the internal V_f ≈ 0.7 V clamp, diverting the surge. Pair with a low-leakage type (BAT54 family) so quiescent leakage doesn't matter.
  • "Ratings violated → bond wire opens." Latch-up current is limited only by package parasitics. If your supply can deliver an amp into the SCR, the bond wire eventually opens — the chip survives as a coaster, the trace burns through, and downstream parts inherit the surge.

Where you'll see this

Real situations

  • Cyberdeck external connectors — anything that leaves the enclosure (USB, headers, jacks) needs TVS + series R. fold-p4-pico GPIO breakouts are the canonical case.
  • Hot-plug events — connecting a powered cable into a grounded chassis is a worst-case CDM scenario. Capacitance on the cable side dumps through the first pin to make contact.
  • 5 V signals into a 3.3 V part — a sensor running at 5 V driving a 3.3 V GPIO through a wire is a latch-up trigger waiting for a level shift. Either level-shift or series-limit.
  • Inductive loads released through GPIO — a relay coil or stepper winding flying back through a freewheel diode that isn't fast enough can drive a pin below V_ss. The flyback diode is the protection; pick a Schottky or an ultra-fast.
  • Supply rail bring-up order — multi-rail parts (V_dda + V_ddc + V_io) must come up in the order the datasheet says. Bring V_io up before V_ddc and you'll forward-bias an internal diode and trigger latch.

Cheat sheet

NUMBERS TO MEMORIZE

  • Clamp turn-on: V_rail ± 0.3 V (Si) or ± 0.3 V (Schottky)
  • HBM: 100 pF · 1.5 kΩ · 2 kV → ~1.3 A peak, 150 ns decay
  • MM: 200 pF · ~0 Ω · 200 V → ~4 A peak, ringing
  • CDM: package C · bond-L · 1 kV → ~5 A peak, <1 ns
  • Clamp diode budget: ±10 mA typical, see datasheet abs-max
  • Series R: 100 Ω – 10 kΩ outside-world to pin

DESIGN RULES

  • Never exceed V_ss − 0.3 V or V_dd + 0.3 V at any pin.
  • Power up V_io last unless datasheet says otherwise.
  • TVS + series R on every connector pin that leaves the board.
  • Schottky clamps for overshoot if Si is too slow / V_f too high.
  • Decouple every V_dd pin within ~5 mm with 100 nF + bulk.
  • Hand assembly: wrist strap + grounded mat. Robotic: ionizer + ESD trays.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • "It's only 5 V into a 3.3 V pin." 5 − 3.3 = 1.7 V over V_dd. Clamp conducts at 0.3 V. Latch-up territory.
  • Forgetting the abs-max is transient too. A ringing edge that briefly hits V_dd + 1 V at 100 MHz is just as bad as a DC violation.
  • TVS clamp voltage > chip abs-max. Pick a TVS whose clamp voltage at peak current is still below abs-max, not just the standoff voltage.
  • Reverse-current path during power-down. If V_io stays high while V_dd drops, internal diodes power the chip backwards. Add a reverse-blocking diode or sequence rails.
  • "Hand-set parts on a wooden bench." No mat, no strap — every part you touch is a CDM event in waiting.
