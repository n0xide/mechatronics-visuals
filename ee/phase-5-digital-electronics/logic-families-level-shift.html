Why level shifting is a real problem

The mistake "just connect 5 V to 3.3 V — it'll be fine" murders chips. The 3.3 V chip's GPIO has internal ESD diodes from pin → V_CC; any voltage above V_CC + 0.7 dumps current through them. Sustained 5 V applied = ~milliamps through tiny diodes = die ablation. Even if you survive, the input "sees" 5 V and may not interpret it as logic high if there's hysteresis or comparator mismatch.

Direction matters: HIGH → LOW (need to drop V_high to V_low — easy) vs LOW → HIGH (need to drive higher than your rail — needs a "switch" to V_high) Drive style: push-pull actively pulls both 0 and 1; open-drain (or open-collector) only pulls 0, requires external pull-up resistor for 1. Bidirectional: I2C and 1-Wire need a single wire that's both input and output for both chips — a true bidirectional level shifter (BSS138 / PCA9306) is required. → The right shifter depends on direction, push-pull-vs-open-drain, and speed.

The four common topologies in detail

1. Resistor divider

Two resistors form a voltage divider that drops V_high to V_low. Cheap, but: only one direction (HIGH → LOW), and slow because the divider's output impedance plus the load capacitance gives an RC time constant.

For 5 V → 3.3 V: V_low = V_high × R2/(R1+R2) = 3.3 → R2/R1 = 1.94 Pick R1 = 1 kΩ, R2 = 1.8 kΩ (E12). Standby current: 5/2800 = 1.78 mA. Output impedance to ground = R1‖R2 = 643 Ω. τ = R_out · C_load = 643 · 100p = 64.3 ns; t_r (10–90 %) ≈ 2.2τ = 141 ns → max f ≈ 2 MHz at 100 pF. → Works for UART (115k baud), GPIO buttons, slow signals. Fails for fast SPI > 1 MHz.

2. BSS138 N-MOSFET (the SparkFun shifter)

Single N-channel MOSFET with the gate tied to V_low (the lower supply). When V_low side is pulled low, V_gs = V_low > V_th, the MOSFET turns on, pulling V_high side low via its body diode and channel. When V_high side is pulled low, current flows through the body diode from V_low side, and then the MOSFET turns on too. Bidirectional, but slow because the rising edge relies on the pull-up resistor charging the cap (no active pull-up).

Low → low pulls both sides low (active). High → high relies on pull-ups (R_pu) to charge load cap up to V_high or V_low. τ_rise = R_pu × C_load on each side; max f ≈ 1/(5·τ_rise) typically. R_pu = 4.7 kΩ, C_load = 100 pF: τ = 470 ns → max f ≈ 425 kHz. → I2C standard 100 kHz easy; I2C fast 400 kHz marginal; I2C+ 1 MHz fails.

3. TXB0104 / TXS010x (auto-direction)

Active shifter with a tiny one-shot detection circuit. When it sees a transition on either side, it active drives the other side to match. Push-pull capable up to 60 MHz on TXB0104. Cannot drive open-drain or contend with pull-ups — it will fight them.

Each side has a 4 kΩ "weak" output (TXB0104) or ~10 kΩ for TXS0108. When external pull-up is below ~40 kΩ, the active drive can't override it — the chip "loses" arbitration. → For I2C: use PCA9306, not TXB. For SPI/UART/MII: TXB or TXS works great.

4. 74LVC1T45 (latch-direction)

Direction is set by an external DIR pin (one bit). Pure push-pull both sides, up to 420 Mbps. Fast, predictable, but you need a GPIO to set DIR — one shifter per direction-determined pair.

Worked example — 5 V Arduino I2C to a 3.3 V sensor

The setup

Arduino Uno (5 V) running 100 kHz I2C to BME280 3.3 V barometer. Distance ~10 cm. Pull-ups 4.7 kΩ on each side.

Pick the shifter

Need: bidirectional, open-drain compatible, ≥100 kHz BSS138 dual: τ_rise = 4.7k · 100p = 470 ns → t_r = 1030 ns → f_max ≈ 200 kHz ✓ PCA9306: tighter spec for I2C, ≤400 kHz; better choice for any fast-mode I2C ✓ TXB0104: NO — would fight the pull-ups ✗

The wiring (BSS138)

Drain of SDA-shifter MOSFET = 5 V SDA (+ pull-up); Source = 3.3 V SDA (+ pull-up); Gate = 3.3 V (the lower rail) Same for SCL

SparkFun's "BOB-12009" is exactly this — two BSS138s and four pull-ups on a breakout.

Worked example — 1.8 V Cortex SPI flash to 3.3 V Pico

The setup

Pico (3.3 V) reads a 1.8 V W25Q64 SPI flash at 25 MHz. Direction: Pico → flash (MOSI, CLK, CS push-pull); flash → Pico (MISO push-pull).

Pick the shifter

Need: push-pull, ≥25 MHz, 4 lines (3 down-shift + 1 up-shift) Resistor divider: only goes 3.3 → 1.8. Need separate solution for MISO. TXB0104 (4-bit auto-dir): handles all 4 lines, push-pull, 60 MHz ✓ 74LVC4T245 (4-bit latched direction): also works, push-pull only ✓ BSS138: too slow (~400 kHz with realistic pull-ups) ✗

Decision

TXB0104 wins — auto-direction is convenient for MISO when no separate DIR is wired up. Cost: ~$1.50 SOIC.

What to notice

  • 5 V tolerance ≠ 5 V drive. A "5 V tolerant" 3.3 V pin can accept 5 V input (no ESD damage) but its output is still 3.3 V max. To drive a 5 V load needing a hard high, you still need to shift up.
  • Resistor dividers fail above ~1 MHz because of RC time constant. Below that, they're cheap, no parts, and immune to direction confusion.
  • BSS138 is the bench staple for I2C, but slow. 1 MHz I2C+ already strains it. For 1 MHz+ I2C use PCA9306; for 5 MHz+ I2C... rethink topology.
  • Auto-direction shifters (TXB) fight pull-ups. If your protocol uses pull-ups (I2C, 1-Wire), use BSS138/PCA9306. If it doesn't (SPI/UART/standard GPIO), use TXB.
  • Common ground is required for all shifters. The two sides must share GND. Galvanic isolation is a separate problem (use opto-couplers or digital isolators like ADuM1xx0).
  • Input impedance matters for high-speed signals. A divider with 1 kΩ output impedance is fine for SPI at 1 MHz; at 50 MHz, it becomes a transmission-line mismatch and causes reflections.
  • Don't forget power sequencing. Many shifters require V_low ≥ V_high power-on; some die if reversed. Tie OE to a power-good signal.

Where you'll level-shift

Real situations

  • Sensors at sensor voltage — BME280 (3.3 V), AS5048A (3.3 V), HX711 (5 V) all talk to your 3.3 V MCU through level shifters.
  • Legacy 5 V boards — Arduino Uno + 3.3 V breakout.
  • SD cards (3.3 V) on 5 V Arduinos — typically MOSI/CLK divider down, MISO already 3.3 V (capable of 5 V interpretation).
  • FPGA Bank voltage — some banks at 1.8 V, others at 3.3 V; need shifters between banks.
  • Multi-rail systems — 12 V industrial input + 5 V relay logic + 3.3 V MCU + 1.8 V flash; up to 4 voltage domains.

SHIFTER QUICK-PICK

  • I2C ≤ 400 kHz → BSS138 / PCA9306
  • I2C 1 MHz+ → PCA9306
  • SPI / UART push-pull → TXB0104
  • SPI 50 MHz+ → 74LVC1T45 / 4T245
  • Single direction, slow → resistor divider
  • Industrial 12-24 V → BSS138 or optocoupler

COMMON PITFALLS

  • TXB0104 on I2C — fights the pull-ups; SDA gets corrupted.
  • Resistor divider for fast SPI — rise time kills the signal at >1 MHz.
  • Missing pull-ups on open-drain shifter — line floats, comms fail.
  • OE pin floating on TXB/TXS — chip enters indeterminate state. Tie OE to V_low via 10kΩ pull-up + assert when ready.
  • Power-on sequence — apply V_low BEFORE V_high (for most shifters); reverse order can latch up the chip.
  • Mixed-rail current path — some shifters create a current path through ESD diodes if one rail is off. Use an OE-controlled shifter for hot-swap.
