Setup, Hold & Metastability

A D flip-flop captures whatever's on D at the clock edge — but only if D was stable for t_setup before the edge and t_hold after. Violate either window and you get metastability: the internal node hovers between 0 and 1 for an unpredictable time before resolving. Slide the input edge across the clock edge to see the violation, the indeterminate Q output, and the mean-time-between-failure that drives the synchronizer chain in every asynchronous interface.

What "setup" and "hold" actually mean

A D flip-flop has a clock pin and a data pin. On the rising clock edge, the internal master latch closes — and what is sampled depends on whether D was steady. The chip has a tiny aperture, t_su before + t_h after, in which D must not change. Inside this window, the master and slave latches are both transparent for an instant; any glitch passes straight through.

t_setup (t_su): minimum time D must be valid BEFORE the clock edge. Typical: 1–20 ns. t_hold (t_h): minimum time D must remain valid AFTER the clock edge. Typical: 0–5 ns. t_co (clock-to-output): time from clk edge until Q reflects D. Typical: 5–15 ns. If t_su is met: Q updates cleanly to D at t_co. If t_su is violated by a margin (D changed too close to edge): internal node may not be at a "safe" voltage when latch closes. → Result: metastability — Q oscillates or hangs at the threshold until thermal noise breaks the tie.

Metastability: the indeterminate state

Inside a D-FF, a bistable element (two cross-coupled inverters) wants to settle into either VOH or VOL. If the input snapshot is exactly at the metastable voltage (mid-rail), the two inverters provide equal gain and the loop has no preference. The voltage drifts toward one rail or the other due to thermal noise — exponentially fast once it starts, but the start time is unpredictable.

Resolution time follows: P(t_res > t) = exp(−t/τ), where τ is the inverter time constant (~50 ps in modern CMOS, ~1 ns in 74HC). Mean time between failures: MTBF = exp(t_res/τ) / (T_0·f_data·f_clk) T_0 is a process constant (~10⁻¹³ s); higher is worse. For t_res = 10 ns, τ = 200 ps, T_0 = 10⁻¹³ s, f_clk = 100 MHz, f_data = 1 MHz: MTBF = e^50 / (10⁻¹³·10⁶·10⁸) = 5.18·10²¹ / 10 ≈ 5·10²⁰ s — never happens. For t_res = 2 ns, τ = 200 ps: e^10 ≈ 22 000 ; divide by (T_0·f_data·f_clk = 10) ⇒ MTBF ≈ 2200 s ≈ 37 minutes between failures. Marginal — needs another stage. → The fix is to provide MORE t_res by adding flip-flops in the chain — each stage gives another e^(Tclk/τ) margin.

The synchronizer chain

When an asynchronous signal (UART RX, button press, sensor edge) enters a synchronous system, you MUST clock it through 2 or 3 flip-flops in sequence — the "synchronizer." The first FF may go metastable, but it has a full clock period to resolve before the second FF samples it. After 2 stages, MTBF is astronomical for typical systems.

Worked example — UART RX into a 100 MHz FPGA

The setup

UART at 115,200 baud feeds a 100 MHz FPGA clock domain. RX is asynchronous; we need a 2-FF synchronizer. FPGA register: t_su = 0.3 ns, τ = 50 ps, T_0 = 10⁻¹³.

The math

T_clk = 10 ns; allowed resolution time per stage = 10 − 0.3 − 0.5 (clk-to-out) ≈ 9.2 ns MTBF per stage = exp(9.2 ns / 50 ps) / (1e-13 · 115,200 · 100e6) = e^184 / 1.15 ≈ 10^80 s — far beyond universe age For 1 stage alone: already astronomical MTBF. 2-stage chain: probability of double-stage failure = 10⁻⁸⁰ — universe heat death first.

The catch

You still need to handle the case where the synchronized RX edge lands between bit periods (sampling phase). That's a separate problem (handled by oversampling at 16× baud rate), not a synchronizer issue.

Worked example — slow MCU sampling a fast async signal

The setup

STM32L0 at 16 MHz CPU clock samples a 100 kHz pulse train from an external sensor. GPIO has t_su = 1.5 ns, t_h = 0.5 ns, τ = 200 ps.

The math (1 stage)

T_clk = 62.5 ns; per-stage margin = 62.5 − 1.5 − 0.5 = 60.5 ns MTBF = exp(60.5 / 0.2) / (1e-13 · 100k · 16M) = e^302 / 0.16 — universe-grade safe with 1 stage. But the MCU itself has internal pipelining — multiple registers samples it. You still need a 2-stage explicit sync if you read the pin in software-polled mode (no auto-resync between SAMPLEs of the IDR register). In practice: read twice with a 1-instruction delay; only accept the read if both match.

What to notice

  • Setup is the most violated — most digital signals arrive close to the clock edge, often "just in time." Hold is rarely violated except when D arrives after the edge.
  • Hold can fail if your clock is too fast OR D arrives too late — common when D has minimum delay through fast paths.
  • Q during metastability is NOT 0 or 1. It's an undefined voltage, often midway between rails. Downstream logic sampling it sees random behavior.
  • Two-stage synchronizers are non-negotiable for crossing clock domains. Even with 0.001 % probability of metastability propagating, it'll bite once a year.
  • Don't fan out an async signal to multiple destinations until after the synchronizer. Each destination samples differently; you'll get inconsistent states across the system.
  • Inter-clock-domain FIFOs are the only safe way to send data across clock domains. They use a 2-FF sync on the read/write pointers plus Gray coding.
  • Reset signals are async too. Released asynchronously? Use an async-assert, sync-deassert reset synchronizer.

Where this matters

Real situations

  • Button presses — async input from user; debounce + 2-FF sync before the state machine.
  • UART / SPI / I2C async sampling — incoming bits aren't aligned with your clock; first thing to do is synchronize.
  • Clock-domain crossing in FPGAs — every signal crossing a clock domain boundary must go through a sync. Vivado / Quartus will flag missing syncs as critical warnings.
  • External interrupt pins — async edge → must sync before NVIC reads it. Most MCUs do this internally; some give you naked edge detection.
  • Reset deassertion — async reset assertion is fine, but deassertion must be synchronous to avoid metastability on PoR.
  • PLL lock signals — output of analog PLL is async to digital; needs sync.
  • USB device controllers — SE0 condition detection from physical layer to logic layer requires sync.

RULES OF THUMB

  • 2-FF synchronizer for all async-to-sync signals — default.
  • 3-FF for safety-critical or ultra-high-clock-rate (1 GHz+).
  • FPGA: set_max_delay between async input and first reg.
  • Synchronizer FFs must be physically adjacent in layout.
  • Gray-code counters when crossing domains (only 1 bit changes per increment).

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Fanning out async signal before sync — different destinations see different states.
  • Mixing sync and async resets in same block.
  • Single-FF sync when speed and async rate are similar — MTBF drops to seconds.
  • Sync chain placed across long wire — defeats the purpose; place adjacent.
  • Ignoring hold in fast paths — short interconnect can violate t_h.
  • Forgetting reset sync — async reset release causes startup metastability.
  • Sampling slow signals with too-fast clock — Whittle MTBF; analyze before deploying.
