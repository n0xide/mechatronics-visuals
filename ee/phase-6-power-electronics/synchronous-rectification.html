Synchronous Rectification

Replacing the freewheel Schottky diode in a buck converter with a low-side MOSFET — turned on synchronously with the inductor current — drops the conduction loss from V_f·I (typically 0.4 V × I, large) to I²·R_DS(on) (typically 5 mΩ × I², much smaller for high I_load). Critical for efficiency above ~5 A or when running from a low-voltage rail where V_f is a big fraction of V_out. The catch: shoot-through risk if both MOSFETs are on at once. Dead-time avoidance is mandatory.

How it works — the dual-MOSFET buck topology

A standard buck has a high-side switch (HS, P-channel or N-channel with bootstrap) and a freewheel diode (D_FW). During the "off" portion of each cycle, when HS is open, the inductor current must continue flowing — D_FW provides the path with V_f drop.

Synchronous rectification replaces D_FW with a low-side N-channel MOSFET (LS). When HS turns off, LS turns on. The inductor current flows through LS's channel with R_DS(on) × I² drop instead of V_f × I drop.

Schottky loss in freewheel: P_D = V_f · I_L · (1−D) where (1−D) is the off-time duty fraction. Sync MOSFET loss in freewheel: P_LS = I_L² · R_DS(on) · (1−D) Crossover current: P_D = P_LS → V_f = I_crossover · R_DS(on) → I_crossover = V_f / R_DS(on) For V_f = 0.4 V, R_DS = 10 mΩ: I_crossover = 40 A. Below 40 A, sync wins by I²/40 factor. For V_f = 0.4 V, R_DS = 100 mΩ: I_crossover = 4 A. Below 4 A, sync barely helps; above 4 A diode wins.

The shoot-through problem (and dead-time fix)

Both MOSFETs cannot be on at once — that would short V_in to ground through the two channels. The gate driver must turn off the outgoing FET BEFORE turning on the incoming one. This gap is called dead time.

During dead time, the inductor current still needs a path. The LS body diode conducts (with V_f similar to a Schottky, ~0.7 V for typical Si MOSFET body diodes). After LS turns on, the channel takes over and the body diode stops conducting. The shorter the dead time, the less body-diode loss.

The tradeoff: dead time too short → shoot-through, possibly catastrophic. Dead time too long → body-diode losses + reverse-recovery losses. Typical optimal: 5–50 ns depending on FET gate charge + drive current. Many modern gate drivers (LM5117, TPS51427, DRV8350) measure the drain voltage and adapt dead-time on the fly.

Worked example — 5 V to 3.3 V buck @ 5 A

The numbers

D = V_out/V_in = 3.3/5 = 0.66 → (1−D) = 0.34 Schottky path: P = 0.4 V × 5 A × 0.34 = 0.68 W Sync MOSFET (10 mΩ): P = 25 A² × 0.010 Ω × 0.34 = 0.085 W Sync savings: 0.68 − 0.085 = 0.60 W → 0.60 W / 16.5 W output = 3.6 % efficiency gain For laptop / smartphone power conversion 1-5 A: 1-2 % efficiency gain matters for battery life.

When NOT to bother with sync

If I_load < V_f/R_DS_on = 0.4/0.010 = 40 A, sync wins. But: At 1 A load, sync saves only 0.4 × 1 × 0.34 − 1² × 0.010 × 0.34 = 0.133 W (out of 3.3 W) = 4 %. Marginal vs added gate-driver complexity. Below ~0.5 A, Schottky often wins (sync MOSFET capacitive switching losses overshadow the conduction-loss win).

Modern controllers

TPS6213x / LM5117 / RT9603: all have integrated low-side FET with adaptive dead-time. Programmable I_skip to drop to Schottky-equivalent at very low loads.

Pitfalls

  • Body-diode reverse recovery — when LS turns off and HS turns on, the body diode of LS has stored charge that must be cleared. This causes a current spike through HS during the dead-edge. Use FETs with low Q_rr (e.g., GaN, modern trench MOSFETs) or schottky in parallel with body diode to bypass.
  • Bootstrap cap sizing — HS gate driver needs a charged bootstrap cap. The cap recharges only when LS is on. At very high duty cycles (D > 95 %), the cap may not refresh enough. Use external charge-pump driver or pulse-skip the LS for refresh.
  • Dead-time too long — body-diode conduction creates 2× the Schottky loss for the dead-time interval. At 5 % of period both ways, that's significant.
  • Cross-conduction during transient — fast load steps can momentarily turn on both FETs through Miller charge injection on the slow-side gate. Use gate-driver with high pull-down strength.
  • Reverse current issues — sync converters can sink current (motor regen), which a diode cannot. Useful for bi-directional, but can dump unwanted current into the supply.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

  • D = V_out / V_in (CCM)
  • P_D = V_f · I · (1−D)
  • P_LS = I² · R_DS(on) · (1−D)
  • I_crossover = V_f / R_DS(on)
  • P_sw = ½·C_oss·V² · f_sw (sync MOSFET adds switching loss)
  • P_body_diode = V_fb · I · 2·t_dead·f_sw

SHORTCUTS

  • Sync helps when I > V_f/R_DS
  • Below ~1 A, often skip sync (use diode)
  • Above ~5 A, sync usually wins
  • Below 0.5 A: Schottky often optimal
  • For ultra-low V_out (1 V or less), sync mandatory
  • Hi-Q FETs + adaptive dead-time → 95+ % efficiency

PITFALLS

  • Shoot-through — dead time too short = catastrophic.
  • Body-diode Q_rr — uses energy and reduces efficiency.
  • Bootstrap depletion at high D.
  • Miller turn-on — slow-side gate can sneak on.
  • Bi-directional unwanted — sync can sink current motor-side.
