Decoupling Capacitors

Real capacitors have parasitic inductance (ESL) and resistance (ESR). Above the self-resonant frequency they look inductive — useless for high-frequency decoupling. Stacking values gives broader coverage, but the join creates an anti-resonance notch.

The classic 100 nF + 1 µF parallel pair you see by every IC is not redundancy — it's two devices, each useful in a different frequency band. Together they cover wider bandwidth than either alone, but only if you understand where the bands meet.

The math behind SRF

A real capacitor is a series RLC: ideal C in series with parasitic ESL (lead inductance, package geometry) and ESR (loss). Impedance magnitude:

|Z(f)| = √(ESR² + (ωL_ESL − 1/(ωC))²) SRF: ω_SRF·L_ESL = 1/(ω_SRF·C) ⇒ f_SRF = 1 / (2π·√(L_ESL · C)) At SRF: |Z| = ESR (minimum) Below SRF: capacitive — impedance drops as 1/(ωC) Above SRF: inductive — impedance grows as ωL_ESL

Worked example — STM32 100 MHz processor decoupling network

Spec

Cortex-M7 at 200 MHz core (100 MHz I/O), peak transient current 200 mA on a 3.3 V rail. Target PDN impedance < 20 mΩ across DC to 200 MHz.

Single cap solutions

100 nF alone (0402, ESL=0.8 nH): SRF = 1/(2π·√(0.8e-9·100e-9)) = 17.8 MHz
|Z| @ 100 MHz = 2π·100e6·0.8e-9 = 503 mΩ ← WAY above target. 100 nF alone fails above ~18 MHz.

Paired network

100 nF + 10 nF + 1 nF in parallel (all 0402, ESL=0.8 nH each)
SRFs: 17.8 MHz (100 nF) · 56 MHz (10 nF) · 178 MHz (1 nF) — staggered by ~3× each
@ 100 MHz (all three above their SRF, so ESL dominates): each is roughly inductive at ~500 mΩ; 1 nF is just past its 178 MHz SRF and runs ~1 Ω capacitive. Three 0.8 nH ESLs in parallel = ~0.27 nH eff → |Z| ≈ 170 mΩ at 100 MHz — still ~8× the 20 mΩ target.
Real fix: cut the effective ESL — wide vias straight into the GND/PWR plane pair, IC's power pin sharing the via field. Layout ESL, not cap value, sets the high-frequency floor.
Add 10 µF bulk (0805, ESL=1 nH, SRF=1.6 MHz) to cover the low frequencies

Anti-resonance warning

Between 100 nF (17.8 MHz) and 10 nF (56 MHz), the 100 nF is inductive while the 10 nF is still capacitive. They form a parallel LC tank — peak near ~40 MHz on the swept plot. With low-ESR ceramics, the |Z| peak can spike to several hundred mΩ — worse than either cap alone in that band. Mitigations: stagger ratio ≤ 4× between adjacent caps, or use a deliberately higher-ESR cap (polymer tantalum) between the two to damp the resonance.

What to notice

  • Every cap is an inductor at some frequency. The "decoupling cap" name only describes the bottom of the V.
  • SRF scales as 1/√LC. Smaller C → higher SRF. That's the whole reason a 10 nF cap is useful at 50 MHz while a 1 µF (~5 MHz SRF) is not.
  • Parallel ≠ free. Between the two SRFs, one cap is inductive and the other is capacitive — they form a parallel LC tank. At its resonance, |Z| spikes (anti-resonance). The notch can be 10× higher than either cap alone.
  • ESR damps the notch. Slide ESR up — the anti-resonance peak shrinks. That's why too-low-ESR caps (high-Q ceramics) sometimes need to be intentionally damped.
  • Layout dominates. The "ESL" you actually have is mostly trace inductance — the cap-to-pin-to-via loop. A nominally 0.8 nH 0402 in a sloppy layout can end up with 5 nH of effective ESL, killing its SRF.

Cheat sheet

MEMORIZE

  • 100 nF MLCC 0402 SRF ≈ 18 MHz (ESL ≈ 0.8 nH).
  • Pair 100 nF + 1 µF for wide-band coverage.
  • Bulk 10–100 µF at supply entry.

DECISION — PLACEMENT

  • Cap goes between IC power pin and via to GND, as close as physically possible. Minimise loop area; that's most of the real ESL.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Anti-resonance notch when paralleling caps of different SRF — |Z| spikes between the two. Measure or simulate before trusting it.
