Ground Bounce & Topology

Ground isn't a magic infinity; it's a wire (or copper pour, or plane) with inductance. When a switching MOSFET dumps 2 A through the trace in 5 ns, V = L·di/dt creates a transient voltage at the "ground" pin of nearby ICs — that's ground bounce. The fix isn't more ground wire — it's the right topology: star (one common point), daisy-chain (worst), or single-point grounding. Slide the inductance, the di/dt, and the chosen topology; watch the transient voltage at the sensor amplifier rise and fall.

Why ground is never zero

The textbook says "ground = 0 V everywhere." The textbook lies. Every centimeter of trace, every via, every cable has inductance. Push current through inductance and you get voltage: V = L·di/dt. The faster the current changes (CMOS edges in nanoseconds), the bigger the voltage spike across the "ground" wire.

Wire inductance (rule of thumb): ~1 nH per mm for thin trace; ~0.3 nH/mm for a wide trace over a ground plane. A 100 nH wire (10 cm trace) carrying 1 A switched in 5 ns: V_GB = 100e-9 · 1/(5e-9) = 20 V transient. That 20 V appears between two points labeled "GND" on the schematic. The "ground" of a sensitive ADC near the switcher just moved by 20 V for 5 nanoseconds. Effect: anything referenced to the bouncing ground sees the bounce subtracted from its signal — false readings, false triggers, comparator firings.

The four ground topologies, ranked

1. Daisy-chain (BAD)

Each chip's ground pin connects to the next chip's ground pin, like Christmas lights. Switching current in one chip flows through all the intervening grounds. The far chip sees the ground bounce of every switcher between it and the source.

2. Star (better)

All ground pins return to a single common point with separate wires. Each wire has its own L; switching current in one branch doesn't lift the voltage of another branch — the branches are independent paths to the common point.

3. Single-point (best for mixed-signal)

Analog ground (AGND) and digital ground (DGND) are separate nets joined at exactly one point — usually under the ADC chip itself. Digital switching currents flow in the DGND loop and never enter the AGND loop. This is the textbook split-ground approach, drawn on every datasheet.

4. Solid ground plane (best for high-frequency)

An uninterrupted layer of copper on the PCB. Return current naturally takes the path of least impedance (which at HF is also the path of least inductance, directly under the trace). Effectively zero impedance for most loop currents — the ground bounce drops to noise levels.

The split-ground rule (and the exception)

Mixed-signal boards (analog + digital) traditionally split the ground plane and bridge the two nets at a single point. But: if you place a high-speed signal trace over the split, its return current has nowhere to go — it loops around the long way and creates massive ground noise. The modern rule is:

  • Don't cross splits with high-speed signals — keep digital signals over digital ground, analog over analog.
  • If you can't avoid it: bridge the split with a small-value stitching capacitor (0.1 µF, low-ESL package) directly under the signal so the return current has an AC path across the split.
  • Better: no split at all. A solid plane works for most modern designs. The split-ground habit is from the through-hole era; SMD parts now use bypass caps and component placement to avoid noise coupling.

Worked example — 16-bit SAR ADC near a buck switcher

The setup

5 V → 3.3 V buck switcher, 500 kHz, MOSFET switches 2 A in ~5 ns edge. A nearby 16-bit SAR ADC (e.g., ADS8866-class, ±2.5 V FS, ~76 µV LSB, sub-µs S/H aperture) sampling a 3.3 V-rail signal. Switcher MOSFET-source trace length to bulk GND: 30 mm narrow + 15 mm wide.

The math

Trace L: 30·1 nH + 15·0.3 nH = 34.5 nH (narrow + wide) Switching current di/dt: 2 A / 5 ns = 4·10⁸ A/s V_GB at switcher's ground pin: 34.5e-9 · 4e8 = 13.8 V peak (5 ns spike) If the ADC shares this ground return, its reference moves by 13.8 V for 5 ns. A SAR aperture of ~100 ns–1 µs straddles many such spikes per conversion. Effect: 13.8 V / 76 µV per LSB ≈ 180 000 LSB of disturbance — the LSB count is meaningless; the conversion is corrupted whenever a switching edge lands inside the aperture.

The fix

Star ground: separate trace from ADC GND to PSU GND; the ADC's spoke sees only the residual inductance of the star-point connection. If that residual is ~3 nH and the switcher's 4×10⁸ A/s edge couples through it with k ≈ 0.2 (typical for short shared trunk), V_at_ADC ≈ k · 3 nH · 4×10⁸ ≈ 0.24 V — better, still bad for a 76 µV LSB. Single-point AGND/DGND: ADC's AGND pin goes to its own quiet plane; DGND goes to digital plane. Tied at one point under ADC. Effective coupling: pF-scale. Best: solid ground plane below the entire layout, switcher and ADC each placed locally; return currents take direct paths under their signals.

Worked example — STM32 GPIO toggling a relay coil

The setup

STM32 toggles a 100 mA relay coil through a transistor. Edge rate of the MCU GPIO: 10 ns. Connecting trace to relay = 20 mm narrow. Sensitive ADC reads strain gauge on same board.

The math

Edge L: 20 mm · 1 nH/mm = 20 nH Peak gate current: MCU GPIO charging a typical 1 nF effective gate input in 10 ns → i_peak = C·ΔV/Δt = 1 nF · 5 V / 10 ns = 0.5 A. di/dt of that pulse: 0.5 A / 10 ns = 5×10⁷ A/s. V_GB = L · di/dt = 20 nH · 5×10⁷ = 1 V transient on shared GND. Strain-gauge ADC (24-bit, ±0.5 V FS after PGA → LSB ≈ 60 nV; or 24-bit at ±2.5 V FS → LSB ≈ 300 nV): a 1 V transient buries the conversion by 17 M LSB / 3 M LSB respectively — way past anything the digital filter can salvage.

The fix

Add a separate "noisy" ground to relay driver + transistor; tie this ground to the "quiet" ground at a single point near the MCU's main GND. Use a 100 nF cap between drain of FET and source — kills the current spike in the gate drive circuit (snubber).

And: add a return cap (1 µF MLCC) close to the MCU's VDD and GND pin — this provides local current for the switching events, bypassing the long ground return.

What to notice

  • Ground bounce is V = L · di/dt, not IR. R doesn't matter much for nanosecond edges — L dominates. A trace's R = 0.5 mΩ but L = 30 nH; the edge bandwidth of a 1 ns transition is ~350 MHz, where ωL ≈ 66 Ω — five orders of magnitude above the DC resistance.
  • Wider traces have less inductance per unit length, but the difference is < 2× for typical PCBs. The bigger benefit of width is current capacity, not inductance.
  • A solid ground plane reduces L to ~0.1 nH per mm of trace over the plane — 10× better than a thin strip.
  • Bypass caps are local current reservoirs for high-frequency switching. A 100 nF X7R right at the IC's V_CC pin prevents the IC's switching current from traveling 30 mm to the bulk cap, drastically reducing trace di/dt.
  • Star ground works best when every "spoke" is short. A 100 mm spoke has 100 nH; it defeats the purpose. Keep spokes < 30 mm or use a plane.
  • "Mixed signal" ICs (ADC, DAC) define AGND and DGND internally — both pins are at the same potential inside the chip. The split is for routing convenience: keep noisy traces near DGND, quiet traces near AGND.
  • Forward and return current loops create magnetic fields proportional to loop area. Small loop area = small inductance = small bounce. Routing return next to signal is the bedrock of low-noise PCBs.

Where ground bounce kills designs

Real failure modes

  • ADC noise — 12-bit can be reduced to 8-bit by ground bounce; 24-bit to 16-bit.
  • False comparator triggers — high-impedance comparator inputs see the bounce as signal.
  • EMI radiated emissions — the bounce is a small antenna at switching f and harmonics.
  • Crystal oscillator phase noise — ground bounce modulates the load cap, jitters the clock.
  • USB / HDMI / fast SPI eye closure — ground bounce eats jitter budget at signal receiver.
  • Reset glitches — power-on reset can fire spuriously from ground bounce events.
  • SMPS instability — feedback voltage references bounce; switcher hunts and oscillates.
  • Audio hum at switching frequency — if > 20 kHz, you don't hear it; if < 20 kHz (rare for SMPS, common for fan PWM), it whines.

RULES OF THUMB

  • Use solid GND plane; avoid splits unless required.
  • Star ground for sub-MHz mixed-signal.
  • 0.1 µF bypass within 5 mm of every IC's V_CC.
  • Don't cross GND splits with high-speed traces.
  • Keep switcher MOSFET loop area < 100 mm².
  • Place sensor IC far from switcher, ideally on opposite end of board.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • "Wider is better" — partly; can't beat a ground plane.
  • Single-point GND at the wrong place — should be under the noisy current source, not the quiet load.
  • Forgetting return current path — signals on top layer want GND on layer 2 directly under them.
  • Slot in ground plane from connector cutout — return current detours; massive loop, massive inductance.
  • Lifting the GND pin of an IC ("ground bounce isolation") — actually makes it worse, not better.
  • Bypass cap far from chip — defeats the purpose; cap + trace L resonates.
  • "Star at the connector" — usually wrong; star should be at the central noise source (the SMPS GND pin).
