Impedance Matching

Three ways to kill reflections on a digital trace: source termination, load termination, series termination. Each compromises something different — see the resulting waveforms side by side.

Every termination strategy is the same trick — add a resistor equal (or close) to Z0 at the right place. Where you put it decides what's absorbed, what's halved, and how much DC current you pay.

Source termination

RS at the driver, sized so Rdriver + RS = Z0. First wave is half-amplitude into the line; doubles at the open load (Γ=+1); the return reflection is absorbed by RS. Full amplitude settles at load, no DC current. Most common digital choice.

Load termination

RT = Z0 at the far end to GND. Absorbs incident wave at the load — no reflection back to source. Full amplitude at load if Rdriver is small. Pays DC current through the terminator continuously.

Series termination

Identical to source termination in topology — small R in series with driver, sized so driver+R ≈ Z0. Practical recipe for digital outputs: 22–33 Ω discrete resistor next to an FPGA pin. No DC current. The launched wave is V₀/2 (half-amplitude); the +1 reflection at the open load doubles it back to the full source amplitude V₀, which is what the CMOS receiver actually sees. The high-Z receiver doesn't re-reflect, so the stair-step is invisible to it.

PCB microstrip Z0 calculator

Approximate formula: Z0 ≈ (87/√(εr+1.41)) · ln(5.98·H/(0.8·W + T)). Valid when W/H is between 0.1 and 3.

Computed Z0

50 Ω

What "H" means: H is the dielectric height from the trace to the nearest reference plane, NOT the total board thickness. On a 2-layer 1.6 mm board, H ≈ 1.55 mm (the full core) — 50 Ω microstrip needs W ≈ 2.9 mm (~115 mil), impractically wide for most signals (one reason 2-layer boards rarely run controlled-impedance). On a 4-layer board with a ground plane on layer 2, H is the prepreg between L1 and L2 (typ. 7-10 mil = 0.18-0.25 mm); 50 Ω then needs W ≈ 13-15 mil (0.33-0.38 mm) at εr=4.4. Always confirm against fab's actual Dk and stackup.

Reality check: use the Saturn PCB Toolkit or your fab's calculator for the final number — this formula is valid for 0.1 ≤ W/H ≤ 3 and is the rough sanity check.

What to notice

  • No termination = ringing. The default un-terminated trace overshoots, undershoots, and rings — each bounce is a round-trip 2·TD. Visible on every panel before you tune R.
  • Source termination — the cleanest single-resistor fix for digital point-to-point. Full amplitude at load, no DC current, no extra parts at the receiver. Most FPGA pins datasheet a recommended Rseries.
  • Load termination — strongest absorption at the load. Required for distributed loads (memory buses) but burns DC current. AC-coupled (split) variants exist to avoid that.
  • Series ≈ source termination for a high-impedance receiver. The half-amplitude wave doubles at the open load, and the return is killed by Rseries. This is what 22 Ω in series with a clock line is doing.
  • 50 Ω is not magic. Pick Z0 for the system. PC boards default to 50 Ω single-ended because RF instruments do; differential pairs target ~90 Ω (USB 2.0/3) or ~100 Ω (LVDS, Ethernet, PCIe, HDMI).

Cheat sheet

MEMORIZE

  • 50 Ω microstrip ≈ W/H ≈ 2 on FR4 (εr≈4.4). On a 2-layer 1.6 mm board: W ≈ 2.9 mm. On a 4-layer board with ~8 mil (0.2 mm) prepreg to L2 GND: W ≈ 13 mil (0.33 mm).
  • Differential pairs: USB 2.0 / USB 3 ≈ 90 Ω; LVDS / Ethernet / PCIe ≈ 100 Ω; HDMI / DisplayPort ≈ 100 Ω.

DECISION

  • Series term at driver — point-to-point digital, no DC current.
  • Parallel term at receiver — distributed loads, memory buses.
  • AC term (R–C) — bidirectional bus, no DC drop.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Under-termination = ringing · Over-termination = slow edges.
  • Never daisy-chain high-speed signals — stubs reflect.
