Skin Effect — current crowds to the surface at high frequency

At DC, current spreads uniformly through a conductor. At MHz and above, it crowds to the outer "skin" — a layer of thickness δ = √(2ρ/(ωμ)). The effective cross-section shrinks, so AC resistance R_AC rises as √f. This is why high-frequency power inductors use Litz wire (many tiny strands) and why thick PCB power traces don't help above ~10 MHz.

Where it matters

  • High-frequency power inductors (SMPS) — at 100 kHz – 1 MHz switching, even thin wire's R_AC dominates. Use Litz wire (many insulated strands) so each strand stays smaller than δ.
  • RF coils and antennas — Q of an inductor at GHz = ω·L / R. R_AC kills Q. Silver-plate the wire for the last 2–5 µm and you skin-effect into the lower-resistivity silver.
  • PCB power planes — for 50 MHz return currents, only the top few µm of copper conducts. A 2 oz copper plane isn't 4× better than 1 oz for HF return; it's marginally better.
  • Bus bars in motor drives — 50–100 Hz at high amperes still has noticeable skin effect. Bus bars are typically flat (high surface-to-volume ratio) to mitigate.
  • Transmission-line conductors — Z₀ at HF depends only on geometry, not the wire's R. But R_AC adds attenuation.

The formula derivation

Maxwell's equations in a good conductor: ∇²E = jωµσE Wave solution: E(z) = E₀ · exp(−z/δ) · exp(−jz/δ) Skin depth: δ = √(2 / (ωµσ)) = √(2ρ / (ωµ)) Where ρ = 1/σ is resistivity, ω = 2πf. For copper: ρ = 1.68e-8, µ = µ₀ = 1.257e-6 → δ = 0.0660/√f meters δ = 8.5 mm @ 60 Hz, 65 µm @ 1 MHz, 2 µm @ 1 GHz

Effective resistance

For a solid round wire of radius r, when r ≫ δ, the current flows in an annulus of thickness δ along the outer circumference. Effective area A_eff = 2π·r·δ (instead of π·r²).

R_AC / R_DC ≈ r / (2·δ) for solid wire, large r Example: 1 mm radius copper at 1 MHz (δ = 65 µm): R_AC/R_DC = 1000/(2·65) = 7.7× Solution: use stranded Litz wire with strand radius < δ. N strands needed ≈ (r/δ)² to fully utilize copper. Each strand must be individually insulated and interleaved (the entire bundle gets twisted so no single strand stays on the surface).

Worked example — 100 kHz SMPS transformer winding

The setup

Half-bridge converter at 100 kHz switching. Primary winding carries 8 A RMS with sinusoidal current at the switching fundamental. Wire selection?

Skin depth math

δ at 100 kHz, copper: δ = 0.0660/√1e5 = 0.209 mm = 209 µm
Solid AWG 14 (1.628 mm dia, r = 0.814 mm): R_AC/R_DC = 0.814/(2·0.209) = 1.95×
Wasted Cu: of the wire's cross-section, only the outer ring carries current → I²R loss nearly doubles vs DC
Litz wire: 40 strands of AWG 32 (0.20 mm dia, r ≈ 0.1 mm) — each strand sees δ_strand > r_strand → R_AC/R_DC ≈ 1.0× ✓
Loss reduction: 8 A² × R × (1.95 − 1.0) = ~50% drop in conduction loss for same DC cross-section

Decision rule

If r_wire / δ < 1: stay solid (small AC penalty). If r_wire / δ > 2: use Litz. Between 1 and 2: marginal — depends on cost / heating budget.

Proximity effect — Litz isn't always enough

Adjacent current-carrying conductors create magnetic fields that push current in their neighbors. In a tightly-wound transformer, this proximity effect compounds skin effect — even Litz wire can suffer if the strands aren't properly interleaved.

  • Litz construction: strands twisted with a specific pitch so that each strand spends equal time in each position of the bundle's cross-section. Without this, the outer strands carry more current.
  • Foil windings: for low-turn, high-current windings (transformer secondaries), use thin copper foil instead of round wire. Foil thickness ≤ 2δ uses all the copper.
  • PCB transformer windings: stacked spiral traces on multilayer PCBs are essentially foil. Common in planar transformers for high-frequency, low-profile designs.
  • Proximity-effect tools: Dowell's equation gives R_AC/R_DC for layered windings. Most magnetics design tools (PI Magnetics Designer, Ridley) compute this automatically.

Common pitfalls

  • Picking wire too thick — at high frequency, extra copper outside δ is dead weight (and may actually increase loss via proximity effect).
  • Forgetting harmonics — square-wave switching current has harmonics at 3f, 5f, 7f, … each with smaller δ. A 100 kHz fundamental with strong 3rd harmonic at 300 kHz has δ = 120 µm (half the fundamental's).
  • Litz wire termination — must connect all strands to the terminal. A single solder dip won't catch insulated strands. Use proper tin-fluxed dip, ultrasonic welder, or crimped lugs.
  • Wire vs foil tradeoff — foil is better for HF but harder to wind by hand and harder to insulate. Use foil for production transformers, Litz for prototypes / one-offs.
  • Silver-plating depth — plating a copper wire with silver only matters if the plating is thicker than δ at your operating frequency. At 1 GHz, ~5 µm Ag is needed; thinner plating is cosmetic.
