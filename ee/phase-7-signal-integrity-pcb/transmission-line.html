Transmission Lines and Reflections

When trace length matters compared to signal wavelength, voltage and current are no longer uniform along a wire. Impedance discontinuities reflect waves. Γ = (Z_L − Z0)/(Z_L + Z0) tells you how much, and with what sign.

At low frequency a trace is a wire — same V everywhere. At high frequency (or fast edges) the trace is a transmission line, and signals propagate as waves down it. Hit an impedance mismatch at the load and energy bounces back. Watch the wave below.

Critical length — when is a trace a transmission line?

Rule of thumb: treat as TL when trace length ≥ λ / 10 for sinewaves, or for digital edges when 2·TD ≥ trise / 5 (round-trip slower than 20 % of rise time). In FR4 microstrip the velocity factor is ≈ 0.5c (εr,eff ≈ 3), so propagation delay ≈ 6 ns/m = 0.15 ns/cm. For a 1 ns rise time, the edge-rule gives critical length ≈ trise·v / 10 ≈ 1.5 cm — past that, you must care about Z0 and termination. (Stripline is slower, ~7 ns/m, because the dielectric fully surrounds the trace.)

What to notice

  • Matched termination = no reflection. The wave reaches the load, all its energy is absorbed, the source sees no return — the cleanest signal.
  • Open at the load doubles the voltage at the load endpoint momentarily — incident + reflected of same polarity. This is what causes overshoot and ringing on un-terminated digital edges.
  • Short inverts the reflection — V at the load is forced to zero (boundary condition), the wave bounces back with sign flipped. Repeated reflections eventually settle to zero across the line.
  • Round-trip delay = 2·T_D — sets the period of the visible ringing. In FR4 a 10 cm trace has T_D ≈ 600 ps; ringing period ≈ 1.2 ns.
  • The cure depends on direction of the signal — series termination at the driver kills the round-trip; parallel termination at the load kills the first reflection. Both work; series is more common for digital because it's free of DC current.
