RS-485 Differential Signaling

Two wires carrying opposite signals. Common-mode noise hits both; the receiver subtracts them; the noise cancels and the signal survives. That's the entire trick.

RS-485 is what you use when UART has to cross 100 meters of factory floor with motor noise, ground potential differences, and reflections. The standard says nothing about protocol — it's purely electrical. Take a TTL UART, run it through a differential transceiver (MAX485 et al.), terminate the bus correctly, and you've solved the long-distance noisy-link problem.

Noise (electromagnetic interference, ground bounce, capacitive pickup) hits A and B equally because the wires run as a twisted pair. When the receiver computes A − B, the common noise cancels. Only the intentional A vs B difference (driven by the transceiver) survives. Spec: |V_A − V_B| ≥ 200 mV = valid data. TIA-485 common-mode range is −7 V to +12 V; rugged transceivers extend that to ±25 V or more for noisy cross-building runs.

Bus topology and termination

Half-duplex direction control (DE/RE)

2-wire RS-485 is half-duplex: only one node drives at a time. Each transceiver has two control pins (often tied together): DE (driver enable) and R̄E (receiver enable, active LOW). Pull both HIGH to transmit, both LOW to listen. Software must coordinate "I'm done, line is yours" — usually with a higher-level protocol like Modbus or a master-poll discipline.

What to notice

  • Noise hits A and B identically — that's the "common-mode" assumption: equal magnetic flux through both wires of the twisted pair, equal capacitive coupling. The subtraction at the receiver cancels what hit both.
  • 200 mV is the minimum — anything less might be misinterpreted. Drivers typically produce 1.5–2 V differential into a terminated line; that gives roughly 7× margin over the threshold.
  • −7 V to +12 V common-mode range (TIA-485 spec) — asymmetric, not ±7. Ground potentials between buildings can differ by volts; RS-485 doesn't care. Modern "rugged" transceivers extend the window to ±25 V (e.g., MAX3430) or higher.
  • Terminate at both ends, not the middle — 120Ω at each end absorbs the wavefront so it doesn't reflect. One termination, or termination at the middle, still produces reflections at the far end.
  • DE/R̄E means firmware must release the line — after transmitting the last stop bit, drop DE. Forgetting to drop it = bus collision when the other side tries to reply. Some protocols add a guard time.

Cheat sheet

MEMORIZE

  • Differential A − B — ±200 mV minimum valid.
  • Common-mode — TIA-485: −7 to +12 V; rugged parts ±25 V or more.
  • Up to 32 unit loads, 1.2 km @ 100 kbps; trade speed for distance.
  • 120 Ω termination at BOTH ends (not middle).

WIRING DECISION

  • Half-duplex 2-wire — share A/B; DE/RE flips direction per node.
  • Full-duplex 4-wire — separate TX-pair and RX-pair (RS-422 style).
  • Twisted pair only — common-mode rejection assumes equal coupling.
  • Bias resistors if no driver is active (defines idle state).

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Missing termination — reflections corrupt high baud rates.
  • DE/RE polarity confusion — RE is active-LOW, DE active-HIGH; tie them OR drive separately per chip.
  • Forgetting to drop DE — bus collision when other side replies.
  • No common ground — common-mode range still needs a return path.
