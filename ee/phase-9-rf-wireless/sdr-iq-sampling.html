SDR I/Q Sampling — the complex baseband trick

Software-defined radios receive RF signals by quadrature down-conversion: multiply the antenna signal by cos(ω_LO·t) for the I (in-phase) component and sin(ω_LO·t) for the Q (quadrature) component. The two-channel result represents complex baseband — I + jQ — which preserves both positive AND negative frequencies relative to the LO. Without quadrature, you'd alias positive and negative frequencies onto each other and lose half the information.

The quadrature mixer

RF signal: s(t) = cos(2π·(f_LO + f_off)·t) Multiply by 2·cos(2π·f_LO·t): output = cos(2π·f_off·t) + cos(2π·(2f_LO + f_off)·t) Multiply by −2·sin(2π·f_LO·t): output = sin(2π·f_off·t) + (high freq) Low-pass filter both, sample → I(n), Q(n) at fs. Recovered complex baseband: I(n) + j·Q(n) = exp(j·2π·f_off·n/fs) The angle of the complex sample is the phase; rotation rate gives the frequency offset from LO.

Why I/Q matters: distinguishing +f_off from −f_off

With only I samples (real sampling), a signal at +200 kHz from LO and one at −200 kHz from LO would alias on top of each other — same |cosine|. You couldn't tell them apart.

With I and Q (quadrature) samples, the +200 kHz signal rotates in one direction in the complex plane; the −200 kHz signal rotates in the opposite direction. Subtracting Q from a hypothetical "Q'" derived from the +signal pattern shows whether the rotation is clockwise or counter-clockwise. Two adjacent samples on a complex plane = clear positive vs negative frequency.

Spectrum visualization

The complex spectrum from I/Q samples is two-sided: from −fs/2 to +fs/2. The image-rejection block in the SDR keeps the negative-frequency portion clean (or maps it intentionally to look at both sides of LO). With a real-only ADC, the spectrum is mirror-symmetric and you only see |F|.

Image rejection

Real-world I/Q paths have mismatch: I and Q gains aren't quite equal; the LO phase between I and Q isn't exactly 90°. This creates an "image" — a ghost of a signal at the mirror frequency. Typical raw image rejection is 30–40 dB; with calibration tables it can hit 60–80 dB.

  • Direct-conversion SDR (zero-IF): both I and Q paths see DC offsets + 1/f noise — appears as a "hump" at zero frequency.
  • Low-IF SDR: shift the LO slightly so the band of interest isn't centered on zero — avoids 1/f hump but uses more bandwidth.
  • Image-rejection mixer (Weaver / Hartley): cancels image with two phase-shifted mixers + adder.
