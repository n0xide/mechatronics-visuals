WiFi Channels & OFDM Subcarriers

802.11's allocation of the 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, and 6 GHz bands, plus the OFDM scheme that breaks each channel into ~64–2048 narrow subcarriers carrying parallel modulated data. Why are 2.4 GHz channels 1/6/11 always recommended? Why does WiFi 6 get faster than WiFi 5 even at the same SNR? OFDM is the answer.

2.4 GHz — the 1/6/11 overlap rule

2.4 GHz has 11 channels in the US (14 in Japan), spaced 5 MHz apart. Each channel's spectral mask is ~22 MHz wide (802.11b/g/n legacy footprint), with OFDM data occupying the central 20 MHz. Channels 1, 6, 11 are spaced 25 MHz apart — just enough that adjacent-channel emission falls below the receiver's tolerance.

5 GHz — many more, but with DFS

5 GHz has 25+ non-overlapping 20 MHz channels in the U-NII bands. The U-NII-2 sub-bands require DFS (dynamic frequency selection) — must detect and avoid radar signals. WiFi 6 and 6E bond channels into 40, 80, or 160 MHz blocks.

OFDM subcarriers — parallel streams of data

OFDM divides a 20 MHz channel into many narrow subcarriers, each carrying a slowly-modulated stream. Three things make this work:

  • Orthogonality — subcarrier spacing equals the symbol rate, so each subcarrier's null aligns with the peak of its neighbors. No inter-carrier interference.
  • Frequency-selective fading handling — a deep notch (multipath null) kills only the subcarriers in that frequency, not the whole channel. Forward error correction recovers.
  • OFDMA (WiFi 6+) — instead of giving each device the full 20 MHz briefly, assign subsets of subcarriers to multiple devices simultaneously. Lower per-device throughput but lower latency and better with many devices.

Worked example — WiFi 6 PHY rate

Configuration

802.11ax (WiFi 6) on a 80 MHz channel with 4-stream MIMO, 1024-QAM modulation, 5/6 coding rate, short guard interval.

Calculation

Subcarriers in 80 MHz: 1024 (256 per 20 MHz × 4) Data subcarriers: 980 (rest are pilots/null) Bits per symbol per subcarrier: 10 (1024-QAM = 2^10 states) Bits per OFDM symbol: 980 × 10 = 9800 bits raw Coding rate 5/6: useful bits = 9800 × 5/6 = 8167 Symbol time + guard: 13.6 µs (ax with 0.8 µs GI) Per-stream rate: 8167 / 13.6e-6 = 600 Mbps 4× MIMO: 4 × 600 Mbps = 2.4 Gbps PHY rate After overhead (MAC, contention): ~1.8 Gbps useful throughput

Pitfalls + reality checks

  • 2.4 GHz is the IoT junk band — Bluetooth, BLE, ZigBee, microwave ovens, baby monitors, garage openers. Use 5/6 GHz for serious traffic.
  • 5 GHz DFS channels can lose connection when radar is detected — base station drops to a different channel mid-stream. Avoid DFS in low-latency apps.
  • Width vs range tradeoff — 160 MHz channel = 2× speed but 6 dB lower spectral density → 4× weaker signal at a given distance. Wider is faster but closer-range.
  • MIMO needs spatial diversity — 4×4 MIMO requires 4 antennas with sufficient λ/2 spacing (~6 cm at 2.4 GHz, 3 cm at 5 GHz). Small devices can't fit 4×4.
  • OFDMA latency win comes with throughput loss per device — useful for many sparse devices (IoT) but worse than full-channel allocation for one heavy user.
  • WiFi 7 features — 320 MHz channels (6 GHz only), 4096-QAM, multi-link operation. Marginal gain in throughput, real gain in latency.

Worked example — distance and rate tradeoff

The setup

Apartment WiFi 6 AP at 5 GHz, 20 dBm TX power, +5 dBi antenna gain. Phone at 5 dBi RX gain. Free-space path loss formula: PL(dB) = 20·log(4π·d·f/c). What's the max range for each MCS?

Link budget

TX power + TX gain: 20 + 5 = 25 dBm EIRP
Thermal noise (20 MHz BW, NF=5 dB): −174 + 73 + 5 = −96 dBm
RX sensitivity for MCS 11 (1024-QAM 5/6): noise + min_SNR = −96 + 34 = −62 dBm
Max path loss for MCS 11: 25 − (−62) + 5 (RX gain) = 92 dB
PL = 92 dB at 5 GHz → d = c/(4π·f) · 10^(PL/20) = 0.0096 × 10^4.6 = ~4 m line-of-sight
MCS 7 (64-QAM 5/6, 25 dB SNR): −96 + 25 = −71 dBm → range ~15 m
MCS 0 (BPSK 1/2, 5 dB SNR): −96 + 5 = −91 dBm → range ~120 m line-of-sight

Reality

Indoor: walls add 5-15 dB per wall. 2.4 GHz penetrates better than 5 GHz; 5 GHz better than 6 GHz. A modern dual-band AP often ends up using MCS 9 at the device's location, not the spec-sheet 1024-QAM.

Cheat sheet

KEY NUMBERS

  • 2.4 GHz: 14 channels, 20 MHz wide, 5 MHz spacing
  • 5 GHz: ~25 non-overlapping 20 MHz channels (U-NII 1/2/3)
  • OFDM symbol time: 3.6 µs (legacy), 12.8 µs (ax/be longer)
  • Subcarrier spacing: 312.5 kHz (n/ac), 78.125 kHz (ax/be)
  • 1024-QAM: 10 bits/symbol
  • 4096-QAM (WiFi 7): 12 bits/symbol

STANDARDS BY YEAR

  • n (2009): 40 MHz, 64 sub, MIMO 4x4, 600 Mbps
  • ac (2013): 160 MHz, 256-QAM, 1.7 Gbps
  • ax / WiFi 6 (2019): OFDMA, 1024-QAM, 2.4 Gbps
  • ax-E / 6E (2020): + 6 GHz band
  • be / WiFi 7 (2024): 320 MHz, 4096-QAM, MLO, 9.6 Gbps

PITFALLS

  • 2.4 GHz is congested. Move serious traffic to 5+ GHz.
  • Wider channel ≠ always better — fewer channels available, more interference.
  • 1024-QAM needs ~31 dB SNR — only close-range.
  • DFS channels = unpredictable disconnects.
  • Mesh and 6 GHz — only 6E+ devices can join 6 GHz, so mixed networks fall back.
