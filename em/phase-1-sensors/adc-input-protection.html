ADC Input Protection

An ADC input is a tiny capacitor (a few pF) connected to a high-impedance node inside the chip — usually with internal ESD clamp diodes good for a milliamp or two. A real-world signal source can hit it with anything: 12 V transients, kV ESD, kHz noise. Three building blocks — Schottky clamp diodes, a series resistor, and an RC anti-alias filter — turn that raw input into something the MCU survives.

The three threats and three answers

  • Over-voltage — the signal goes above V_DD + 0.3 V or below GND − 0.3 V. Answer: clamp diodes (Schottky for fast, low V_F).
  • Surge current — even with clamps in place, the spike has to be limited. Answer: a series resistor (typically 1–10 kΩ).
  • High-frequency noise above Nyquist — aliasing folds the noise into your signal band. Answer: an RC low-pass filter at f_c ≤ f_sample/2.

Clamp diodes

The MCU's input pin has internal ESD diodes from pin → V_DD and from GND → pin. Both are forward-biased only when the pin tries to leave the supply rails. Their I-V characteristic is the usual silicon diode: ~0.7 V drop, exponential below, hard above.

Internal clamps are weak — they're sized for ESD events (a few amps for nanoseconds), not sustained DC. The datasheet usually rates the absolute max input current at ±10 mA. Even 10 mA into the V_DD rail of an unloaded board can lift V_DD until something else clamps it; "phantom powering" the chip through its own input pin is a real failure mode.

External Schottky diodes solve this by clamping before the internal diodes turn on. They have a lower V_F (typically 0.25–0.35 V at 1 mA vs. silicon's 0.65 V), so the input rail never reaches the internal diode's turn-on threshold. The external Schottkys can sink amps without complaint.

Without protection: V_signal = 12 V → internal clamp turns on at V_DD + 0.7 ≈ 4.0 V → fault current into V_DD pin = (12 − 4.0) / R_source If R_source is tiny (50 Ω), fault I = 8/50 = 160 mA → chip destroyed. With R_series = 10 kΩ between signal and pin: fault I = 8/10k = 0.8 mA → safe. With external BAT54 Schottky to V_DD: V_pin clamps at V_DD + 0.35 V; chip never sees a fault current at all.

Series resistor — the current limit

Pick R_series large enough that the worst-case fault current (signal at maximum possible voltage, clamped down to V_DD+0.35) is below the chip's input limit.

R_series ≥ (V_signal_max − V_DD) / I_clamp_max Example: 24 V max signal, 3.3 V supply, 10 mA limit → R_series ≥ 20.7/10mA = 2.07 kΩ → round up to 4.7 kΩ. For "transient only" (ESD, lightning-induced): use a TVS diode in front, R_series can be smaller (100–1000 Ω).

But there's a trade-off: bigger R_series means slower charge of the ADC's sample-and-hold capacitor. Which is the next constraint.

Source impedance limit

The ADC sample-and-hold takes a snapshot at the start of each conversion. During the acquisition window (typically 0.5–5 µs), the S/H cap (1–15 pF inside most MCUs, plus the external C_filter) must charge through your source impedance. If acquisition time is too short, you sample a partial charge — yielding low readings.

Most MCU vendors publish a recommended source impedance:

If your divider's Thévenin resistance (R1‖R2) exceeds the limit, options:

  • Add a buffer op-amp in unity-gain (voltage follower) — input Z = ∞, output Z ≈ 0.
  • Add a sample-cap (100–1000 pF) directly at the ADC pin — pre-charges before sampling, lower S/H ripple.
  • Increase acquisition time — most STM32 ADCs have ADC_SMPR registers; pick a slower channel for high-Z sources.
  • Lower divider impedance — drop from 100 kΩ pair to 10 kΩ pair if standby current allows.

RC anti-alias filter

If your signal has frequency content above f_sample/2 (Nyquist), it folds into the baseband — appearing as low-frequency noise in your reading. The cure is a low-pass filter at or below Nyquist:

f_c = 1 / (2π · R · C) For f_sample = 100 kSps → f_Nyquist = 50 kHz → choose f_c ≤ 25 kHz for safe rolloff at the fold. R = 1 kΩ, C = 10 nF gives f_c = 15.9 kHz — fine. Going to higher orders (Sallen-Key 2nd-order, or anti-alias active filters) gets you a sharper cutoff, but for resistive sensors a single RC is enough.

Note that R here serves double duty: current limit AND filter pole. That's elegant — but it also means choosing R involves balancing both constraints. The settling-time check at the bottom of the table flags when you've made R too big for the configured acquisition window.

Worked example — battery monitor with protection

The scenario

Pico 2 W (RP2350) monitoring a 4S Li-ion pack (16.8 V max). ADC has 3.3 V reference, ≤ 1 kΩ source impedance for 12-bit linearity. Worst-case fault: a slip with a probe shorts the input to the +14 V battery rail.

Step 1 — divider

Ratio needed: 16.8 / 3.3 ≈ 5.09 → divider 4.7 kΩ / 1.0 kΩ (5.7:1, slightly conservative) (R_th = 825 Ω, OK for ≤ 1 kΩ)

Step 2 — clamp + series

Add a 1 kΩ in series after the divider tap, plus a BAT54S dual-Schottky (clamp to V_DD and GND). Fault scenario: tap shoots to 14 V before divider does anything → 1 kΩ limits I_clamp = (14 − 3.65) / 1000 = 10.4 mA — at the limit, but with Schottky helping, the internal diode never turns on. Safer: bump R_series to 4.7 kΩ → I_clamp = 2.2 mA, comfortably safe.

Step 3 — anti-alias

Battery voltage varies on the millisecond timescale (PWM-driven motor draws). Sample at 1 kSps in firmware. Choose f_c around 100 Hz for 10× margin.

R · C = 1 / (2π · 100) = 1.59 ms → with R = 4.7 kΩ, C = 1.59 ms / 4.7 kΩ = 340 nF → use 470 nF X7R. Settling check: 5τ = 5 · 4.7 kΩ · 470 nF = 11 ms — but we're sampling at 1 ms intervals. Sampling rate must drop to 100 Hz, or this voltage isn't going to be a fast measurement. For a battery voltage that doesn't change fast anyway, a slower sample rate is fine. Adjust f_sample to match the RC, not the other way around.

Step 4 — BOM

R1 = 4.7 kΩ (1 %), R2 = 1.0 kΩ (1 %), R_series = 4.7 kΩ, C = 470 nF X7R, D1 = BAT54S. Total per channel: ~$0.20. Adds 50 mil² of board area. Eliminates a class of "I plugged in the wrong thing" failures.

What to notice

  • R_series does triple duty — current limit on fault, source-Z for ADC, and anti-alias pole. Pick it as the larger of those three constraints.
  • Don't trust the internal clamps for sustained over-voltage. They're for ESD only. External Schottky + R_series is cheap insurance.
  • Schottky V_F is the headline spec. BAT54: 0.32 V at 1 mA. BAS40: 0.38 V. PMEG: 0.25 V. Lower is better — keeps the pin closer to V_DD during a fault, less stress on the internal diode.
  • Anti-alias before sample. There's no software trick that recovers signal aliased into your band. The filter must be analog, ahead of the ADC.
  • The "source impedance" spec is real. Some MCUs (RP2350 at full 500 kSps) are surprisingly picky — a 10 kΩ divider drops the LSB into noise. Either buffer or drop the rate.

Where you'll see this

Real uses

  • Battery monitor inputs — always behind a divider plus clamp diode.
  • External sensor jacks on dev boards — users plug in unknown voltages; must survive.
  • Motor current shunts — high-side resistor measured across with a differential amp; protection on both inputs.
  • Industrial 0–24 V / 4–20 mA loops — burden resistor converts to voltage, divider scales to ADC, clamp + R_series protects.
  • User-input audio (line in, mic) — AC-coupled, bias to mid-rail, clamp + RC, into ADC.

RULES OF THUMB

  • R_series = 1 kΩ default for hobbyist 3.3 V/5 V projects.
  • Anti-alias C: 100 nF–1 µF X7R for slow signals.
  • Schottky clamp: BAT54S (dual, common cathode) is the default.
  • 5τ rule: leave 5 RC time-constants for settling.
  • 1 kΩ ≤ R_source for fast SAR; 10–50 kΩ for slow MCU ADCs; ∞ for ΔΣ.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • R_series too big → slow settling, ADC reads low. Match to acquisition time.
  • Skipping the clamp diode "because the signal is always 0–3 V" — until a probe slip puts 24 V on it.
  • Clamp into a powered-off rail — if V_DD is at 0 V and you clamp to it, your signal gets dragged to ground through the diode. Worth checking before assuming the clamp is "free."
  • RC pole too high — aliasing wrecks SNR with no error indication. Always verify.
  • Forgetting the divider's R_th counts as R_series — a 10 kΩ:10 kΩ divider already has 5 kΩ source impedance.
