Capacitive Touch — Self-Cap, Mutual-Cap, and Charge Shift

A finger near a copper pad changes its capacitance by a few picofarads. The whole industry of smartphone glass, appliance buttons, sliders, wheels, and proximity panels rests on detecting that change reliably in the presence of water, gloves, EMI, and a noisy 50/60 Hz mains world.

Two architectures dominate. Self-capacitance measures the capacitance of a single electrode to ground; a finger adds a few pF of body-to-earth coupling, registering as an increase. Mutual capacitance measures the small coupling capacitance between two electrodes (a transmit row and a receive column); a finger near the crossing point steals some of the field lines, registering as a decrease. Self-cap wins on sensitivity and proximity range; mutual-cap wins on multitouch, water rejection, and grid scaling. Modern phones and trackpads use mutual-cap exclusively. Single-button appliances, sliders, and wheels are mostly self-cap because the controller is cheap and the geometry is simple.

Self-capacitance — one electrode against ground

The electrode is charged from V_dd through a known resistor (or a current source pulse). The time it takes to reach a comparator threshold is proportional to C_pad. A finger adds C_finger in parallel because the user's body is a large grounded conductor (~150 pF to earth at audio frequencies, and at touch frequencies of 10–200 kHz the finger looks like an effective ground via the body's bulk capacitance).

The math (RC charge to threshold)

V(t) = V_dd · (1 − exp(−t/τ)), τ = R·C Solve for t when V = V_th: t_th = −τ · ln(1 − V_th/V_dd) Touch increases C by ΔC → t_th grows by approximately R·ΔC · ln(V_dd/(V_dd−V_th)) → Detection threshold: difference in counts of a high-speed timer captured at the comparator-toggle event.

Mutual capacitance — TX drives, RX integrates the lost charge

Two PCB tracks cross with a small overlap. The fringing field between them is a tiny capacitor C_M (~0.3–3 pF). Drive the TX with a square wave; on RX, an integrator (or charge amplifier) collects the charge q = C_M · V_TX every cycle. A finger near the crossing absorbs some of the field lines — both to ground and to the finger's body capacitance — so the receiver sees less charge. This is the inverse polarity of self-cap.

Why mutual-cap scales for multitouch: with N rows and M columns you only need N+M I/O pins to probe N·M intersections. Drive one row at a time, sample all columns simultaneously, frame rate = scan period × N. A 10×16 panel runs at ~100 Hz with a single 16-channel front end.

Charge-Shift Principle (CSP / charge-transfer)

Instead of timing an RC, CSP repeatedly dumps the sensor charge into a known reference capacitor C_s and counts how many transfers it takes to fill C_s to a threshold. This is the basis of the Atmel/Microchip QTouch family, the STM32 TSC (Touch Sensing Controller), and the ESP32 RTC capacitive-touch peripheral. The count is essentially a high-resolution measurement of C_pad/C_s, and it rejects DC bias and slow drift naturally.

Each cycle: charge C_pad to V_dd, then short to C_s (with C_s ≫ C_pad) After N cycles: V_s ≈ V_dd · (1 − (1 − C_pad/(C_pad+C_s))^N) Counter stops when V_s > V_th: N_th ≈ (C_s/C_pad) · ln(V_dd/(V_dd−V_th)) Finger increases C_pad → fewer transfers needed → N_th decreases

Why the count is so big: with C_s/C_pad = 1000 and V_th/V_dd = 0.5, you get N_th ≈ 693. A 1 pF finger drops it by ≈ 63 counts — easy to detect with a 16-bit timer and trivial filtering.

Slider, wheel, and proximity patterns

Geometry encodes position into a ratio of two or more channel readings. A slider uses two interlocking serpentine pads — finger position interpolates between them. A wheel uses three or four sectors and reports angle via centroid. Proximity uses a single very large electrode (or a guard-driven sense pad) for ranges up to ~10 cm.

The guard ring is the trick that makes proximity work at distance: a copper plane underneath and around the sense pad is actively driven to the same voltage as the sense pad. With no voltage difference between them, no field forms between them — all the field is forced upward, multiplying the sense range several-fold.

Water, gloves, EMI, and the rejection bag of tricks

Self-cap and mutual-cap fail in opposite ways under water. Water on self-cap looks like a giant finger — a continuous puddle increases C_pad uniformly, generating false touches everywhere. Water on mutual-cap increases C_M slightly (water has high permittivity) but a real finger still steals field lines, so the touch signature is opposite the water signature: you can subtract baseline and recover a finger underwater. This is why automotive infotainment, marine controls, and shower-friendly appliances always use mutual-cap.

Baseline tracking is the firmware survival kit: maintain a running average of "untouched" capacitance per channel with a slow time constant (seconds). Subtract it before applying the touch threshold. When the user is gone (no touches for N seconds), the baseline drifts toward the new untouched reading, accommodating temperature, humidity, dust, and slow water buildup.

The ESP32 capacitive-touch peripheral

The ESP32 family includes a hardware capacitive-touch sensor on up to 14 GPIO pins (ESP32-S2/S3 use a redesigned analog front end with better water tolerance). It implements a CSP-style charge-transfer measurement and reports a 16-bit count. The classic firmware idiom:

// ESP32 Arduino: read a touch channel
void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  // No pinMode needed; touchRead handles it
}

void loop() {
  int raw = touchRead(T0); // GPIO 4 on classic ESP32
  // raw is INVERSELY proportional to capacitance:
  // higher count = less capacitance (no touch)
  // lower count  = more capacitance (touch)
  Serial.println(raw);
  delay(50);
}

// ESP32-S2/S3 use the opposite polarity:
// higher count = touch (more capacitance)
// Always test the direction before setting a threshold!

For production, use touchAttachInterrupt(pin, isr, threshold) so the CPU stays asleep until the user touches; this drops idle current from ~25 mA to ~150 µA on the S3 in deep-sleep + touch wake.

Worked example — capacitive wake-up button for a battery node

The design

30 mm circular copper pad on 1.6 mm FR4, no overlay, ESP32-S3 GPIO 1. Target: detect finger contact at > 5σ over noise floor, draw < 200 µA average while waiting.

The numbers

C_pad (30 mm round, 1.6 mm FR4 to ground plane) ≈ ε₀·ε_r·A/d ≈ 8.854e-12 · 4.4 · π·(0.015)² / 0.0016 ≈ 17.5 pF Stray fringing + trace + IC: add ~7 pF → C₀ ≈ 25 pF Finger pressed: ΔC ≈ 2–4 pF (with no overlay, on bare copper) CSP count at C_s = 1 nF, V_th/V_dd = 0.5: N₀ ≈ 1000/25 · ln(2) ≈ 27.7 → 28 cycles N_touch ≈ 1000/29 · ln(2) ≈ 23.9 → 24 cycles Δ counts ≈ 4 → only 1 bit of separation; need to oversample Increase C_s to 100 nF → N₀ ≈ 2772, N_touch ≈ 2391, Δ ≈ 381 → easy detection

Power strategy

Run the touch peripheral in deep-sleep wake-on-touch mode. The peripheral takes ~100 µA average; the ESP32-S3 RTC consumes ~7 µA when only touch is alive. Total idle ≈ 110 µA — meets the 200 µA budget with margin. The CPU wakes only when the threshold is crossed, runs whatever app code, then sleeps again.

What to notice

  • Self-cap = increase, mutual-cap = decrease — sign of the signal flips. Get this wrong and your firmware will think touches are releases.
  • SNR drives everything — the actual capacitance change is so tiny (often < 1 pF) that the entire architecture is built around noise rejection: shielding, baseline tracking, oversampling, spread-spectrum, synchronous demodulation.
  • Overlay thickness directly trades sensitivity for protection — finger ΔC drops roughly as 1/d for thin overlays. 0.5 mm acrylic is easy; 3 mm tempered glass needs careful tuning; 6 mm is the upper end for normal touch (proximity-class designs go further).
  • Guards everywhere — driven-shield copper underneath every sense pad reduces parasitic-to-ground capacitance, which is the dominant noise source on long traces. Without guards, a 100 mm trace adds 10–20 pF of unhelpful C₀ that swamps your finger ΔC.
  • Frequency hopping defeats chargers — switch-mode chargers radiate 50–500 kHz tones that look like signal if you happen to scan at their fundamental. Production designs scan at 3–5 different frequencies and discard frames with anomalous noise.
  • Water and gloves are opposite problems — water needs less sensitivity (so it doesn't false-trigger), gloves need more. Adjustable-sensitivity firmware modes and dual-threshold designs handle both.

Where you'll see this circuit

Real uses

  • Smartphone touchscreens — mutual-cap grids, 50–200 Hz scan, 10-point multitouch, water rejection.
  • Appliance control panels — sliders for cooktops, wheels for ovens, single buttons for coffee makers. Self-cap dominates.
  • Automotive infotainment — mutual-cap exclusively for water and glove tolerance; ASIL-rated controllers for safety-critical inputs.
  • Wake-up buttons on battery devices — ESP32 touch peripheral, MSP430 CapTIvate, STM32 TSC, all in deep-sleep wake modes.
  • Proximity sensors — large-area pads in soap dispensers, gesture-aware appliances, robot collision avoidance.

SHORTCUTS

  • Finger to bare copper: ΔC ≈ 1–5 pF.
  • Through 1 mm overlay: ΔC drops to ~0.3–1 pF.
  • C₀ goal: keep below 50 pF (shorter traces, guard, minimize ground plane under pad).
  • SNR target: ≥ 5:1 (some designs accept 3:1 with strong filtering).
  • Scan rate: 100–200 Hz feels instant, 50 Hz feels sluggish, < 30 Hz misses fast taps.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Floating system — battery devices with no chassis ground reference report inconsistent ΔC. Add a "guard plane" or rely on body capacitance to earth via free-space coupling at 100+ kHz.
  • Long traces — every extra cm of trace adds ~1 pF of parasitic. Route touch traces direct and short, never along the edge of a noisy bus.
  • No baseline tracking — temperature, humidity, dust, all change C₀ over hours. Always implement a slow baseline filter.
  • Threshold tuning at the desk — your wooden desk is grounded poorly; your customer's metal countertop changes everything. Tune on the actual mechanical assembly, in production conditions.
  • EMI from nearby radios — BLE/Wi-Fi/LTE PA bursts couple into touch traces. Add ferrite beads or RC filters at the controller pin.
