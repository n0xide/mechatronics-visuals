Encoders — Counting, Absolute, Magnetic

Three families: incremental (count edges, fast and cheap), absolute (read position directly, survives power-off), magnetic (no contact, no wear). Pick by whether you need to know where you are at startup.

An encoder converts mechanical angle (or linear position) into a signal a microcontroller can count or read. Incremental encoders are deceptively simple — two square-wave channels A and B, 90° out of phase, and you count edges. Quadrature decoding lets you detect direction and multiply resolution by four. The catch: at power-up you don't know where the shaft is. Absolute encoders solve that by encoding every position with a unique pattern (parallel Gray code or serial readout), so the very first sample tells you exact position. Magnetic encoders (AS5048, MT6701) replace optical discs with a Hall sensor array reading a diametrically-magnetized rotor magnet — 14-bit resolution, no contact, no wear, ideal for compact robot joints.

Every valid quadrature transition flips exactly one of A or B. A simultaneous flip of both means you sampled too slow — you missed a step. The state machine catches this as an error.

Gray-code disk (multi-ring)

Binary code would flip multiple bits between adjacent positions (e.g., 0111 → 1000); momentary mid-transition reads could give wildly wrong values. Gray code guarantees only one bit changes per step, so any sample is either current or previous position — never garbage.

What to notice

  • Incremental quadrature gives 4× CPR — a 100 CPR optical disc gives 400 counts per rev when you count both edges on both channels. Manufacturers sometimes spec PPR (pulses per rev = CPR), sometimes lines per rev. Always check.
  • Direction = which channel leads — A-leads-B is forward, B-leads-A is reverse. The transition table is the decoder.
  • Index pulse Z is the home reference — fires once per revolution; use it on startup to find absolute zero with an incremental encoder.
  • Absolute encoders survive power loss — your robot wakes up and instantly knows the joint angle. No homing routine needed, which matters when the arm can't safely traverse its own range.
  • Gray code's only-one-bit-changes invariant — eliminates the "between two codes" garbage that plain binary suffers from. Same idea as graycoding in ADCs and 7-bit address decoders.
  • Magnetic encoders need a magnet that matches — diametrically magnetized, 6 mm Ø typical, mounted concentric with the shaft. Off-axis >0.5 mm degrades accuracy quickly. Air gap typically 0.5–2 mm.
  • SPI burst read — AS5048A returns the angle in two bytes (14 bits + parity + EF flag) over a single SPI transaction. ~1 µs read at 10 MHz.

MEMORIZE

  • ×4 quadrature — both edges of both channels.
  • A leads B = forward.
  • Gray = 1 bit per step — never two.
  • AS5048 = 14-bit, 0.022° — SPI/PWM/ABI selectable.
  • Diametric magnet — N/S split across the diameter, not axially.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Software polling at high RPM — misses edges. Use hardware quadrature decoder (PIO on RP2350, TIM-encoder on STM32).
  • CPR vs PPR confusion — some specs are pulses (edges/4), some counts.
  • No index → no abs home — incremental needs a home switch or Z pulse on power-up.
  • Off-axis magnet mount — sin/cos demod assumes the magnet is centered; off-axis ≫ 0.5 mm injects 2nd-harmonic error.
  • Magnet too far / too weak — AS5048 OCF/COF flags warn you; respect them.
