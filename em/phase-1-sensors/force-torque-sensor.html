Force/Torque Sensor — Strain Bridges and the 6-Axis Wrist

A robot wrist needs to know what its end-effector is pushing against. A 6-axis F/T sensor reports F_x, F_y, F_z, M_x, M_y, M_z by measuring the strain in a precisely-machined elastic element with up to eight Wheatstone bridges and decoupling the cross-talk through a calibration matrix.

Force/torque sensors live between the robot flange and the tool. They measure how hard the tool is pushing on the world in three orthogonal directions (forces F_x/F_y/F_z, in newtons) and how hard it's twisting around each of those axes (moments M_x/M_y/M_z, in newton-meters). All six channels are needed for compliant assembly (peg-in-hole, electronics insertion), polishing, screwdriving with torque feedback, ultrasonic welding, surgical robots, and tactile inspection. The mechanical heart is an elastic element — usually a cross-shaped or Maltese-cross steel/aluminum monolith with thin spokes — instrumented with strain gauges wired into Wheatstone bridges. Each bridge outputs a few millivolts; an INA-style instrumentation amplifier brings that to volts, a 24-bit Σ-Δ ADC quantizes it, and a 6×8 decoupling matrix turns raw bridge readings into the six F/T values.

Strain → ΔR → mV

A strain gauge is a thin conductor whose resistance changes when it stretches. The gauge factor (GF) is the proportionality constant: ΔR/R = GF · ε, where ε is strain (ΔL/L, dimensionless). Foil gauges (the workhorse) use a serpentine grid of constantan or karma alloy, GF ≈ 2.0–2.1, very temperature-stable. Semiconductor (piezoresistive) gauges use doped silicon, GF ≈ 100–200, much more sensitive but temperature-dependent and fragile. Used in MEMS pressure sensors and high-end load cells where the extra gain matters.

The gauge resistance changes by a microscopic amount. Even at 1000 microstrain (1 mε, a very heavy load), a 350 Ω foil gauge changes by ΔR = 2 · 0.001 · 350 = 0.7 Ω. With a 5 V excitation in a single-arm bridge, you get V_out ≈ 5/4 · 0.7/350 = 2.5 mV. That tiny signal is exactly why we need instrumentation amplifiers and full-bridge topology: to boost the signal, reject common-mode, and cancel temperature drift.

Full bridge (4 active gauges) → INA → 24-bit ADC

The classic load-cell circuit. Four gauges, two stretching (R + ΔR), two compressing (R − ΔR), all the same nominal R. Wired as a Wheatstone bridge with V_exc across one diagonal and V_out across the other, the differential output is exactly ε · GF · V_exc — four times more sensitive than a single-gauge arrangement, and inherently temperature-compensated because all four gauges drift together.

The bridge output is a tiny differential voltage on top of a midrail common-mode (V_exc/2). The INA's job is to reject that common-mode (typically 90+ dB CMRR) and amplify the differential. A single external gain resistor R_G sets the gain. Output goes into a 24-bit Σ-Δ ADC (typically TI ADS124xS or AD7124) which gives sub-microvolt resolution — needed because the bridge output at 1/1000 FS is below the noise floor of a 12-bit SAR ADC.

The signal-chain math

V_bridge = V_exc · GF · ε (full bridge with 4 active gauges) V_amp = G · V_bridge (instrumentation amp) N_adc = V_amp / V_ref · (2^bits − 1) Strain per LSB = V_ref / (V_exc · GF · G · (2^bits − 1)) → typical chain: V_ref=2.5V, V_exc=5V, GF=2.0, G=128, 24-bit → 2.5 / (5·2·128·16 777 215) = 1.16×10⁻¹⁰ ε per LSB (massively oversampled — noise sets the real limit at ~10⁻⁷ ε)

The Maltese cross — six axes from eight bridges

A 6-axis sensor's body is a monolithic spider with 4 spokes (Maltese cross) machined from one piece of high-strength aluminum or steel. Eight strain bridges (two per spoke, top and bottom or radial pairs) capture the bending and shear of each spoke. Different combinations of bridge readings extract different force/torque components:

The 6×8 decoupling matrix

Because no machining is perfect and no spoke is purely loaded in one direction, raw bridge readings are mixed — pushing on F_x produces a small reading on the F_y and M_z bridges. The fix is a calibration matrix [C] computed at the factory by applying known loads and least-squares fitting. The runtime pipeline is:

b = [b₁, b₂, b₃, b₄, b₅, b₆, b₇, b₈]ᵀ (8 bridge readings, mV) b' = b − b_offset (subtract baseline; weight tare) F = [F_x, F_y, F_z, M_x, M_y, M_z]ᵀ = C · b' (C is 6×8) → Six dot products: one matrix-vector multiply per sample, ~150 flops at the rate of the ADC (1–10 kHz).

The matrix is stored in non-volatile memory on the sensor (or in the host driver), and a typical row looks like:

F_x: +0.452+0.018−0.005+0.011−0.448−0.022 −0.002−0.009+0.004−0.014+0.001+0.007

Diagonal entries dominate; off-diagonals (cross-coupling) are typically < 5% of the diagonal. After calibration, residual cross-talk is < 1% in good designs, < 2% even in cheap ones. The matrix also encodes the calibration constants (mV/N, mV/N·m), so the F output is in newtons and the M output is in newton-meters directly — no further scaling.

The market splits into two tiers. Industrial/surgical sensors (ATI, Bota, Schunk) cost $4k–$15k, deliver 1/4000 of FS, 1+ kHz BW, EtherCAT or CAN-FD. Cobot-tier sensors (OnRobot, Robotiq) trade resolution and bandwidth for plug-and-play integration with UR/Doosan/Techman arms and Python SDKs. Open-source / DIY (Bota's research-grade EthZH spinoff, university labs) typically use 8× Wheatstone bridges with HX711 ADCs or AD7124 modules, total BOM under $200, ~2% accuracy.

Force-controlled task examples

  • Peg-in-hole (assembly): apply 5 N along Z, sense lateral forces on X/Y, command corrective velocity in proportion to those forces. Search spiral until contact reduces below threshold.
  • Polishing: hold F_z at constant 10 N along the surface normal, regardless of surface curvature. Path-follow in tangential plane.
  • Screwdriving: command axial force F_z to keep bit engaged, monitor M_z torque, stop on torque threshold (digital torque wrench).
  • Teach-by-demonstration: human grabs the end-effector, applies forces, robot moves in compliance. Joint torques and F/T sensor cooperate.
  • Surgical retraction: hold tissue at controlled force, avoid damage. Bandwidth and resolution matter most.

Worked example — UR5 cobot with ATI Mini45 polishing aluminum

Task

Polish a curved aluminum surface, holding a constant 12 N pressure normal to the surface, traveling at 50 mm/s, with the polishing pad oriented tangent to the path.

The control loop

Read F/T at 1 kHz: F = [F_x, F_y, F_z, M_x, M_y, M_z]ᵀ in tool frame Transform to surface normal direction (from current tool orientation): F_normal = (F · n̂) Force error: e_F = F_target − F_normal = 12 − F_normal (N) Apply admittance: v_command = K_admit · e_F (m/s along n̂) Typical K_admit ≈ 0.001 m/s/N → 1 mm/s for every 1 N of error Combine with tangential path velocity: v_total = v_tangent + v_command Send to UR5 servoj() at 125 Hz with ~8 ms cycle

Why a 6-axis is needed (not just a 1-axis load cell)

A 1-axis sensor would only measure force in one direction; as the surface curves, the contact normal rotates, but the sensor frame doesn't, so the 1D reading wanders. With a full 6-axis sensor you compute the normal force vector in any frame, you measure tangential drag force (useful to detect the pad sliding vs. sticking), and you measure the torque around the tool axis (catches the pad spinning unevenly or hitting a foreign object).

What to notice

  • Eight bridges, six outputs — over-determined (eight equations, six unknowns) lets the matrix average out noise and reject some manufacturing imperfections during calibration.
  • Cross-talk is the dominant non-ideality — even with a perfect cross machined from billet, axes are 5–20% cross-coupled before calibration. The matrix is what makes the sensor usable.
  • F_z (axial) is usually the highest-range channel — payload weight goes through F_z, so designers spec F_z 2–3× higher than F_x, F_y. Conversely, M_z (axial twist) is often the most sensitive because the lever arms are short and a small torque produces a big bridge response.
  • Temperature drift is the worst real-world enemy — full bridges cancel first-order R-drift, but mechanical thermal-expansion of the body itself shows up. Production sensors include a temperature sensor for software compensation.
  • Sampling at 1–10 kHz is mandatory for control loops — at 100 Hz, the loop is too slow to react to fast impacts (a peg striking the rim of a hole produces a 50 N spike in < 1 ms). High-end sensors run at 10 kHz internally with hardware filtering and CAN/EtherCAT delivery.
  • Tare often, especially after tool changes — the tool's own weight produces a baseline F_z (and M_x, M_y if off-axis). Subtract a known weight model OR re-tare after every grip change.

Where you'll see this circuit

Real uses

  • Robot wrists — every collaborative robot integrates one, every research arm has one.
  • Assembly and screwdriving — automotive line, electronics, appliance.
  • Polishing and deburring — constant-force surface following on castings, weldments.
  • Surgical robots — da Vinci, Mako (knee replacement), Versius — all need force feedback to avoid tissue damage.
  • Haptic devices — Phantom Omni, Force Dimension — bidirectional F/T for VR/teleoperation.
  • Wind tunnel models — sting balances measuring lift/drag/side-force and three moments on aircraft scale models.
  • Sports science — running gait force plates, golf swing analysis.

SHORTCUTS

  • Foil GF ≈ 2.0, semiconductor GF ≈ 100–200.
  • 350 Ω is the universal "default" gauge resistance.
  • Full bridge ≈ 4× the single-arm output AND temp-compensated.
  • Bridge sensitivity is typically 1–3 mV/V at full scale.
  • Use 24-bit Σ-Δ, not SAR. The bridge noise floor is way below 12-bit LSB.
  • UR5 wrist standard payload is 250 N normal, 10 N·m torque.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Skipping tare — tool weight + cable stiffness baseline ≠ zero. Always tare with the tool in its operating pose.
  • Ground loops — exciting the bridge from one supply and amplifying with another that has different ground reference. Use shielded twisted-quad cable and a single-point ground.
  • EMI from the robot servo drives — high-current PWM lines run in the same cable harness can inject hundreds of µV into the bridge. Separate cable runs, shielded sensor cable.
  • Overload damage — strain elements have a limited safe overload (typically 5–10× FS for shock, 2× for static). One peg-stuck-in-a-collision and your $4k sensor reads wrong forever.
  • Improper mounting — bolting the sensor to a non-flat surface adds preload, shifts the offset, and reduces accuracy. Mount it on a precision-machined adapter plate.
  • Treating output as instantaneous — sensors have group delay (1–5 ms) from their internal filters. Account for it in fast control loops.
