Inductive Proximity — Eddy Currents and Q Loss

A coil resonant at MHz. Bring a piece of metal close and its losses go up, its inductance goes down, its resonant frequency shifts. The LDC1614 family measures those three numbers continuously — metal proximity, position, and identity, all without contact.

Inductive proximity sensors work because of eddy currents. The sensor's coil radiates an AC magnetic field; that field induces circulating currents in any nearby conductor (the target). Those eddy currents dissipate energy in the target's resistance and create their own opposing magnetic field. Two measurable effects: (1) the coil's effective resistance goes up (Q drops) because of the energy bleeding away into the target, and (2) the coil's effective inductance goes down because the target's counter-field partially cancels the coil's own field. The LDC1614/LDC1612 chips drive an LC tank and read both effects digitally, giving 28-bit position resolution at sub-millimeter precision. The same physics underlies every "inductive prox sensor" cylinder you see on a CNC limit switch — only the format is different, the principle is identical.

How eddy currents change L and Q

Model the target as a single-turn loop with resistance R_t and inductance L_t. The coil and target form a mutual-inductance pair M, with coupling coefficient k = M/√(L_coil·L_t). As the target approaches, k grows from ~0 (far) to ~0.7 (very close). The reflected impedance seen from the coil at angular frequency ω is:

Z_reflected = (ωM)² / (R_t + jωL_t) Real part (added to coil R): R_added = (ωM)² · R_t / (R_t² + (ωL_t)²) Imag part (subtracts from coil L): ΔL = −(ωM)² · L_t / (R_t² + (ωL_t)²) / ω → ferrous targets (high µ_r) add MORE inductance than they subtract — net L goes UP for steel below saturation → non-magnetic conductors (aluminum, copper, brass) subtract inductance → net L goes DOWN

The LDC1614 chip — turning L, R into 28-bit codes

The Texas Instruments LDC1614 is the dominant inductive-sensing chip. It has 4 channels, each driving an external LC tank and reading two numbers: a 28-bit resonant-frequency count and a 16-bit Rp (parallel-resistance) code. The trick is that the chip injects exactly enough current at each cycle to maintain a fixed resonant amplitude; that injection current is proportional to 1/Rp. Result: position from frequency, identification (or temperature) from Rp.

uint32_t readData(uint8_t reg) {

Wirewound vs planar PCB spiral

Two coil families. Wirewound: copper enamel wire wound on a bobbin or air core, often with a ferrite pot core to focus the field. Used in industrial M8/M12/M18/M30 cylindrical prox switches. High inductance per cm³, narrow field. Planar PCB spiral: spiral traces on FR4 (often a multi-layer "stacked" spiral), no machining needed, integrates with the rest of the board, but lower inductance per area. Modern apps (touchless buttons, position encoders) almost always use planar.

Planar single-layer round spiral inductance (Wheeler approximation):

L (µH) ≈ N² · d_avg · K₁ / (1 + K₂·ρ) where K₁ = 2.34, K₂ = 2.75 (round shape), d_avg in mm, ρ = (d_out − d_in)/(d_out + d_in) d_avg = (d_out + d_in)/2 is the average diameter Multi-layer: L_total ≈ N_layers² · L_single_layer × coupling factor (~0.9 for tight stack)

Gear-tooth pickup — sensing ferrous teeth as they pass

An inductive coil aimed at a rotating ferrous gear sees a strong inductance modulation as each tooth crosses the field. This is the principle behind every crankshaft / camshaft sensor in an internal-combustion engine, every wheel-speed sensor in an ABS, and every gear-speed sensor in industrial gearboxes. The output looks like a sine wave whose amplitude grows with rpm (because dΦ/dt grows with speed).

Below ~100 rpm the signal becomes too small to detect — that's why most ABS systems use Hall-effect sensors instead (constant signal vs. speed), reserving VR for high-rpm engine applications where the simplicity and ruggedness pay off.

What metals do the field "see"?

The sensing response depends on the target's conductivity, magnetic permeability, and skin depth at the sensing frequency. Skin depth δ = √(2 / (ω·µ·σ)) tells you how deep the eddy currents penetrate. If target thickness ≪ δ, the eddies are weak and the signal is small. If target thickness ≥ several δ, you've saturated the response.

Worked example — non-contact lid-closed sensor for a coffee robot

The problem

A coffee-making robot needs to detect that the steam-wand cap is in place before pressurizing the boiler. The cap is brushed stainless 304 (austenitic, ~poor conductor, non-magnetic). The cap rests 1 mm above a sense board inside a plastic shroud. False negatives = 30 PSI of steam in someone's face; we need rock-solid detection.

The design

Coil: 14 mm OD planar spiral on PCB, 25 turns, 2 layers → L ≈ 18 µH (Wheeler approx, validated with LCR meter to 17 µH) C_tank = 220 pF (NP0 ceramic, 1% tolerance, low temp drift) → f_res = 1/(2π√(18e-6 · 220e-12)) ≈ 2.53 MHz LDC1614 settles to ~25 µs per measurement → easy 10 kHz update No cap: count ≈ X (recorded at boot, before pressurization) Cap present: count drops by ~3000 LSBs (steel-derated SS304 is 0.15× of mild steel) Threshold: midpoint with 50% hysteresis → set at boot using sliding window

The firmware policy

Hard interlock: pressurize only if cap-present count is > 2× threshold-margin away from "no cap" baseline. Use a 100 ms median filter to ignore mechanical jitter. Cross-check with a redundant Hall sensor that detects a small magnet bonded to the inside of the cap. Both must agree — single-sensor failure shouldn't release pressure.

What to notice

  • Inductive ≠ "metal detector" alone — modern LDC chips deliver sub-micron position, gear-tooth speed, and material discrimination, not just on/off.
  • Steel goes UP in inductance, aluminum goes DOWN — ferromagnetic targets boost L (until they saturate the field); non-magnetic conductors cut L by eddy reaction. The LDC1614 reports both.
  • Range ~ coil diameter — a 10 mm coil senses ~3–10 mm; a 30 mm coil senses out to ~30 mm. Larger coil = longer range but also lower spatial resolution.
  • Q drop = lossy target = small or thin or poor-conductor — Q drop tells you about the target's conductivity, not its position. Use it for material identification or compensating for temperature drift in coin acceptors and metal sorters.
  • Mounting matters — any metal in the chip's field counts as a target. Use plastic standoffs, keep metal mounting screws far from the coil, and provide a "free field" backplane (or no backplane) for the coil.
  • Surface roughness creates noise — a milled target with bumps and grooves modulates inductance as it moves. Filtering helps but doesn't eliminate. Polished or precision-ground targets give the cleanest signal.

Where you'll see this circuit

Real uses

  • Industrial proximity switches — M8/M12/M18/M30 cylindrical cans on machine guards, conveyor end-of-line, robot home positions.
  • Wheel-speed and crank-position sensors — automotive ABS, engine timing, RPM tachometers.
  • Touchless buttons under metal panels — appliance UIs that put a stainless plate over the sensor for a vandal-proof exterior.
  • Position encoders — LDC1101/1614 with a custom target pattern can do absolute linear or rotary position with sub-µm precision.
  • Coin and metal-object sorters — discriminate metal type using the Q-drop signature.
  • Robot end-stop limit switches — industrial CNC and pick-and-place machines.

SHORTCUTS

  • L ↑ for ferrous, L ↓ for non-mag — read the sign to know your metal.
  • Range ≈ coil diameter for ferrous targets, 0.5× for non-ferrous.
  • Below ~100 rpm a VR (gear-tooth) sensor signal is unusable — switch to Hall.
  • Q drops the most when target thickness ≈ a few skin depths.
  • LDC chips need NP0 / C0G capacitors on the tank — ±0.5% drift over temp; X7R will swamp your sensitivity.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Metal in the chip's field — mounting hardware, traces, ground planes all alter the baseline. Provide a "keep-out" of at least 2× coil diameter.
  • Temperature drift — coil copper resistance + Cu inductance + cap drift all shift the reading. Add a reference coil channel on the same board.
  • Cross-talk — multiple LDC channels at similar frequencies interfere. Use different L·C combos per channel, or scan one at a time.
  • Saturation — at very close range to ferrous, the field saturates the steel and the sensitivity collapses (L stops going up). Plan for the linear region; treat saturation as "fully closed".
  • Sensitive to vibration — any micro-motion of coil or target shows up in the count. Mechanical decoupling and median filtering help.
