Strain Gauge Bridge

A strain gauge is a thin foil resistor whose resistance changes about 0.1 % per millistrain — too small to see directly with an ADC. The Wheatstone bridge solves that: balance two voltage dividers, then watch the tiny imbalance produced by the gauge. Quarter, half, and full bridges trade off cost, sensitivity (1×, 2×, 4×), and thermal rejection.

Bridge configurations

All three bridges have four arms. The difference is how many are active gauges (responding to strain) versus fixed precision resistors. The number of active arms multiplies sensitivity directly: 1, 2, or 4.

  • Quarter bridge (1 gauge): cheapest, simplest. ΔV ∝ ε/4. No temperature compensation — the gauge resistance also drifts with ambient, indistinguishable from real strain.
  • Half bridge (2 gauges): two gauges on opposite arms of the bridge, mounted so one stretches while the other compresses (e.g., top + bottom of a beam, or one axial + one transverse). 2× the output, plus differential thermal effects cancel.
  • Full bridge (4 gauges): all four arms active, two stretching and two compressing. 4× output and the best temperature rejection. Found in load cells, torque transducers, and pressure sensors.

How the bridge math works

Imagine the bridge as two stacked dividers, both tapped at half-rail. With all four arms equal (R), the tap on the left is V_exc/2, the tap on the right is V_exc/2, and the differential V_out = 0. That's the balance condition. Now nudge one arm by ΔR.

1. Left tap voltage (R + ΔR on top, R on bottom): V_L = V_exc · R / (2R + ΔR) 2. Right tap voltage (R on top, R on bottom): V_R = V_exc · R / 2R = V_exc / 2 3. Difference: V_out = V_R − V_L = V_exc · (1/2 − R/(2R+ΔR)) 4. Simplify: V_out = V_exc · ΔR / (2·(2R + ΔR)) 5. For small ΔR ≪ R, the denominator ≈ 4R: → V_out ≈ V_exc · ΔR / (4R) = V_exc · (ΔR/R) / 4 6. Substitute the gauge factor relation: ΔR/R = GF · ε 7. Result (quarter bridge): → V_out ≈ V_exc · GF · ε / 4

The "small signal" approximation (step 5) is the entire reason the bridge is the dominant strain-readout topology: linear in ε right up to ~5 mε, where strain gauges fail anyway. For half-bridge double the numerator (two equal-and-opposite ΔR terms add); for full-bridge, double it again (four ΔR terms cancel the denominator's bias).

Gauge factor — what's actually changing

A strain gauge is a long, thin, zigzagged foil. Stretching it does two things at once: makes it longer (raises R), and makes it thinner (also raises R). Compressing reverses both. The combined effect:

Resistance: R = ρ · L / A Differentiate: dR/R = dρ/ρ + dL/L − dA/A = (1 + 2ν) · ε + π_L · σ The two effects are bundled into a single empirical constant: GF = (ΔR/R) / ε Common foil gauges: GF ≈ 2.0; semiconductor gauges (piezoresistive): GF ≈ 100–200

Temperature compensation

The single biggest measurement error in a quarter bridge is not mechanical strain — it's the gauge's own resistance drifting with ambient temperature. Foil gauges drift on the order of 30 ppm/°C. A 20 °C swing looks like 600 µε of apparent strain.

Half and full bridges fix this by putting the second active gauge on the same piece of material at the same temperature. Both arms drift identically, so their difference cancels. The same logic applies to lead-wire resistance: a half-bridge in three-wire configuration (or full bridge in four-wire) puts lead resistance in both arms of the same divider, where it subtracts out.

Worked example — 50 kg load cell on a HX711

The cell

A 50 kg straight-bar load cell (the kind used in kitchen scales and Sesame quadruped foot sensors). Four foil gauges in full-bridge, rated 1.0 mV/V at full scale, GF ≈ 2.0. Excited from a 5 V source with the HX711 24-bit ADC on a 128× gain channel.

The bridge output at full scale

V_out (full scale) = 1.0 mV/V · 5 V = 5.0 mV (50 kg load → 5 mV differential) Reverse-solve strain: for a full bridge with 4 active gauges, V_out = V_exc · GF · ε (no /4). So ε = V_out / (V_exc · GF) = 5.0 mV / (5 V · 2.0) = 500 µε (500 µε at 50 kg — typical for a stiff beam load cell)

Reading on the HX711

HX711 input range @ 128× scales with AVDD: ±0.5·(AVDD/128) = ±AVDD/256 per the datasheet. At AVDD=5 V → ±19.5 mV (half-range; peak-to-peak = 39 mV); at AVDD=2.7 V → ±10.5 mV. (The often-quoted "±20 mV" is the AVDD=5 V half-range rounded up — not the 2.7 V case.) LSB at 128×, AVDD=5 V = peak-to-peak / 2^24 = (AVDD/128) / 2^24 = 5 V / (128 · 2^24) ≈ 2.33 nV (theoretical; noise floor ~80 nV RMS limits real resolution) Effective resolution ≈ 5 mV / 80 nV = 62,500 counts ≈ 16 bits noise-free (at 10 Hz output rate) Grams per count: 50 kg / 62,500 ≈ 0.8 g (matches typical bench-scale spec)

What to notice

  • The bridge multiplies, not just adds. Half-bridge gives 2× quarter, but it also rejects common-mode thermal drift — both wins for the price of one extra gauge. That's usually the right starting place for any precision measurement.
  • The signal is tiny but linear. 5 V excitation × 2.0 GF × 1 mε = 2.5 mV at the bridge output. That's about 0.05 % of the supply. Without amplification, this would be dwarfed by noise; with a low-offset instrumentation amp or a dedicated ΔΣ ADC (HX711, ADS1232, AD7124), it resolves cleanly.
  • Excitation voltage and gauge resistance trade self-heating against signal. Higher V_exc raises the output linearly but also dissipates more power (V²/R) in each gauge. 350 Ω gauges at 5 V dissipate 18 mW each — fine for steel, marginal for plastic. Switch to 1 kΩ gauges or lower the excitation to 2.5 V if the substrate is heat-sensitive.
  • Beam strain at yield is ~3 mε. Engineering steel yields around 0.2 % strain (2000 µε); aluminum, 4 mε. So load-cell gauges typically see 0.5–2 mε at full-rated load, which keeps them well inside their linear range (5 mε max) and well below yield (so they survive overload).
  • Lead-wire matters at 350 Ω. 1 m of 24 AWG copper is about 84 mΩ. In a quarter-bridge 350 Ω arm, that's already 240 µε of apparent strain. Three-wire half-bridge or four-wire full-bridge moves the leads to a place where they cancel out.

Where you'll see this

Real uses

  • Load cells — every digital scale, kitchen to truck. Full bridge, mounted on a precision-machined aluminum spring element.
  • Foot force sensors on the Sesame quadruped feet — used to detect ground contact and step force.
  • Torque transducers — full-bridge gauges on a shaft, measuring shear strain. The basis of every torque wrench above $200.
  • Pressure sensors — gauges on a diaphragm. Pressure deflects the diaphragm, stretching gauges on top and compressing those on bottom: a perfect natural full-bridge configuration.
  • Modal analysis — rosette gauges (three at 0°/45°/90°) measuring principal strain on aircraft wings, bridge girders, race-car chassis.

RULES OF THUMB

  • GF foil ≈ 2.0; GF semiconductor ≈ 100–200 (50× more sensitive, but brittle).
  • R_nom standard values: 120 Ω, 350 Ω, 1000 Ω.
  • Working strain range: ±2 mε (foil); ±0.5 mε (semi).
  • Bridge sensitivity at full load: 1–3 mV/V.
  • For temperature stability, always use half or full bridge.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Forgetting self-heating on small substrates — gauge dissipates I²·R and heats itself, drifting its own resistance. Solution: lower V_exc, or pulse-excite.
  • Two-wire quarter bridge over long leads — the lead resistance is in series with the gauge, indistinguishable from strain. Three-wire fixes the temperature drift but not the offset; four-wire fixes both.
  • Ground loops with separately grounded V_exc — float the bridge or use a differential ADC.
  • EMI pickup — bridge output is millivolts; long unshielded leads near a motor will read noise. Twist + shield + low-pass.
  • Initial offset — even a brand-new bridge has 1–5 % offset from gauge tolerance. Always tare after mounting.
