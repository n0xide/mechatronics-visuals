Thermistor Linearization

A bare NTC thermistor's resistance varies by 50× over its operating range. The β-equation captures most of that curve in a single fit parameter; Steinhart-Hart's three-parameter form adds 100× more accuracy where it counts. Pairing the NTC with a series resistor inside a voltage divider gives you a sweet spot of near-linear voltage vs. temperature — the trick is choosing R_series correctly.

The R(T) curve

Negative-temperature-coefficient (NTC) thermistors are sintered metal-oxide ceramics. Their conduction is dominated by thermal activation across a band gap, giving an exponential R-vs-T relationship: roughly halving every 10 °C above 25 °C, doubling every 10 °C below it.

The most common type is the 10 kΩ @ 25 °C bead. Its full curve spans about 33 kΩ at 0 °C, 10 kΩ at 25 °C, 3.6 kΩ at 50 °C, and 0.32 kΩ at 125 °C — over two decades of variation across a typical "room-to-oven" range.

The β-equation: one parameter

The Arrhenius-flavored Beta equation models the NTC with a single fit constant. It's accurate within ~1 % across a 50 °C span; outside that, errors grow to several degrees.

Definition (T and T₀ in kelvin, β in K): R(T) = R₀ · exp[ β · (1/T − 1/T₀) ] Inverse: 1/T = 1/T₀ + (1/β) · ln(R / R₀) Datasheet often gives β as β(25/85) — fit between those two temperatures.

β captures the curve's steepness. Typical 10 kΩ NTCs have β ≈ 3380–3950 K. Higher β → steeper R-vs-T → better sensitivity but worse linearity at the extremes.

Steinhart-Hart: three parameters

The full third-order fit. Used when 0.1 °C accuracy matters across a wide range (medical, calibration baths).

1/T = A + B · ln(R) + C · (ln R)³ A, B, C are fit constants determined by measuring R at three known temperatures (usually 0, 25, 50 °C or similar). Typical values for a Vishay 10 kΩ NTC: A ≈ 1.40 · 10⁻³ , B ≈ 2.37 · 10⁻⁴ , C ≈ 9.90 · 10⁻⁸ Inverse (Steinhart-Hart for R given T) — solve the cubic in ln(R), or just use a table lookup with bilinear interp.

Difference between β and Steinhart-Hart over a typical 0–100 °C range: β might be 1 °C off at the extremes, Steinhart-Hart 0.01 °C. For thermal-management firmware (CPU fan curves, hotend control, battery temperature) β is plenty.

The divider's linearization sweet spot

R(T) is exponential — V(T) doesn't have to be. Putting the NTC in a voltage divider with a well-chosen series resistor straightens the V-vs-T curve over a useful range. The output voltage is:

Standard divider: V_out = V_ref · R_NTC / (R_series + R_NTC) Or the inverse arrangement (NTC on top, R_s on bottom): V_out = V_ref · R_series / (R_series + R_NTC)

Plot V_out vs. T and you'll see an S-shape, nearly linear in the middle, flattening at the extremes. The inflection point — where the curve is steepest and the second derivative is zero — sits where R_NTC = R_series. So if you pick R_series = R_25 (10 kΩ for a 10 kΩ NTC), your sensitivity is best around 25 °C.

To target a different sweet spot, set R_series = R(T_target). For battery monitoring you'd want maximum sensitivity around 45–55 °C (where overheating starts): use R_s ≈ R(50 °C) ≈ 3.6 kΩ instead of 10 kΩ.

Sweet spot: Pick R_series ≈ R(T_target). Maximum dV/dT occurs where R_NTC = R_series, giving |dV/dT|_max = V_ref · β / (4·T²) ≈ 11 mV/°C at 25 °C with β=3950.

Worked example — Bambu hotend thermistor reading

The thermistor

A typical 3D-printer hotend uses a Semitec 104GT-2 or equivalent: 100 kΩ @ 25 °C, β = 4267 K. Range: 25 °C to 300 °C. Read by the printer mainboard via a 4.7 kΩ pull-up to 3.3 V, ADC on the divider midpoint.

Computing R at 200 °C (typical PLA temp)

R(200 °C) = 100 kΩ · exp[ 4267 · (1/473.15 − 1/298.15) ] (T must be Kelvin) = 100 kΩ · exp[ 4267 · (-1.244·10⁻³) ] = 100 kΩ · exp[ -5.307 ] = 100 kΩ · 0.00494 = 494 Ω (roughly the same as room-temp gauges' lead wire!)

The divider output

V_out = 3.3 V · 494 Ω / (4700 + 494) = 0.314 V (at hot) V_out @ 25 °C = 3.3 V · 100 kΩ / (4.7 + 100) kΩ = 3.152 V (at room) Full ADC swing (3.152 − 0.314 V) maps to (25 °C → 200 °C) — about 16 mV/°C average.

Sweet-spot choice

The 4.7 kΩ pull-up was chosen because R(200 °C) ≈ 5 kΩ. That puts maximum sensitivity right at printing temperature — exactly where tight control matters. At room temperature, R_NTC ≫ R_series, the divider saturates near V_ref, and sensitivity is poor — but you don't care, because the printer is cold and any reading "above 50 °C" suffices for safe-to-touch detection.

What to notice

  • R doubles per 10 °C below 25 °C, halves per 10 °C above — for a typical β ≈ 3950 K NTC. This is the back-of-envelope rule.
  • Sensitivity peaks where R_NTC = R_series — design R_series to land your "interesting" temperature range there. Don't just default to R_25.
  • β-equation is fine for ±25 °C around the design point. Steinhart-Hart only earns its cost for wide-range or high-accuracy instruments. Most firmware uses a lookup table anyway.
  • Self-heating at higher excitation currents lifts the apparent temperature. Rule of thumb: I·V across the NTC should stay below 50 µW for ±0.1 °C error in still air, below 500 µW in well-stirred fluid. 10 kΩ @ 3.3 V dissipates about 100 µW — borderline.
  • Linearization in software beats analog. Don't try to add op-amp circuits to "fix" the curve. Read raw ADC counts, look up T via β or table, and let the firmware handle the math.

Where you'll see this

Real uses

  • 3D-printer hotends and beds — most ubiquitous NTC application. 100 kΩ Semitec for hotend, 100 kΩ for bed.
  • Battery pack monitoring — one NTC per parallel group, checked by the BMS for over-temperature shutdown.
  • CPU/GPU fan curves — laptop and desktop motherboards use NTCs near the VRM, near the CPU socket, in the chassis.
  • Inrush current limiters — special "self-heating" NTCs starting at 10 Ω cold, dropping to 0.1 Ω after thermal soak. Used in series with power-supply diode bridges.
  • Compensation networks — paired with op-amp gain to make a circuit's temperature drift cancel out.

RULES OF THUMB

  • Common nominal: 10 kΩ @ 25 °C with β=3950, or 100 kΩ @ 25 °C with β=4267.
  • R doubles per −10 °C, halves per +10 °C — for β ≈ 3950.
  • Kelvin always: T₀ = 298.15 K = 25 °C in all β-equation work.
  • Lookup table over math — 32-entry, lerp between: 0.01 °C accuracy, sub-µs.
  • Self-heating budget: < 50 µW in air, < 500 µW in liquid.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Celsius in the β-equation — instant 273 °C error. Always Kelvin.
  • Wrong polarity in code — confusing higher voltage = lower R with higher voltage = higher T (depends on which arm the NTC is on).
  • Using 5 % carbon-film for R_series — that error propagates 1:1 to your temperature reading at the inflection point. Use 1 %.
  • Ignoring lead resistance for hotend thermistors — 1 m of 28 AWG = ~0.2 Ω, but R(200°C) is only ~500 Ω, so 0.04 % error. Mostly fine. For cryo applications, four-wire is mandatory.
  • One-point calibration assumes the datasheet β. Real-world tolerance on β is ±1–3 %. Two-point cal (room + ice bath) corrects most of it.
