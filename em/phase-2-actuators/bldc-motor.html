BLDC Motor — 3-Phase Commutation

A brushless DC motor is a brushed DC motor with the commutator moved into the driver. Six MOSFETs replace mechanical brushes; rotor position tells the driver which two phases to energize next.

A BLDC has a 3-phase Y-wound stator (three coils sharing a star point) and a permanent-magnet rotor. To spin it, you must always pull the rotor field forward — energize phases such that the resulting stator field leads the rotor by 90 electrical degrees. The driver needs to know rotor position; choices are Hall sensors (three switches detecting magnet position, gives 6 sectors of 60°), encoders (continuous angle for sinusoidal/FOC), or sensorless back-EMF (watch the un-energized phase voltage cross zero, infer position). Three commutation flavors: 6-step trapezoidal (simple, audible torque ripple), sinusoidal (smooth currents, suboptimal at high speed), FOC (sinusoidal + Park/Clarke transform to control torque-producing current directly — the modern default).

What to notice

  • Mechanical RPM ≠ electrical RPM. With P pole pairs, the rotor turns 1× while the driver completes P electrical cycles. A 14-pole (7 pole-pair) motor at 2000 RPM mech runs at 14000 RPM electrical — that's why high-pole BLDCs (drone motors, gimbals) want fast PWM and fast MCUs.
  • Trapezoidal commutation has 6 discrete states per electrical rev. Switching between them produces a torque ripple visible as a "cogging" feel at low speed. FOC eliminates this by smoothly rotating the stator field.
  • Hall sensors give 60° resolution. Enough for 6-step trapezoidal. For sinusoidal or FOC you need continuous angle (encoder or sensorless observer).
  • Sensorless = no Halls = simpler, but needs minimum speed. The driver watches the floating phase's back-EMF cross zero to infer rotor position. Below ~5–10% of rated speed there's no BEMF, so you need either a startup ramp or HFI (high-frequency injection) for sensorless start.
  • Kv [RPM/V] is the hobby dual of Ke [V·s/rad]. Datasheet Kv is no-load RPM per applied volt. Conversion: Kv = 60 / (2π · Ke) ⟺ Ke = 60 / (2π · Kv). Because Kt [N·m/A] = Ke in SI, a high-Kv motor (e.g. 2300 KV racing drone) has tiny Kt ≈ 4.15 mN·m/A — spins fast, makes little torque per amp. Low-Kv (90 KV robotics joint) has Kt ≈ 106 mN·m/A — slow, torquey.
  • FOC is current vector control. The Park transform projects three phase currents into rotor-frame Id (flux) and Iq (torque-producing). Hold Id = 0, control Iq → torque is exactly proportional to current, like a brushed DC motor with a perfectly behaved gearbox.

MEMORIZE

  • 3 Halls = 6 sectors — perfect fit for 6-step trapezoidal.
  • FOC needs encoder — Hall-only = no FOC (well, no good FOC).
  • Star vs Delta — Y-wound (star) typical for low-speed/high-torque; Delta for high-speed.
  • Pole count — drone: 12–14 poles; servo: 4–8; large traction: 4–6.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Wrong Hall order — motor judders or won't start. Try permutations of phase wiring or Hall mapping.
  • Sensorless startup — open-loop ramp needed; expect 1–3 s before BEMF detection takes over.
  • Mech vs elec angle — always work in electrical for control math.
  • Overheating low-Kv motors at low torque is rare; overheating at high torque + stall is constant.
