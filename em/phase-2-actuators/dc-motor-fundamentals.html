Brushed DC Motor — Torque, Speed, Power

One linear equation describes the whole machine: V = Ke·ω + I·R. From it falls torque-speed, peak power at half speed, and peak efficiency somewhere else.

A permanent-magnet brushed DC motor is the simplest electromechanical converter you will ever own. A rotating armature winding, fed current through a commutator and brushes, sits in a fixed magnetic field. Two constants describe it: Kt (torque per amp, N·m/A) and Ke (back-EMF per radian/sec, V·s/rad). In SI units they are numerically equal — the same physics that turns current into torque turns rotation into voltage. Drive the motor at voltage V and the armature accelerates until back-EMF Ke·ω plus the I·R drop equal V. Slow it down by loading the shaft: ω falls, BEMF falls, current and torque rise. At stall the BEMF is zero, all V drops across R, and current is dangerously high.

Commutator + brushes — current reversal in the rotating armature

Every half-turn the commutator swaps which brush feeds which coil end. The current through the wire stays oriented the same way relative to the B-field, so force is always tangential and torque is unidirectional. Brushes wear; this is why brushed motors are eventually replaced by BLDC in long-life applications.

What to notice

  • Torque-speed is a straight line — slope = −Kt²/R. Two anchor points fully define it: stall (ω=0, T=T_stall) and no-load (T=0, ω=V/Ke). Everything else follows.
  • Peak power sits at half speed, half torque — P = T·ω is a parabola; its apex is at ω = ω_no_load/2. Useful, because peak power is rarely the operating point — it is exactly where heat dissipation also peaks.
  • Peak efficiency is well past peak power — efficiency rises toward no-load because losses are I²R and BEMF is doing more of the voltage drop. At stall, efficiency is zero (all electrical input becomes heat).
  • Stall current is V/R, often 10–30× rated — held for more than a second or two, the winding burns out. Always current-limit your driver, or fuse the supply, before connecting an untested motor.
  • Back-EMF makes the motor a generator — disconnect the supply and let the shaft spin: voltage appears across the terminals. This is how DC motors short-brake (short the terminals → BEMF drives circulating current → braking torque).
  • Gearbox shifts the operating point — output torque ×N, output speed ÷N. Choose N to put the motor near its high-efficiency zone while the load sees what it needs. Reflected load inertia drops by N².

MEMORIZE

  • Kt = Ke in SI — N·m/A and V·s/rad are the same number.
  • Peak P at ω/2 — never the steady operating point.
  • η peaks near no-load — light loading is efficient, not heavy.
  • Gear ratio N — T×N, ω÷N, J_load÷N² reflected to shaft.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Stall = burnout — I_stall = V/R lasts seconds, not minutes.
  • "Bigger motor" is wrong — usually you need a different gear ratio, not more motor.
  • Reading rated power as max — peak P is 2–4× rated, but only for seconds.
  • Forgetting brush life — brushed motors die at 1000–5000 hrs continuous; pick BLDC if duty cycle is high.
  • Kv units trap — hobby "Kv" is RPM/V (no-load), not the SI Ke. Convert via Ke [V·s/rad] = 60/(2π·Kv_RPM/V); Kt [N·m/A] = Ke in SI.
