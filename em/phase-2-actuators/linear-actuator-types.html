Linear Actuator Types

Lead screw, ball screw, belt, linear motor — four ways to turn rotation (or electromagnetic force) into linear motion. Each occupies a different point on the force-vs-speed envelope. Lead screw: slow, strong, self-locking. Ball screw: fast, smooth, back-drivable. Belt: fastest, lowest force, no holding. Linear motor: precision and stiffness, but expensive. Pick by load, speed, and accuracy — not by what's on the shelf.

Lead screw — slow, strong, self-locking

A lead screw is a coarse-threaded screw and matching nut: turn the screw, the nut translates (or vice versa). Friction between thread surfaces converts rotary motion to linear motion at a controlled mechanical advantage. The same friction is the actuator's main characteristic: it absorbs energy, so the screw cannot back-drive when properly designed — the nut won't push the screw around no matter how hard you pull the load.

Force/torque relation: F_axial = (2π · τ_motor · η) / lead — where lead is mm/rev, τ is motor torque in N·m, η is efficiency. For a NEMA-17 stepper (40 N·cm = 0.4 N·m) driving a Tr8×8 lead screw (8 mm lead) at 35% efficiency: F = 2π × 0.4 × 0.35 / 0.008 = 110 N axial. Triple the motor → triple the force. Halve the lead → double the force, halve the speed.

Ball screw — fast, smooth, back-drivable

A ball screw replaces sliding thread contact with rolling balls in a recirculating raceway. The balls roll instead of slip; friction drops to a few percent; efficiency jumps to 90 %+. The screw is no longer self-locking — a downward load will back-drive the screw unless a brake is added. Worth the extra cost when you need speed, accuracy, and long life.

The key trade-off: ball screws give back what they take. The 85–95 % efficiency that makes them fast also makes them back-drivable. For a vertical Z-axis on a CNC, that means a power-off brake is mandatory — without it, gravity drops the head. For a horizontal axis with no gravity component, back-drive doesn't matter and the high efficiency is pure win.

Belt + pulley — fastest, lowest force, no holding

A timing belt over two pulleys converts rotation to translation directly. The driving pulley winds belt around itself; the slack side delivers length to the moving carriage. Linear speed = pulley_teeth × pitch × motor RPM/60. For force: F = 2π · τ_motor / (teeth · pitch). Belts give the highest acceleration of any actuator type, but force scales inversely with pulley size — and large pulleys eat your range.

Why belts win on speed: a 20T GT2 pulley at 1000 RPM moves 40 × 1000/60 = 666 mm/s. The same RPM on a 5 mm lead screw moves only 83 mm/s. Belts are 5–10× faster at the same motor speed; the trade is they can't hold position without power and they stretch.

Linear motor — precision, stiffness, expensive

A linear motor unrolls the rotary motor — the stator is a track of magnets (or coils), the slider is the matching active element. Direct electromagnetic force, no mechanical linkage between the motor and the load. The result: no backlash, no wear-out from converting rotation, near-infinite acceleration capability, and a price tag 3–10× the equivalent ball-screw stage.

Two flavors you'll see: iron-core (high force density, magnetic cogging from teeth = positional ripple) and coreless (lower force per length, completely smooth — used where servo bandwidth needs < 1 ms response). U-channel coreless is the type in most wafer-handling stages and semiconductor pick-and-place.

Sub-types: voice-coil (covered in its own visual) is a short-stroke iron-core variant for ~5 mm strokes with very high bandwidth. Brushless DC linear motors have segmented magnets and Hall sensors, like a rotary BLDC unrolled.

Worked example — Z-axis for a benchtop CNC

The constraints

Z-axis on a small CNC mill: 200 mm stroke, must hold ~10 kg spindle motor mass against gravity (~100 N), feed rate 5 mm/s during cutting, rapids 50 mm/s, position accuracy 25 µm.

Candidate evaluation

Lead screw (Tr10×2): 5 mm/s easy. Self-locking → no brake needed for hold. Force = 2π × 0.5 × 0.4 / 0.002 = 628 N. Backlash 50 µm... marginal. Ball screw (SFU1204): Same speed easy. 90% efficient → much smaller motor. Backlash < 10 µm preloaded. Needs brake for hold. Belt: 5 m/s achievable but 100 N hold force impossible without brake. Z-axis ruled out. Linear motor: precision overkill, cost 5× ball screw. Ruled out for benchtop budget.

Decision

Lead screw (anti-backlash nut) — self-holding, cheap, marginal-but-acceptable accuracy OR ball screw + power-off brake — better accuracy, smoother motion, ~3× cost Choice: SFU1204 ball screw + 24 V power-off brake on the motor Acceleration headroom: ball screw efficiency lets the motor accelerate twice as fast Long-term: ball screw lasts 10×+ longer than lead screw before backlash creeps up

Notes

A common compromise: lead screw (TR8 ×2) for benchtop hobby CNC because cost-effective and self-locking; upgrade to ball screw on the upgrade path. The arm-6dof-pa612 prototype uses a ball-screw Z because of cutting forces; the so100-mega prototype keeps lead screws on hold-required joints and belt drives on speed-required ones.

What to notice

  • Self-locking is the lead screw's headline feature. Cut power, the screw doesn't move. For vertical or safety-critical axes, this is the difference between a working machine and one that drops the spindle on the part.
  • Ball screws need brakes. Anywhere a load can back-drive (gravity, spring, residual cutting force), a ball-screw axis needs a power-off brake or an active hold servo. Ignore at your peril.
  • Belts can't hold position long-term. Stretch + temperature drift + tension creep means a belt-driven axis is only accurate when it's actively servoing. Power off, position is lost.
  • Linear motors are direct-coupling — no mechanical drift. No screw to wear, no belt to stretch. The accuracy budget is the encoder, not the actuator. This is why semiconductor and metrology stages always use them.
  • Force × speed is the deciding cut. A given motor torque × actuator type sets a hyperbola in F-v space. Lead screws bend it strongly toward force; belts bend it strongly toward speed. Pick the curve you need.
  • Anti-backlash matters more than people expect. A 100 µm backlash hurts position accuracy by 100 µm — and worse, it changes sign each reversal direction. For CAM operations with frequent reversal, anti-backlash nuts (lead screws) or preloaded ball nuts (ball screws) are mandatory.
  • Hybrid systems work. A printer's X+Y are belts (need speed); the Z is a lead screw (need hold). The extruder is a stepper-driven gear (the wire-feed analogy is a tiny lead screw). One design uses different actuator types per axis based on need.

Where you'll see this

Real uses

  • 3D printer XY: belt, Z: lead screw — printer split is canonical.
  • CNC mill: ball screw on all axes — accuracy + speed; Z needs brake or pneumatic counterbalance.
  • Laser cutter: belt on both axes — head is light, force is small, speed wins.
  • Linear stage (lab / optics): lead screw or ball screw — depends on positioning vs throughput.
  • Wafer/PCB assembly: linear motor — required precision and bandwidth.
  • Door / lift actuator: lead screw — slow, strong, self-locking (door doesn't fall when power cuts).
  • Robot end-effector grippers: voice-coil + linear motor for force-controlled grip; screw-jack for hold-and-clamp.
  • CT scanner / MRI bed: ball screw — heavy, slow, accurate.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

  • F = 2π · τ · η / lead axial force from motor torque
  • v = lead · ω / (2π) = lead · RPM / 60 linear speed
  • η_lead_screw ≈ 30–50 %
  • η_ball_screw ≈ 85–95 %
  • η_belt ≈ 95 %+
  • Self-locking if pitch angle < arctan(µ)

SELECTION TREE

  • Need holding without power → lead screw (or add brake).
  • Need > 200 mm/s → belt or linear motor.
  • Need < 10 µm accuracy → preloaded ball screw or linear motor.
  • Need > 5 kN force → ball screw or hydraulic.
  • Need highest bandwidth → linear motor (voice-coil for short stroke).
  • Budget-constrained, hobby → lead screw + stepper.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Ball screw without brake on vertical axis — gravity drops the load when power cuts.
  • Belt for long-term position-hold — stretch and creep destroy accuracy.
  • Sizing motor for steady-state load instead of accel + load — undersizing torque is the #1 cause of motor stall.
  • Ignoring critical speed on long ball screws — screw spinning above its critical speed whips, breaks bearings.
  • Wrong DN value for ball nuts — exceeding rated DN (diameter × RPM) destroys ball recirculation.
  • Lead-screw nut wear — hobby Acme nuts wear 50 µm in 100 km of travel. Plan replacements.
  • Skipping a coupling between motor and screw — direct-coupling forces shaft misalignment → bearing failure. Always use a flexible coupling (jaw, spring, or bellows).
