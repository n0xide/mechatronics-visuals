MEMS Sensors — Comb Drives, Capacitive Sense, Sigma-Delta Readout

Silicon chips with tiny moving parts: 10–100 µm springs, 1–10 µm gaps, all etched in a foundry. Accelerometers, gyros, microphones, pressure sensors — all the same building blocks: a proof mass on a spring, a comb-finger capacitor to sense motion, a sigma-delta loop to digitize it.

MEMS (Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems) are integrated circuits with moving mechanical structures etched into the silicon itself. The dominant pattern: a small proof mass (10–1000 µg, suspended on cantilever or folded springs that flex by 10 nm to a few µm), with interdigitated comb fingers on its sides that form a variable capacitor with stationary anchor fingers. When the proof mass moves under acceleration, rotation (Coriolis), pressure, or sound, the capacitance changes by aF (attofarads). A sigma-delta loop continuously feeds back electrostatic force to null the motion, and the resulting bit-stream is filtered into a high-resolution digital output. Same architecture, four kinds of input → four kinds of sensor.

Interdigitated comb fingers — the universal MEMS capacitor

Each pair of comb fingers (one fixed, one moving) forms a parallel-plate capacitor. Many fingers are arrayed in parallel to multiply the total ΔC for a given mass displacement. The capacitance depends on overlap area: C = ε₀·A_overlap/g, where g is the gap. When the proof mass moves laterally by Δx, the overlap area changes by N · h · Δx, where N is the finger count and h is the silicon thickness (typically 10–25 µm in foundry SOI processes).

The proof-mass accelerometer

A small mass m suspended on a spring k inside the chip. When the package accelerates at a, the mass — by Newton's 2nd law — lags behind the package by Δx = m·a/k = a/ω₀², where ω₀ = √(k/m) is the natural resonance frequency. The comb-finger sensor reads Δx. The dominant constraints: spring softness (high sensitivity, low BW), Brownian noise floor, and over-range mechanical stops.

The 3-axis IMU you find in phones (LSM6DSO, BMI270) typically uses 3 separate proof masses, each measuring one axis. The full-scale displacement is on the order of 0.1–1 µm at FS, and the comb fingers turn that into ~100 fF of total capacitance change. Σ-Δ digitization gives 16-bit output at 1 kHz, ~80 µg/√Hz noise density. ODR up to 6.66 kHz on modern parts.

MEMS gyroscope — Coriolis-effect sense

You can't directly measure angular velocity Ω with a simple proof mass — there's no static deflection from rotation. The trick: drive the proof mass into a fixed oscillation (the "drive mode") at its drive resonance frequency, ~20 kHz. When the chip rotates at Ω, the moving mass experiences a Coriolis force F_c = 2·m·v_drive × Ω perpendicular to both the drive motion and the rotation axis. This deflects the mass in a perpendicular "sense mode" by an amount proportional to Ω. A separate set of comb fingers reads the sense-mode amplitude, which is demodulated against the drive oscillation phase to recover Ω.

Drive and sense modes should have similar resonant frequencies (mode-matching) for maximum sensitivity — but too close and temperature/process drift destroys stability. Most commercial gyros split Δf by 100–500 Hz; high-performance navigation gyros use closed-loop mode-matching trimmed at the factory.

MEMS microphone and absolute pressure

A MEMS mic is a tiny diaphragm (~1 mm² of perforated silicon nitride or doped polysilicon) over a back-volume cavity. Sound waves push the diaphragm by ±10 nm at typical levels; the diaphragm forms one plate of a capacitor with a fixed back-plate. ΔC ~ aF, but with a high-impedance bias and a sigma-delta front-end on the same chip (or stacked die), you get 60–70 dB SNR and very low noise floor (~25 dB SPL equivalent).

An absolute pressure sensor uses the same building block but with a sealed reference cavity (often vacuum). Atmospheric pressure pushes the diaphragm in, deflecting it by amounts proportional to P − P_ref. Often paired with a piezoresistive readout instead of capacitive, because piezoresistors integrate naturally into the silicon strip.

Sigma-delta capacitive readout — the killer architecture

Direct capacitance measurement at the aF level is impossible with a standard analog front-end — parasitics and amplifier noise destroy you. The Σ-Δ trick: don't measure C; force-balance the proof mass to its zero position by feeding back an electrostatic force, and count the feedback bits. The bit-stream IS the digital output; a digital filter (decimator) integrates it to a high-resolution N-bit word.

Why force balance helps: the mechanical structure stays near its rest position, so nonlinearity in the C-x relationship (which is sharp because gap-mode capacitance is 1/g) doesn't kick in. The bit-stream is dense (1's and 0's mixed in proportion to input), and a decimating low-pass filter (sinc³ typically) averages thousands of bits per output sample, raising the effective resolution to 16–24 bits.

Mode-mismatched vs mode-matched gyros, ODR vs noise

Tunable trade between effective sample rate (ODR = output data rate, 12.5 Hz to 6.66 kHz on consumer parts) and noise. Decimation ratio decides: more averaging = lower noise but lower ODR. A typical IMU has a register for "BW filter" that picks the decimator response and is interpreted as a -3 dB cutoff.

Why this beats discrete

Integrated 3-axis-of-each MEMS shares the same crystal, same temperature, same calibration — making sensor fusion (Madgwick / Kalman) far more accurate than separate dies. The on-die DMP can even handle quaternion fusion without waking the main MCU. For a drone running at 8 kHz inner loop, ICM-42688's "low-noise mode" and synchronization signals are exactly what's needed.

What to notice

  • All four sensors are the same building block — proof mass, spring, comb-capacitor, Σ-Δ readout. The difference is what excites the mass (gravity / acceleration / Coriolis / pressure / sound).
  • Static displacement is tiny — at 1 g, a 1 µg/1 N·m⁻¹ mass moves 10 nm. That's how Σ-Δ + force-balance is essential.
  • Self-test is built in — a test bit drives a known electrostatic force to the proof mass and reads the response. Used at factory calibration and at runtime safety-critical checks (automotive ASIL).
  • Brownian noise sets the noise floor — finite-temperature random buffeting of the mass. Larger mass and stiffer spring help; both also hurt sensitivity. Always a tradeoff.
  • Frequency response is package-dependent — mics have an internal helmholtz resonance from the port hole + back volume; pressure sensors have viscous damping from venting; gyros need quadrature correction. Datasheet plots are mandatory reading.
  • Cross-axis sensitivity — typically < 2% for production parts, but matters in high-end applications (navigation gyros, geophysical sensors).
  • Vibration rectification — non-linear effects in the comb-finger sensor convert symmetric vibration into a DC offset. Mount IMUs on soft pads on a racing drone or your gyro drifts during throttle-up.

Where you'll see these sensors

Real uses

  • Phones, watches, AR/VR headsets — 6-DOF IMU (accel + gyro), mic, altimeter all MEMS.
  • Drones and robots — IMU is the inner loop's heart; without it you can't stabilize.
  • Automotive ABS/ESP, airbag — high-g accel for crash, gyro for ESC; ASIL-rated parts (Bosch SMI230 family).
  • Medical / hearing aids — tiny mic packages, blood-pressure cuffs, CGM patches with motion compensation.
  • Industrial CBM (Condition-Based Monitoring) — vibration-spectrum analysis on bearings, motors, compressors.
  • Smart home — flood sensors, glass-break detectors (high-frequency mic), bedside motion sensors.

SHORTCUTS

  • ε₀ = 8.854 pF/m; ε₀·h·L / g gives fF at MEMS scales.
  • 1 g on 1 µg with 1 N/m gives ~10 nm displacement.
  • Gyro drive at ~20 kHz, sense slightly different.
  • Σ-Δ + force balance keeps the mass essentially still → linear.
  • FIFO buffer in modern IMUs lets you batch reads and sleep the MCU.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • PCB stress — flexing the PCB applies stress through the package to the die, shifting offsets. Mount IMUs in the centroid of the board, away from connectors and large components.
  • Reflow stress — first power-on after reflow has a different offset than after a few hours of operation. Always re-tare after assembly.
  • Vibration rectification — high-frequency vibration becomes DC bias error. Soft-mount sensitive IMUs.
  • Forgetting temperature compensation — accelerometer ZGO drifts ~5–20 mg over -40 to +85 °C without compensation.
  • Treating gyro as absolute angle — gyro is rate; you must integrate, and integration accumulates drift. Always pair with accel + mag (sensor fusion).
