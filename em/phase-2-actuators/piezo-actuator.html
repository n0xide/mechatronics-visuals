Piezo Actuator — Volts to Nanometers

Crystals (lead-zirconate-titanate, PZT) that deform a few parts per thousand when you apply hundreds of volts. Stacks give big force, small stroke. Benders give big stroke, small force. Hysteresis and creep are real, but for nm-precision and µs-bandwidth nothing else competes.

Piezoelectric materials produce a mechanical strain proportional to applied electric field (the converse piezo effect) and produce charge when mechanically stressed (the direct piezo effect — used in microphones, force pickups, vibration sensors). The strain is small (~0.1 % at maximum drive) but the response is essentially instantaneous (limited by acoustic propagation in the crystal, microseconds for cm-scale parts) and the force at small displacements is enormous (thousands of N from a multi-layer stack). The two big design families: stack actuators (many thin layers in series electrically, sum of motions mechanically) for high-force, low-stroke nm-positioning, and bender actuators (bimorph cantilevers) for low-force, larger-stroke applications like fuel injectors, scanning-probe scanners, and tunable optics.

Piezoelectric coefficients d₃₃ and d₃₁

A piezo coefficient d_ij relates an applied field along direction j (in V/m) to a strain along direction i (m/m). The subscripts use IEEE notation: 1 = X, 2 = Y, 3 = Z (the polarization direction). d₃₃: voltage along Z produces strain along Z (the thickness mode — the wafer gets thicker). d₃₁: voltage along Z produces strain along X (the planar mode — the wafer gets wider). PZT-5H has d₃₃ ≈ 593 pC/N (or 593 pm/V — units happen to coincide) and d₃₁ ≈ −274 pm/V (negative: applying +Z field shrinks the X dimension).

Strain along axis i: ε_i = d_ij · E_j (E_j = V / thickness) Length change: ΔL = strain · L = d_ij · V · (L / t) For d₃₃ thickness mode (V across the thickness, motion along thickness): Δt = d₃₃ · V (independent of t — that's why we stack!) For d₃₁ planar mode (V across thickness, motion along length): ΔL = d₃₁ · V · (L / t) (the L/t lever)

Coercive field: exceeding ~ ±1 kV/mm (in soft PZT) can flip the domain polarization, permanently changing d₃₃ and d₃₁. Always keep |E| well below coercive, and never apply negative bias to monolithic multilayer stacks.

Multilayer stack — adding tiny motions for useful stroke

A single d₃₃ layer 100 µm thick at 100 V gives only 59 nm of motion. Useful for atomic-force microscopy, but not for fuel injectors or active vibration cancellation. The fix: stack N thin layers, all polarized so they expand in the same direction when driven by alternating polarity electrodes — total motion is N × per-layer motion, at the per-layer voltage. A modern multilayer co-fired stack has hundreds of 20–60 µm layers; 20 mm of stack at 150 V drive gives ~15 µm of motion and can push thousands of newtons.

Total stroke = N · d₃₃ · V_per_layer = (L_stack · d₃₃ · V) / t_layer.

Stroke-load tradeoff: a piezo stack has a force-vs-stroke triangle. At zero load → free stroke. At zero stroke → blocked force (= stiffness × free stroke). Anywhere in between, draw a straight line. To maximize useful work, operate at half the blocked force, giving half the free stroke.

Bimorph bender — leveraging d₃₁ for large stroke

Bond two thin piezo plates with opposing polarizations on top of each other. Apply voltage; one wants to grow, the other wants to shrink, and the assembly bends like a thermal bimetal. Free-tip deflection of a cantilevered bimorph is much larger than a stack's stroke — millimeters of motion are easy. The penalty: low force (typically newtons or less), and the motion is in a circular arc, not pure linear.

Hysteresis, creep, and how to deal with them

Open-loop piezo position is a lie. The real x-vs-V curve traces a hysteresis loop (~10–15 % of FS), and after settling, the position keeps drifting (creep) for seconds to minutes at a rate of ~1 % per decade of time. For sub-µm precision, this is fatal. Two solutions: closed-loop control with a strain gauge or capacitive position sensor, or charge drive instead of voltage (charge is much more linear with displacement than voltage). PI, Newport, and Cedrat all sell closed-loop versions with built-in capacitive sensors at typical price multipliers of 3–5× over open-loop.

  • Hysteresis area represents lost energy per cycle — heating! At high drive frequencies (resonance), self-heating can be the limiting factor (not amplifier or actuator damage).
  • Charge drive linearity is 100× better than voltage drive — the actuator's displacement IS proportional to net charge, not net voltage. But charge amplifiers are expensive and have DC drift; few commercial drivers offer them.
  • Closed-loop with a capacitive or strain sensor and a slow integral controller reduces hysteresis to < 0.1 % and eliminates creep. Bandwidth is usually 100 Hz – 1 kHz depending on resonance.
  • Feedforward hysteresis compensation (Preisach, Bouc-Wen models) is mid-tier — corrects 80 % of the hysteresis open-loop, useful when you can't add a position sensor.

Ultrasonic transducer drive — atomization, welding, NDT, imaging

Drive a piezo at its mechanical resonance and the displacement amplifies enormously (the Q is 100–10000), turning small electrical drive into large mechanical motion. Ultrasonic applications use this to do work: 40 kHz atomizing nozzles, 20 kHz plastic welders, 1–10 MHz medical imaging arrays, 50 kHz NDT transducers, 28 kHz piezo motors. The driver must track resonance over temperature and load (because the impedance changes), usually using a PLL that locks to the minimum-impedance point.

Worked example — fast-steering mirror for an EDM aiming system

Spec

Steer a 15 mm mirror ±1 mrad with 1 µrad resolution and 500 Hz bandwidth. Two-axis tip-tilt platform; total mirror mass + flexure ≈ 5 g.

Design

Mirror tip travel for ±1 mrad on 7.5 mm lever = ±7.5 µm PI P-721.10 piezo stack: 15 µm stroke at 100 V, 50 mm stack length → just enough — pick the 25 µm stroke version for margin (P-721.20) Three stacks in a flexure triangle do tip + tilt + Z (piston) Stiffness ~25 N/µm, blocked force ~600 N — overkill for 5 g mass Resonance with mass: f_res = (1/2π)√(k/m) = (1/2π)√(25e6/0.005) ≈ 11 kHz — way above 500 Hz BW target

Drive electronics

PI E-509.K3 closed-loop controller — strain-gauge feedback, 1.5 kHz BW Resolution: 0.2 nm RMS → 0.027 µrad on the mirror, beats 1 µrad target Total: ~$8k including 2-axis platform + driver. Compact PZT vs. ~$30k for voice-coil galvos at this precision

Why piezo over galvo

Galvo mirrors have higher stroke (±15° vs. ±0.06°) but with more inertia and lower precision. For sub-µrad tasks the piezo wins. The galvo wins for laser scanners that need to sweep degrees over a workpiece.

What to notice

  • d₃₃ doesn't depend on thickness — the per-layer stroke is set by the coefficient and the voltage, not the layer thickness. Thinner layers = more layers in the same length = more total stroke at the same voltage.
  • Stroke and force trade hyperbolically — at half stroke you can deliver half the blocked force. The actuator's "useful work" = ½·k·δ², maximized at half stroke.
  • Capacitance is large and shorts your driver — a 20 mm stack might be 1–10 µF. Driving 10 µF at 100 V at 1 kHz needs 6.3 A. The driver is usually the BOM cost-dominant part.
  • Hysteresis and creep ruin open-loop — for > 1 % accuracy use feedback. Period.
  • Bender = big stroke, low force; stack = big force, small stroke. Pick the morphology before tuning the formula.
  • Self-heating limits AC operation — at resonance, hysteresis losses dissipate 1–10 W of heat per cm³ of piezo. Liquid cooling for industrial-power ultrasonic horns.
  • Reverse bias kills monolithic stacks — soft PZT depoles below ~ -250 V on a +150 V part. Drive with strictly positive bias, or use a "bipolar-stable" formulation.

Where you'll see this circuit

Real uses

  • Diesel fuel injectors — Bosch CRI 3, Denso, Delphi — piezo stacks open the injector needle in < 100 µs, twice as fast as solenoid.
  • Scanning probe microscopy — AFM, STM tip scanners.
  • Adaptive optics — telescope deformable mirrors with hundreds of piezo actuators.
  • Semiconductor lithography — wafer-stage fine positioning, mask aligner.
  • Inkjet printers — piezo-droplet print heads (Epson, Brother) vs. thermal Canon/HP.
  • Buzzers and haptic clickers — cheap PZT discs in everyday gadgets.
  • Energy harvesting — vibration-to-electricity from bridge structures, shoe heels, factory machinery.
  • Ultrasonic motors — Nanomotion, Squiggle (New Scale), Canon USM autofocus lenses.

SHORTCUTS

  • PZT strain ~0.1 % at full drive — 100 mm long → 100 µm.
  • Single layer at 100 V ≈ 60 nm for soft PZT.
  • Multilayer 20 mm × 50 µm layers = 400 layers → ~24 µm at 100 V.
  • Stack stiffness ~30 N/µm; blocked force ~ kN range.
  • C ~ µF range for industrial stacks → big drive currents.
  • Hysteresis ~10–15 % open-loop; closed-loop < 0.1 %.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Negative bias on monolithic multilayer → depolarization → permanently degraded stroke.
  • Tension on a stack → cracking. Stacks live under preload, never in pure tension. Use spring loading.
  • Sharp voltage edges → ringing at acoustic resonance → wild overshoot. Always slew-rate-limit or apply S-curve.
  • Self-heating at AC drive → drift, depolarization, eventually failure. Limit duty cycle and add thermistor.
  • Forgetting to derate at high temp — d₃₃ rises 30 % from 25 °C to 100 °C in soft PZT, but Curie temperature (~190 °C) is a hard ceiling.
  • Treating the actuator as a pure capacitance for driver design — it's also a mass-spring-damper with sharp resonances at MHz. Driver stability needs to consider both.
