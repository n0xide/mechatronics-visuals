Pneumatics Basics

Compressed air pushes pistons. The math is simple — force = pressure × area — but the components have their own grammar of symbols. A 5/2 (five-port, two-position) valve switches a double-acting cylinder; flow valves control speed; the cylinder's bore sets the max force. This explorer covers the four parts of a basic pneumatic circuit: supply, valve, actuator, and flow control.

Force from pressure and area

Pressure exerts force on every surface it touches. In a cylinder, that surface is the piston face. Push pressure through the cylinder's port, the piston feels:

F = P · A where A is the effective piston area, in m²; P is gauge pressure, in Pa (or N/m²); F is force in N. For round pistons: A = π · (bore/2)² Useful mixed-unit version: F (lbf) = P (psi) · A (in²) (exact, since psi = lbf/in²) And: F (N) = P (bar) · A (cm²) · 10 (exact, since 1 bar·cm² = 10 N) Conversions: 1 bar = 14.5 psi = 100 kPa (1 bar = 10⁵ Pa exactly). Typical shop air is 6–7 bar (87–100 psi); industrial systems run 4–10 bar.

The 5/2 directional valve

The most common pneumatic valve. "5/2" means five ports, two positions. The five ports:

PortFunctionDescription
1 (P)Pressure inCompressed air supply
2 (A)Output ATo cylinder cap end (push side)
4 (B)Output BTo cylinder rod end (pull side)
3 (R)Exhaust AVents air from port 2 to atmosphere
5 (S)Exhaust BVents air from port 4 to atmosphere

In position 1, P → A and B → R. Cylinder extends. In position 2, P → B and A → R. Cylinder retracts. The valve is actuated by a solenoid (electrical), pilot air (pneumatic), lever (manual), or roller (mechanical limit-switch).

A 5/3 valve adds a center "neutral" position. Three common 5/3 center types: all ports closed (cylinder locks in place), A & B vented (cylinder floats), P pressurized to both (cylinder rigid). Each suits a different safety story.

Single-acting vs double-acting

A double-acting cylinder has two ports (cap and rod end). Air drives both directions. Faster, stronger, no spring fatigue — the workhorse.

A single-acting cylinder has one port (cap end) and a spring inside that returns the piston when air is exhausted. Half the air consumption, simpler valving (3/2 instead of 5/2), and a built-in safe state (spring returns to rest if power dies). But: the spring eats some of the extend force, and the spring's force is usually small (10–30 N), so retract is weak. Used for press-and-release: clamps, indexing fingers, ejector pins.

Speed control — flow valves

Pressure determines force; flow rate determines speed. A flow control valve is a needle valve (variable orifice) bypassed by a check valve, so it restricts flow in one direction only:

  • Meter-out (most common) — flow valve restricts exhaust. The cylinder always faces the full pressure on the drive side; exhaust back-pressure limits speed. Smooth motion, resistant to load reversal.
  • Meter-in — flow valve restricts supply. Cheaper to plumb but jerky under varying load — when the load drops, the cylinder accelerates.
  • Common practice: meter-out on both directions of a double-acting cylinder. Two flow controls, one per port.

Cylinder speed is set by the flow valve's orifice area, supply pressure, and the volume the piston has to displace each stroke. For a 25 mm bore × 100 mm stroke at 100 L/min flow: 49.1 mL stroke ÷ 1667 mL/s = 30 ms. Real cylinders are slower because the regulator and tubing throttle the actual flow.

Worked example — 0.5 kg pick-and-place gripper

The requirement

End effector grips a 0.5 kg part with two jaws. Each jaw needs 50 N clamp force (target ~10× the part's ~5 N weight for a secure friction grip). Shop air at 6 bar. Cycle time target: 0.5 s open + 0.5 s close.

Cylinder sizing

F_target = 50 N (per jaw) (double-acting cylinders inside the gripper, one per jaw) A_push = F / P = 50 N / 600 kPa = 83.3 mm² (SI: 600 kPa = 6×10⁵ Pa = 0.6 N/mm²; 50/0.6 = 83.3 mm²) Bore: √(4 · 83.3 / π) = 10.3 mm → round to 12 mm standard bore (next ISO size up). Re-check: A = π · 6² = 113 mm² → F_actual = 6e5 · 1.13e-4 = 67.8 N (35 % margin)

Stroke and volume

Stroke = 20 mm (jaw open ↔ closed) (per-jaw gripper geometry) Volume per stroke = 113 mm² · 20 mm = 2.26 mL Per cycle (extend + retract): ~4.5 mL = 0.0045 L If gripper cycles at 2 Hz: 0.0045 L · 2 = 0.009 L/s = 0.54 L/min (per jaw, both jaws = 1.08 L/min total)

Valve choice

5/2 single-solenoid spring-return SMC SY3120 or Festo CPE10. ~10 ms response, 200 L/min flow rating — abundant for our 1 L/min demand.

Speed control

Two flow valves (one per direction) inline with the cylinder ports. Adjust until visible bounce stops, then trim. Typical setting: 50–80 % open.

Air budget

Each 5 mL stroke at 6 bar = 5 mL · (1 + 6) = 35 mL free air equivalent (atmospheric volume vented to drive the cylinder). 35 mL/cycle · 2 cycles/s · 2 jaws = 140 mL/s = 8.4 L/min — tiny load on a typical 50 L/min shop compressor.

What to notice

  • Force is set by pressure and bore, period. Stroke doesn't change force. Doubling the bore quadruples the force (area scales as r²).
  • Pull is always weaker than push. The rod reduces the rod-side effective area. Designers compensate by sizing the cylinder for the worst direction.
  • Air is "free" but the compressor isn't. Energy efficiency of pneumatics is poor (10–25 % overall). Use them where speed-to-cost and binary actuation matter — gripping, clamping, indexing — not for continuous motion.
  • Pneumatics fail soft. Cut power, lose air, cylinder usually goes to a known state. Compare to electric servos that hold position electromagnetically — when their power dies, they coast.
  • Speed control is about volume flow, not pressure. The flow valve doesn't change force; it throttles fill/exhaust rate. The cylinder moves slower but with the same pressure-driven force.

Where you'll see this

Real uses

  • Automation assembly lines — pick-and-place, parts feeders, clamps, indexers. Pneumatics dominate because cycle time is sub-second and motion is between two fixed positions.
  • Robot grippers — pneumatic 2-jaw and 3-finger grippers are the cheapest option for warehouse robots (Soft Robotics, Schunk MPG).
  • Door openers — bus doors, train doors. Choice is rugged and well-understood.
  • Press brakes and stamping — pneumatic for light work, hydraulic for heavy.
  • 3D-printer build-plate ejectors — some prototypes use a small single-acting cylinder to pop the printed part free after cooling.

RULES OF THUMB

  • Shop air ≈ 6–7 bar (87–100 psi); industrial 4–10 bar.
  • F (lbf) ≈ P (psi) · A (in²) — handy mixed-unit form.
  • F (N) ≈ P (bar) · A (cm²) · 10.
  • Meter-out on both directions = smooth, predictable.
  • Sizing margin: 2× expected force. Pneumatics are cheap to upsize.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Forgetting rod area — pull force is always less than push.
  • Sizing for theoretical force — real friction and seal drag eat 5–15 %.
  • Cycle time vs flow — small valve, big cylinder = slow stroke.
  • No regulator — line pressure varies with compressor cycling; output force drifts.
  • Skipping the FRL (filter-regulator-lubricator) — debris kills valves, no oil shortens cylinder life.
