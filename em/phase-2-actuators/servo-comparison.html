Servos — Hobby, Closed-Loop DC, Industrial AC

"Servo" means "closed-loop position controller in a box." The box might cost $4 or $4,000. Three tiers, three internal architectures, three pricing realities.

A servo bundles a motor, a feedback sensor, and a position controller into one package. You command a position; it goes there and holds. The differences between tiers are how good the controller is, how good the sensor is, and how big the motor is. A hobby servo uses a brushed DC motor, a potentiometer, and an analog (or low-cost digital) error amplifier — input is a 50 Hz PWM pulse, 1.0–2.0 ms wide, encoded as position 0–180°. A closed-loop DC/BLDC servo uses an encoder, a microcontroller running PID, and an H-bridge or 3-phase driver — input is step/dir, CAN, or RS-485. An industrial AC servo uses a high-pole BLDC (called "PMSM" or "AC servo motor"), a 20-bit absolute encoder, a sophisticated FOC drive with current/velocity/position cascaded loops at 10–40 kHz — input is EtherCAT, PROFINET, or pulse-train. Tutorials say "servo" and people picture all three.

Internal block diagrams

Hobby servo (~$5–30)

Analog error amp drives brushed DC motor through H-bridge. Pot inside gives position back. Cheap, slow, hold-torque drops under load.

Closed-loop DC servo (~$50–500)

MCU runs digital PID. Encoder feedback. Multiple control modes (position / velocity / current). Bus comm. e.g. Dynamixel, Hiwonder LX series.

Industrial AC servo (~$500–$5k)

Cascaded loops, FOC current control, absolute encoder (knows position at power-on), brake, regen resistor. Bandwidth and stiffness orders of magnitude beyond hobby.

What to notice

  • "Servo" without context is meaningless — always ask which tier. A robotics engineer saying "servo" usually means industrial AC; a hobbyist means MG996R-style PWM; a maker robot like Hiwonder Spiderbot uses bus-controlled DC servos.
  • PWM convention is brittle — 1.5 ms is "supposed" to be center but actual range varies ±10% per brand. Calibrate per servo if precision matters. Continuous-rotation servos repurpose the same PWM signal as speed, not position (1.5 ms = stop).
  • Hobby servo bandwidth ~3–10 Hz. Industrial AC reaches 500–2000 Hz. This is why your hobby servo "feels squishy" under disturbance: the controller can't react in time.
  • Holding torque ≠ moving torque. Hobby servo specs almost always cite stall (locked rotor with full current); actual continuous holding is 30–60% of that before overheating. Plan accordingly.
  • Bus servos solve "many servos one wire" — the daisy-chain protocol (TTL or RS-485) means a 24-DOF spider needs one signal wire, not 24. Each servo has an address.
  • Industrial servos need an absolute encoder for safety. After power-cycle, a 20-bit absolute encoder knows where it is without homing. Hobby + many DC servos use incremental encoders and must home to a switch at startup.

SELECTION RULES

  • Toy/RC → hobby PWM servo.
  • Robot joint, < 5 kg payload, hobby budget → bus servo (Dynamixel/Hiwonder).
  • Industrial pick & place, repeatability < 0.01° → AC servo.
  • Continuous shaft rotation → not a servo; use BLDC/DC + ESC.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • "Just buy a bigger servo" — stiffness comes from bandwidth, not torque.
  • Brownouts — hobby servos draw 1–3 A peak; a 4-servo arm needs a beefier supply than you think.
  • Powering servo from MCU 5V — always separate the servo rail, common ground only.
  • Incremental encoder homing — forgetting the home-switch step means random startup pose.
