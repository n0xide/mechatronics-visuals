Solenoids & Relays — Inductive Kickback and the Flyback Diode

A coil is a magnetic energy bank. Switch it off and that energy must go somewhere — through your transistor, in a voltage spike that fries it, unless you give it a path.

A solenoid is just a coil of wire that, when energized, pulls a ferromagnetic plunger into the field. A relay is the same coil, but the plunger flips an electrical contact instead of doing mechanical work. Both store energy in their inductance: E = ½·L·I². When you open the switch driving the coil, the coil tries to maintain its current — and since I = dQ/dt, the only way to do that against an open circuit is to develop whatever voltage is needed: V = −L·dI/dt. With dI/dt going from amps to zero in microseconds and L in millihenries, the spike easily exceeds 100 V on a 12 V system. That spike arcs across switch contacts, burns transistors, and resets nearby MCUs. The fix is a flyback diode across the coil: it gives the inductor current a path to circulate while the magnetic energy decays as I²R heat in the coil itself.

Drive circuit — transistor + coil + (optional) flyback diode

Low-side switching: MOSFET drain is between coil and ground. When MOSFET turns off, drain voltage tries to fly up to whatever is needed to maintain coil current. Without a diode, it spikes hundreds of volts. With the diode (cathode at V+, anode at drain), current freewheels through the diode + coil + V+ until the magnetic energy is dissipated as I²R in the coil.

What to notice

  • Energy storage is the problem — at steady state, the coil is just a resistor with I = V/R. The danger comes during turn-off, when the field collapses and dumps ½·L·I² into whatever path it can find.
  • Without a flyback diode, the drain voltage spikes to whatever it takes to maintain current. In practice this is limited by the MOSFET's avalanche rating (often 60–100 V for low-V parts), but each avalanche event degrades the part. The fix costs three cents.
  • Diode polarity matters. Cathode (banded end) goes to V+, anode to the drain. Reverse it and you've created a short circuit through the diode when the transistor is on — which destroys the diode and then the transistor.
  • Diode + zener for fast turn-off. A plain flyback diode clamps the drain at V+ + 0.7 V, which means the coil current decays slowly (5τ = 5L/R). For fast-cycling solenoids, add a zener in series with the diode (clamp at e.g. 36 V): higher decay voltage = faster current decay.
  • Solenoid duty cycle matters. Peak current can be much higher than continuous (PWM-modulated for fast pull-in, then reduced for hold). This is how injectors and many industrial valves work.
  • Relays vs MOSFETs — relays handle AC and high voltage easily, isolate the control side, but switch in milliseconds and wear out after 100k cycles. MOSFETs switch in microseconds, last forever, but need careful driver design.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

E = ½·L·I²
Time constant
τ = L / R
Current rise
I(t) = (V/R)(1 − e^(−t/τ))
Spike voltage
V_L = −L · dI/dt
Solenoid force
F ∝ I² / gap²

MEMORIZE

  • Flyback diode rule — every inductive load gets a diode. No exceptions.
  • Diode orientation — cathode (band) to V+.
  • Diode rating — I_avg ≥ I_coil_max, V_R ≥ 2·V+.
  • 1N4007 or 1N5819 — covers 90% of hobby cases.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • "My MCU keeps resetting" — usually no flyback diode on a relay or solenoid.
  • Reversed diode polarity — short circuit when transistor turns on.
  • Driving solenoid with MCU pin directly — MCU pin can't sink the current; use a MOSFET or driver IC.
  • Relay coil shared with logic supply — voltage dip = MCU reset. Separate supply or large bulk cap.
