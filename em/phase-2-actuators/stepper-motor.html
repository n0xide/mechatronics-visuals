Stepper Motor — Coil Energization, Torque vs Speed, Skipped Steps

A stepper is just a salient-pole synchronous motor driven open-loop. Energize coils in sequence, the rotor snaps to align — full-step, half-step, microstep all change only what currents you push, not the motor.

A bipolar 2-phase stepper has two coils, A and B, each capable of being driven positive or negative through an H-bridge. Four discrete combinations of polarity give four full steps per electrical cycle. Energize both coils equally and at full current and the rotor sees a torque vector pointing along the resultant magnetic field. By scaling I_A and I_B as cos(θ) and sin(θ) you can place the field at any angle — that's microstepping. The rotor follows as long as the field rotates slower than the rotor's pull-out torque allows; outrun it and the rotor skips steps, silently desyncing your open-loop position.

Full-step: rotor sits aligned with the field (4 discrete positions per electrical cycle). Half-step: 8 positions. Microstep 1/16: 64. Higher microstepping means smoother motion and less audible noise, but each microstep carries less than full holding torque (~70% at 1/16, falling further with finer divisions).

What to notice

  • Microstepping doesn't add new positions — it adds intermediate ones. The motor still has 200 (or 400) detents; microstepping uses current-shaping to hold the rotor between them. Position accuracy at fine microsteps is dominated by detent torque error, not driver resolution.
  • Torque collapses with step rate — inductance L means the coil takes time τ = L/R to charge. Past the L/R corner, current never reaches I_max before you commutate to the next step, so torque drops. Higher V helps; this is why stepper drivers want 24–48 V even though motor "rated voltage" might be 2.8 V.
  • Skipped step = silent failure. Open-loop steppers have no encoder, so missed steps are invisible until your part is in the wrong place. Mitigations: oversizing (run at 50% of pull-out), acceleration ramps, StallGuard / closed-loop steppers.
  • Resonance dip around 100–300 steps/s on real motors. Microstepping flattens it considerably; full-step at resonance can outright stall.
  • Holding ≠ running torque. Datasheet quotes holding torque (static, both coils at I_max). Running torque is always lower; pull-out curves are the spec you actually want.
  • Heat is constant, not duty-cycled. A stepper draws full current even at zero speed (it's holding). Plan thermals for continuous I²R, not for whatever average current you imagine.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

Step angle
θ_full = 360° / N_stepsN=200 for 1.8°
Microstep
θ_µ = θ_full / MM = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 ...
Coil current
I_A = I_max·cos(θ), I_B = I_max·sin(θ)
L/R corner
f_c ≈ V / (2π·L·I_max)
Pull-out
T(f) ≈ T_hold / √(1 + (f/f_c)²)

MEMORIZE

  • 1.8°/step — default NEMA17. 200 full steps/rev.
  • 0.9°/step — high-res NEMA17. 400 full steps/rev.
  • Microstep ratios — 8, 16, 32, 256 (TMC silent-step).
  • Holding torque drops to ~50% by 1000 sps for typical NEMA17.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Driving at "rated voltage" — that spec is for DC. Use 5–20× rated, current-limited.
  • No acceleration ramp — commanding max rate from rest = guaranteed skip.
  • Microstepping for accuracy — buys smoothness, not absolute position.
  • Forgetting holding heat — idle current = full current; add heatsink or lower hold current.
