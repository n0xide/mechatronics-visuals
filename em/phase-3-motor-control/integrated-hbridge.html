Integrated H-Bridge Drivers

DRV8833, DRV8871, DRV8251A — three TI parts that span hobby motors, light industrial drives, and current-limited closed-loop applications. Each integrates 4 MOSFETs, a charge pump for the high side, current limiting via sense resistor, slow/fast decay control, thermal shutdown, and shoot-through-prevention blanking. This visual maps the internal block diagram to the datasheet pins and walks the current-limit, decay, and protection knobs.

Integrated vs discrete H-bridge

You can build an H-bridge with four discrete MOSFETs, four gate drivers, two flyback diodes, a sense resistor, comparator, and dead-time generator. You'd end up with ~15 ICs and 30 passives for what an integrated driver does in 1 chip and ~3 passives. Integrated drivers exist because the engineering of high-side gate drive, shoot-through prevention, and current limiting are tedious enough that a fixed-function silicon implementation wins on almost every axis.

Internal block diagram

Inside every integrated H-bridge driver:

  • 4 power MOSFETs arranged as two half-bridges (HS-A + LS-A on OUT_A, HS-B + LS-B on OUT_B). Modern parts use NMOS for both high and low sides; PMOS for high-side appears in older / low-voltage parts.
  • Charge pump generating a voltage above VM to drive the high-side NMOS gates. Without it, an N-channel high-side FET can't be turned fully on. Typical charge pump: 2–3 stage doubler with on-chip capacitor (DRV8833) or external bootstrap (DRV8871 uses BST pin).
  • Gate driver per FET with shoot-through-prevention deadtime (typically 100-500 ns blanking between half-bridge complement edges).
  • Current sense via external R_sense in the low-side return path (DRV8833, DRV8251A) or via integrated mirror sensing (DRV8871).
  • Reference + comparator for current trip. When I_motor × R_sense ≥ V_ref, the comparator trips and the driver enters current-limit mode for one PWM cycle.
  • Logic interface — IN1 / IN2 (PWM control) and sometimes nSLEEP (low-power) or EN (enable).
  • Protection block — thermal shutdown (TSD), over-current (OCP), under-voltage lockout (UVLO).

Current limit — R_sense sets the trip point

Concept: driver continuously monitors voltage across R_sense → I × R_sense. When V_IPROPI/SENSE ≥ V_ITRIP (internal reference, typically 0.2 V on DRV8833 / DRV8871, ~0.6 V at I_TRIP pin on DRV8251A): → comparator trips → driver enters fixed off-time chopping (defaults to ~30 µs on DRV8833 / 25 µs on DRV8871). Result: motor current chops at I_trip regardless of motor / supply combination. Formula: I_trip = V_ref / R_sense

R_sense sizing for DRV8833: V_ITRIP = 0.2 V. For 1.0 A trip: R_sense = 0.2 / 1.0 = 0.2 Ω (200 mΩ). For 1.5 A trip (the chip's max): R_sense = 0.13 Ω. For lower currents (delicate motors): increase R_sense. Power dissipation in R_sense: P = I² × R = 1² × 0.2 = 0.2 W at 1 A continuous (peak/RMS depends on duty — size for worst case, not for average). Sense resistors must be rated for this; use 1/4 W or higher and add headroom for chopping spikes.

DRV8871 IPROPI: uses an internal 200 mV reference and an external resistor on the IPROPI pin to scale. DRV8251A uses the IPROPI pin to set the current limit by external resistor to AGND; current limit is dialed in by a single resistor and the chip handles the sensing internally.

Slow vs fast decay — what happens when PWM is OFF

During a PWM cycle, the FETs are alternately conducting (current rises) and off (current decays). What "off" means depends on which FETs you turn on during the off period — that's the decay mode.

ModeState during off-timeDecay rateBest for
Slow decayBoth low-side FETs on (or both high-side) → motor shorted across same rail. Current recirculates through the motor.Slow — only loss is winding RSmooth low-speed, lower torque ripple, lower noise. Default for hobby motors.
Fast decayBoth diagonal FETs swap → motor connected reverse-polarity. Current drives back through the supply.Fast — V drops to opposite railActive braking, fast direction reversal, precise current control (low motor inductance).
Mixed decayFast for first portion of off-time, slow for rest. Programmable ratio (DRV8825 stepper).AdjustableMicrostepping steppers, balancing speed vs ripple.

For a DC motor at constant speed, slow decay minimizes torque ripple and acoustic noise. For a stepper microstepping at high speed, fast decay (or mixed) prevents current overshoot at low-PWM points in the sine wave. For active braking, fast decay dumps motor kinetic energy back to the supply through the H-bridge.

Charge pump for high-side NMOS gate

An NMOS source-grounded low-side FET turns on when its gate is at +10 V (with respect to its grounded source). An NMOS high-side FET — source connected to the load, which goes to ground — has its source jumping up to VM when conducting. Its gate needs to be at VM + V_gs, which is higher than the supply. The charge pump generates this gate voltage on-chip.

1. An oscillator drives a flying capacitor (or several in cascade). The cap charges to VM while one side is connected to ground. 2. Switching network swaps the cap so its bottom plate is now at VM and the top plate is at VM + V_charged ≈ 2·VM. 3. A buffer capacitor on the boosted node holds this voltage for the gate driver to use. 4. A linear regulator clips it to V_gs_max (typically 10–15 V above VM). Result: high-side NMOS gate sees +10 V relative to its source, regardless of supply voltage.

For DRV8833, the charge pump is internal (no external components needed). For DRV8871, the BST (bootstrap) pin needs an external 10 nF cap to OUT for the high-side gate supply. Bootstrap differs from charge pump: it relies on the low-side FET being on periodically to recharge the bootstrap cap from VM via a diode. If the high-side is on continuously (100 % duty), bootstrap fails — the bootstrap cap discharges through gate leakage. DRV8871's reference design specifies max ~98 % duty cycle for this reason.

Blanking time — preventing false current-limit trips

When a FET turns on, there's a brief current spike (the motor's reverse-recovery in the freewheeling diode, parasitic capacitance, etc). If the current-limit comparator was always active, this spike would falsely trigger the trip — every PWM cycle. Drivers solve this by blanking the current-sense for a short time after each switching edge.

DriverBlanking timeOff-time after trip
DRV88331.6 µs (fixed)30 µs typical (sets PWM chopping period)
DRV88711.5 µsExternal via IN1/IN2 (manual PWM only)
DRV8251A1.6 µs40 µs (sets internal chopping rate)

DRV8825 (stepper)1 µs30 µs

If the motor has very high inductance (large coils), the rise time of the current spike is slow and blanking is irrelevant. If the motor has very low inductance (small high-RPM motors), the current can rise from 0 to trip in less than the blanking time — the chip will under-regulate, and you get higher than expected peak current. Solution: lower R_sense slightly to give a margin, or pick a driver with adjustable blanking.

Worked example — DRV8833 driving a 12 V, 1 A geared motor

The setup

arm-6dof-so100-mega prototype: 12 V geared DC motor for the gripper actuator. Continuous current 0.6 A, stall ~1.5 A. Need direction control and PWM speed control. Driver: DRV8833 (one of the dual H-bridges).

Compute R_sense

V_ref = 0.2 V (DRV8833 internal) Target current limit at stall = 1.2 A (slight margin below abs-max) R_sense = V_ref / I = 0.2 / 1.2 = 167 mΩ Closest E12: 180 mΩ → I_trip = 0.2 / 0.18 = 1.11 A. Use 1206 or 2010 thick-film resistor, 1 W minimum. Sense-resistor power at trip: P = 1.11² × 0.18 = 0.22 W (continuous in current-limit mode)

PWM frequency

Pick 20 kHz — above human audible range, below the Si-MOSFET switching-loss knee Period = 50 µs, 8-bit duty cycle = 200 ns resolution. Plenty. DRV8833 max PWM ≈ 250 kHz — limit is FET switching loss + body diode reverse-recovery.

Decay choice

Slow decay during constant-speed running → smooth, low ripple Fast decay during direction reversal → quick brake before reverse drive DRV8833 selects via IN1/IN2 pin combination per truth table: IN1=HIGH, IN2=PWM (toggles between 10=forward and 11=brake) → forward, slow decay — during PWM off both low-sides ON IN1=PWM, IN2=LOW (toggles between 10=forward and 00=coast) → forward, fast decay — during PWM off body diodes freewheel current back to V_M

Protection

Internal: TSD at T_j ≈ 150 °C, OCP at ~2 A (chip abs-max) External: 100 nF + 10 µF decoupling on VM. Schottky between OUT and GND optional (body diodes integral).

For PCB layout: keep R_sense within 10 mm of the SENSE pin to avoid trace-resistance error. Star-ground R_sense return to the chip's PGND (not analog GND). The current loop (VM → HS → motor → LS → R_sense → GND) should be a small loop area to minimize EMI radiation.

What to notice

  • R_sense sets the current trip — don't omit it on parts that need it. DRV8833 with no R_sense (sense pin floating) trips at random; DRV8871 with no I_PROPI resistor never current-limits. Either way, the motor sees whatever current the supply can deliver.
  • Slow decay = quieter, fast decay = faster response. For most DC motor applications at constant speed, slow decay. For steppers, mixed. For braking, fast.
  • Charge pump is what makes integrated H-bridges possible. Without it, you'd need PMOS high-sides (lower performance) or external bootstrap (more components). Modern parts use all-NMOS + integrated charge pump.
  • Blanking time matters at high PWM frequency. If your PWM period approaches the blanking time (e.g., 200 kHz PWM = 5 µs period vs 1.6 µs blanking), you've effectively disabled current limiting. Slow down or pick a different driver.
  • Thermal shutdown is your friend. When the chip gets too hot, it shuts off the H-bridge — preventing destruction. Recover by waiting (~3 °C hysteresis). If you trip TSD regularly, add a heatsink or pick a higher-current driver.
  • Body diodes carry recirculation current. When a FET turns off during slow/fast decay, the body diode of the complementary FET conducts. This is silicon body diode (~0.7 V V_f), not a Schottky — high V_f means power dissipation. Modern drivers turn the complementary FET on (synchronous rectification) to bypass the body diode and cut dissipation by ~10×.
  • Layout matters at high current. The current loop (VM → HS → motor → LS → R_sense → GND) creates EMI; keep it small. R_sense to chip should be a few mm at most.

Where you'll see this

Real uses

  • Hobby robotics — DRV8833 is the canonical drop-in driver for Arduino / RP2040 small-motor builds.
  • 3D printer extruder motors — A4988 / DRV8825 / TMC2208 are stepper variants of the same architecture.
  • RC car ESCs — single-direction PWM = half-bridge; bidirectional = H-bridge integrated.
  • Servo motors (continuous-rotation) — internal H-bridge driving small DC motor + feedback potentiometer.
  • Stepper motor microstepping — two H-bridges per stepper (one per phase), sinusoidal PWM with mixed decay.
  • Coreless cube motors / vibration motors — small enough that current trip is set to ~50 mA, R_sense huge.
  • Pi-Flux underwater motor — DRV8871 + magnetic angle sensor for closed-loop direction control.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

  • I_trip = V_ref / R_sense
  • P_R_sense = I² × R_sense (continuous in chop)
  • P_FET ≈ I² × R_DS(on) + 0.5 × V_DS × I × (t_r+t_f) × f_sw (cond + switching)
  • V_charge_pump ≈ VM + 10 V (regulated to V_gs target, ~10 V above source)
  • Blanking ≈ 1–2 µs after each switching edge
  • Chopping period ≈ 25–50 µs when in current-limit

BY MOTOR CURRENT

  • < 0.5 A → DRV8833 / L9110
  • 0.5–1.5 A → DRV8833, TB6612FNG, DRV8835
  • 1.5–3.5 A → DRV8871, BTS7960
  • 3.5–6 A → DRV8251A, DRV8244
  • 6–10 A → BTN8982TA, IFX007T
  • > 10 A → discrete FETs + DRV8316 / L6234 gate driver

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Forgetting R_sense → no current limit → magic smoke at stall.
  • R_sense too small → trip never fires; motor sees full supply current.
  • Trace resistance in R_sense return → measured V_sense includes trace IR; trips early.
  • PWM faster than chopping period → current limit doesn't engage cleanly.
  • No flyback for inductive load → most chips have integrated body diodes that do this, but verify on datasheet.
  • VM decoupling too small → ringing on switching → false OCP trips.
  • Choosing wrong decay mode for application → unwanted noise / torque ripple.
  • Ignoring T_j budget → thermal shutdown trips repeatedly during normal operation.
