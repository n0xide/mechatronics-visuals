Integrator Anti-Windup — when actuator saturation breaks your PID

Every textbook PID assumes the actuator can produce whatever the controller demands. Real motors clip at their torque limit; real valves close at 0% and 100%; real DACs are bounded. When the integrator demands more than the actuator can deliver, error stays nonzero and the integral term winds up to enormous values. By the time the error finally reverses, the integrator is bloated and takes a long arc to wind back — producing massive overshoot. This visual simulates the problem live and compares three fixes: do-nothing, clamping, and back-calculation tracking.

The math behind the three strategies

PID: u_pid = K_p·e + K_i·∫e dt + K_d·de/dt Actuator: u_actual = sat(u_pid, ±u_max) Plant: τ_p · dy/dt = u_actual Strategy 1 — None (raw PID): Always integrate: dI/dt = K_i · e Even when u_pid is saturated and the actuator can't act on the extra demand. → I grows unbounded; large overshoot when error finally reverses. Strategy 2 — Clamping (conditional integration): dI/dt = K_i · e only when NOT(saturated AND e has same sign as sat direction) i.e. if u_pid > u_max and e > 0: freeze I (likewise for negative side) Simple, no extra parameter, works well for most cases. Edge case: I can be stuck high if error never reverses (e.g., constant load). Strategy 3 — Back-calculation (tracking, "Astrom-Hagglund"): dI/dt = K_i · e + (u_actual − u_pid) / T_t The second term is zero when not saturated (u_actual = u_pid). When saturated, it pulls I toward the value that would give u_pid = u_actual exactly. T_t = tracking time constant; smaller T_t = faster wind-down. Typical: T_t ≈ √(T_i · T_d) for ZN-tuned PID, or 0.1·T_i to 1·T_i. Equivalent block diagram: I-state is fed back with gain 1/T_t from the saturation block.

Why windup matters in real systems

  • Motor torque saturation — a position controller demands more torque than the motor can deliver during a fast setpoint step. Without anti-windup: 30-50% overshoot. With BC: smooth.
  • Valve fully open / closed — flow controller hits the mechanical end-stop. Integrator continues climbing for minutes; when load finally drops, valve closes far past setpoint and oscillates.
  • Heater duty-cycle limited — temperature controller wants 150% PWM. Saturated at 100%, error stays huge, integrator builds. When target reached, heater stays on for too long → overshoot.
  • Battery current limit — EV speed controller; high acceleration request hits the current limit. Integrator winds, then huge regen demand when target reached.
  • Aircraft control surfaces — historical accidents (the 1993 JAS 39 Gripen crash had elevator-channel windup as one contributing factor). Modern fly-by-wire all uses BC tracking.

Tuning T_t — practical guidance

The tracking time constant T_t determines how fast the integrator unwinds after saturation. Astrom-Hagglund's classic recipe:

T_t = √(T_i · T_d) for PID T_t = T_i for PI (no derivative) Where T_i = K_p / K_i, T_d = K_d / K_p (in standard form)

Smaller T_t → faster unwind, but too small causes oscillation in the I-loop. Practical range: 0.1·T_i to T_i. If your system overshoots only modestly (5-10%), increase T_t. If it lingers in saturation, decrease.

Rule of thumb: start with T_t = 0.5·T_i and tune ±50% by step-response.

Worked example — robotic-arm joint position controller

Setup

Arm joint (arm-6dof-pa612 design): 100 N·m peak torque, 50 N·m continuous. Joint inertia 2 kg·m². PID position loop with K_p = 30, K_i = 8, K_d = 4. Commanded step: 90° in 0.5 s.

Without anti-windup

Required peak torque (no limit) ≈ K_p·90° = 47 N·m (just under continuous) With dynamics + payload: demand briefly exceeds 100 N·m peak → saturation During 200 ms saturation, ∫e ~ 90·0.2 = 18 → K_i·∫e = 144 N·m of pent-up integral After target reached, integrator drives motor backwards through equilibrium → 14° overshoot, 1.2 s settle

With back-calculation (T_t = 0.5 s)

During saturation: dI/dt = K_i·e + (u_sat − u_pid)/T_t ≈ 8·e − 280/0.5 (huge negative) I quickly stabilizes at value that would give u_pid = 100 N·m exactly — no excess buildup Result: 2° overshoot, 0.6 s settle. Same K_p / K_i, no other change.

Common pitfalls

  • Saturating without anti-windup, ever — the textbook setpoint-tracking simulations never saturate, so the textbook PID always "works." Real systems saturate within the first second of operation.
  • Forgetting the direction of saturation — clamping at +u_max should freeze I only when error is also positive. Negative error wants the actuator down, so I should still update. A naive "if saturated, freeze" deadlocks.
  • T_t too small — back-calculation with T_t → 0 creates a tight inner loop that can oscillate around u_sat. Increase T_t if you see chatter at the limit.
  • Anti-windup on a non-PI controller — pure P controllers don't need it (no integrator). LQR / state-feedback can need similar care if they have integral action.
  • Setpoint slew-rate limiting hides the issue — pre-filtering the setpoint to be reachable (slew-rate limit) avoids saturation, but if the disturbance comes from the load side, you still need anti-windup.
  • Reset windup vs derivative kick — different problems. Derivative kick is solved by computing dy/dt instead of de/dt (the "PI-D" form). Anti-windup is independent.
  • Bumpless transfer — switching from manual to auto mode with a non-zero I-term creates a bump. Initialize I from current u_manual: I_init = u_manual − K_p·e_init.
  • Cascaded loops — inner-loop (current) and outer-loop (velocity) both have integrators. Both need anti-windup, but the inner's saturation feeds back to the outer's setpoint, so apply tracking on the outer using inner's saturation state.
