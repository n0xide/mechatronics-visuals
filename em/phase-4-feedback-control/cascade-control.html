Cascade control — nested loops

A single PID controlling a complex plant has to be tuned for the worst-case bandwidth. Cascading two (or three) PIDs — an inner fast loop and an outer slow loop — separates concerns and rejects disturbances closer to where they enter. Classic example: a brushless motor controlled with a current loop (~10 kHz) inside a velocity loop (~1 kHz) inside a position loop (~100 Hz).

Three-loop cascade for FOC arm joint

Each inner loop is the actuator of its outer loop. The outer loop trusts that the inner already converged before its next sample. Rule of thumb: inner bandwidth ≥ 5–10× outer.

What to notice

  • Inner ≥ 5× outer is the practical rule. Drag the ratio slider below ~3 and the cascade response starts oscillating because the outer loop is faster than the inner can keep up — the inner loop becomes a delay element to the outer.
  • Disturbance rejection is the cascade win. A load torque is rejected by the velocity loop before it can affect position much. Watch the orange (single-loop) trace recover slowly while the blue (cascade) barely flinches.
  • Each loop has its own PID with its own gains. Tune from the inside out: inner first (with outer in manual or open), then middle, then outer. Once an inner loop is tuned, you don't retune it when adding the outer.
  • The outer loop "sees" the inner closed-loop transfer function as its plant. If the inner is well-tuned (high bandwidth, low overshoot) the outer's plant looks like a unity gain with a small lag — easy to control.
  • Sample rates often nest: 10 kHz current ISR → 1 kHz velocity → 100 Hz position. The control structure mirrors the timing structure.
  • Don't try to control a flexible mechanism with a single PID. The bandwidth gap between current control (very fast) and position of a long arm (slow, oscillatory) is enormous — cascading is mandatory.

Cheat sheet

BANDWIDTH RULE

Frequency separation
ω_inner ≥ 5–10 · ω_outer
Sample rate inner
f_s,inner ≥ 10 · ω_inner / 2π
Cascade transfer (idealised inner)
G_total ≈ G_outer · (1)when inner is fast vs outer

TUNE FROM THE INSIDE OUT

  • 1. Tune inner loop alone (high gain, low overshoot, fast settling)
  • 2. Close inner, tune middle treating closed inner loop as the plant
  • 3. Close middle, tune outer last
  • Never go back to retune inner unless you change the plant
  • Each loop runs at its own rate in firmware — ISR or RTOS task per loop

PITFALLS

  • Inner too slow — outer demands faster response than inner can deliver → oscillation
  • Inner saturated propagates as outer-loop failure; clamp outer's request to inner's limit
  • Multiple integrators across loops can wind up independently — anti-windup on each
  • Stale sample from inner if you forget to update before outer reads — strict timing matters
