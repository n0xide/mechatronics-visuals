Gain Scheduling — different PID for each operating point

A plant whose dynamics change with operating condition (motor with variable inertia, aircraft at different altitudes, robot at different payloads) can't be tuned with one fixed PID. Gain scheduling switches between pre-tuned PID sets indexed by a measured scheduling variable — usually altitude, speed, or load. It's the workhorse technique for nonlinear control before resorting to MPC or adaptive control. This visual sweeps the scheduling variable through a simulated mass-variable robot arm, shows fixed-PID degradation vs scheduled-PID tracking, and walks through both interpolation strategies.

The scheduling design recipe

1. Pick the scheduling variable σ. It must: • Be measurable (or estimable) in real time • Capture the dominant dynamic variation • Vary slowly compared to closed-loop bandwidth (decade slower is the rule of thumb) 2. Choose operating points σ_i covering the expected range (3-7 typical) 3. Tune a PID at each σ_i independently (Ziegler-Nichols, model-based, or by hand) → table of (σ_i, K_p_i, K_i_i, K_d_i) 4. Pick an interpolation method: • Nearest-neighbor: simplest, but produces gain "jumps" at boundaries (transients) • Linear interpolation: smooth between adjacent points; standard for slowly-varying σ • Bilinear (2-D scheduling): when 2 variables matter (altitude + Mach) • LPV (Linear Parameter-Varying): design that explicitly guarantees stability across σ — uses LMI tools (LMI toolbox) 5. Validate at off-design points: gain-scheduling is NOT guaranteed stable unless designed with LPV. Run linearization at intermediate σ values and check eigenvalues. 6. Add rate limits + slew on K_p/K_i/K_d: prevent sudden gain changes that can excite resonances. Typical: rate-limit K change to 10 %/sample.

Worked example — autopilot at three altitudes

Aircraft pitch dynamics

Pitch attitude θ vs elevator δ_e: G_θδ(s) = K_α / (s² + 2ζω_n·s + ω_n²). Both ω_n and ζ change with dynamic pressure q = ½·ρ·V². At low altitude (sea level, high q), short-period mode is fast (ω_n ≈ 4 rad/s, ζ ≈ 0.4). At 40,000 ft (1/4 the density), ω_n drops to 2 rad/s and ζ to 0.2 — much more lightly damped, prone to PIO (pilot-induced oscillation).

K_p grows ~3× from sea level to 40 kft because the plant gain (control-surface effectiveness ∝ dynamic pressure q̄ = ½ρV²) drops with thinner air, so the controller has to push harder to keep loop bandwidth. K_d also grows (here ~2.75×) to compensate the loss of natural damping — at high altitude the controller has to supply most of the damping itself. Real aircraft (F-16, A320) use scheduled control-surface gains driven by air data (altitude + Mach number).

Common pitfalls

  • Scheduling on a fast variable — if σ changes faster than the closed-loop bandwidth, gain transients excite the very dynamics you're trying to control. Slow σ down with a low-pass filter or pick a different variable.
  • Sparse operating-point grid — only 3 op points across a 10× parameter range leaves big interpolation errors. Linear interp on a curved K(σ) is biased. Add more points or use spline.
  • Stability not guaranteed mid-range — gain scheduling is "pointwise stable" but the trajectory between points can destabilize if σ varies fast. LPV provides actual guarantees.
  • Bumpless transfer broken — switching PID sets without preserving the integrator state causes step jumps in u. Always re-initialize I from the current u when switching: I_new = u_current − K_p_new·e − K_d_new·de.
  • Choosing σ that doesn't matter — scheduling on engine RPM when the actual variation is from cooling-fluid temperature wastes lookup tables. Profile your plant to find the dominant σ.
  • Wind-up across schedule — integrator built up at one σ may saturate when σ changes (lower actuator headroom). Combine with anti-windup (see anti-windup visual).
  • Schedule vs trim — different concepts: trim cancels steady-state inputs (gravity, friction), scheduling tunes the dynamic response. Need both for full nonlinear control.
