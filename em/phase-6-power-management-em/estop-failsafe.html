E-Stop & Fail-Safe Shutdown

Dual-channel e-stop, safety contactor, STO inputs, watchdog. Fail-safe means: when something breaks, motion stops — not "the software handles it."

A motor doesn't care that your code panicked — it has angular momentum and a 50 A drive. Safety architecture starts from the principle that any single fault must result in a safe state. The safe state is usually "motors freewheeling or actively braked, power removed at the contactor, no commands accepted until manually reset." This is enforced in hardware: physical buttons, dual-redundant relays, STO (Safe Torque Off) inputs on the drive that bypass the firmware path entirely. Click scenarios to see what kicks in.

What to notice

  • Two independent paths cut the motor. The contactor removes bus power. STO removes gate drive to the FETs. Either one alone stops torque; both together is defense in depth. A failure of one (welded contacts, broken STO trace) is caught by the other.
  • E-stop is dual-channel. Channel A and Channel B both must close to allow operation; both must open to permit motion. If a single wire shorts or breaks, the safety relay detects mismatch and drops out anyway. Single fault tolerance is the baseline of any safety-rated system.
  • Watchdog is hardware, not software. The Main MCU pets a hardware watchdog timer (typically inside a small Safety MCU or PMIC) at a fixed cadence. Miss a pet and the watchdog opens the safety relay autonomously — the crashed MCU can't stop it.
  • STO bypasses firmware. "Safe Torque Off" is an industry-standard input on motor drives that disconnects gate drive at the silicon level. No code runs. Even if the drive's firmware is in an infinite loop or stuck commanding 100% PWM, STO=low means zero torque on the motor.
  • Reset is intentional, not automatic. After an e-stop or fault, the system stays safe until a human takes a positive action (release button, press reset, power cycle). Automatic recovery hides intermittent faults and re-energizes mid-fault — dangerous.
  • Define the "safe state" up front. For a robot arm: brake engaged (or freewheeling, depending on gravity load). For a drone: motors off (will fall — but rapidly). For an EV: drive disabled, brake on regen. There's no universal safe state — you decide per machine.

Cheat sheet

SAFETY LAYERS (DEFENSE IN DEPTH)

  • L1: STO inputs on drives — cuts gate drive, no FW.
  • L2: Safety relay + contactor — removes bus.
  • L3: Dual-channel e-stop loop — hardware control.
  • L4: Watchdog on Safety MCU — catches FW crash.
  • L5: BMS OC/OV — catches electrical faults.
  • L6: Fuse — last-resort, slow.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Single-channel e-stop — one stuck switch = no e-stop at all.
  • Software-only stop — "I'll set PWM=0 in firmware" fails the moment FW crashes.
  • Auto-reset on e-stop release — button releases, motors slam back on. Always require positive reset.
  • Power-to-release brake misconfigured — if brake needs power to release, losing power engages it (correct); losing power releasing it (wrong wiring) means power loss = freefall.
  • Tested only when new — e-stop circuit accumulates faults. Test on every powerup.
  • No I²t margin on contactor — opening 50 A under load arcs the contacts; pick a contactor rated for the inductive load type.
