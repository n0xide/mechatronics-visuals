Inductive Kickback

Coils don't want their current to change. Regime: transient at switch-off of a current-carrying inductor (relay coil, solenoid, motor winding, transformer primary). When you abruptly open the switch, the inductor fights back by generating a voltage spike — sometimes hundreds of volts — across the switching element. The sign is set by Lenz's law: V_L = −L·di/dt, so when current is collapsing (di/dt < 0) the inductor voltage flips polarity vs. the steady-state drive direction. Without a snubber, that spike fries MOSFETs and BJTs in microseconds. The classic fix is a freewheeling diode; the advanced options are TVS clamps (for fast PWM), Zener+diode for fast drop-out, and RC snubbers (for AC loads).

Why the spike happens

An inductor stores energy in its magnetic field: E = ½·L·I². When you open the circuit, that energy can't disappear in zero time. The inductor instantly raises its voltage to whatever it takes to keep current flowing — including breaking down the air gap of the open switch, breaking down a MOSFET's drain-source junction, or just blasting through the body diode.

Voltage across inductor: V_L = L · di/dt If switch opens fast: di/dt approaches infinity → V_L approaches infinity Fast switching transient: di/dt ≈ 10⁶ A/s (1 A → 0 in 1 µs, typical MOSFET turn-off) For L = 100 mH (large relay/solenoid): V_L = 0.1 · 10⁶ = 100 kV theoretical peak Push di/dt to 10⁹ A/s (sub-ns) and you'd hit 100 MV — but air arcs at ~30 kV/cm, MOSFETs avalanche at V_DSS. In practice the spike is clamped by something — arcing, parasitic capacitance, breakdown of the nearest semiconductor.

Freewheeling diode (catch diode)

Wire a diode across the coil, anode to the low side of the coil, cathode to the high side. When the switch is on, the diode is reverse-biased and invisible. When the switch opens, the inductor tries to raise the low-side terminal above V+ — but the diode turns on at V+ + V_F (about 0.7 V for silicon, 0.3 V for Schottky), and the current freewheels through the diode, decaying exponentially with τ = L / R_coil.

During switch-on: I_L = V / R_coil (steady state) At switch-off: I_L starts at the same value, freewheels through diode + coil Decay: I(t) = I₀ · exp(-t / τ), τ = L / R_coil V across switch is clamped to: V_supply + V_F ≈ V_supply + 0.7

Trade-off: a freewheel diode is cheap and bulletproof. But the slow exponential decay delays relay drop-out — the relay's mechanical action waits for the magnetic field to collapse. For fast-acting (10 ms) relays this can push drop-out to 50 ms. If you need fast turn-off, use a Zener+diode (or TVS) flyback clamp: a Zener (V_Z = e.g. 15–30 V) in series-back-to-back with the freewheel diode. The inductor now has to climb to V_supply + V_F + V_Z before current freewheels, so |di/dt| during decay is V_Z/L instead of (V_F)/L → drop-out is several times faster.

TVS diode — for fast PWM switching

MOSFETs switching PWM into a motor coil might do 20 kHz — that's an inductive kickback every 50 µs. A standard rectifier diode (1N4007) has trr (reverse recovery) too slow; it doesn't turn off in time and contributes its own ringing.

TVS (Transient Voltage Suppressor) diodes are designed for high-current, very-fast clamps. They behave like back-to-back Zeners but with picosecond response. Specs to look at:

  • V_WM — working voltage (must be ≥ V_supply with margin)
  • V_C — clamping voltage at peak surge current
  • I_PP — peak pulse current (typical 100 A for 10/1000 µs)
  • Bidirectional vs unidirectional — bidirectional for AC, unidirectional for DC

RC snubber — for AC loads

On AC loads (mains relays, AC motors), a freewheeling diode would conduct continuously on every negative half-cycle. Bad. Instead, use an RC snubber: a series resistor + capacitor across the switching contacts (or across the load). When the switch opens, the inductor's voltage rise charges the cap (energy storage), and the resistor damps the L–C ringing and dissipates the energy as heat.

Cap absorbs initial transient: ΔV_cap = ∫I dt / C; for a critically-damped snubber, C ≈ I²·L/V_peak² R damps the L–C ringing — characteristic impedance match: R ≈ √(L/C). For L=80 mH, C=0.1 µF → R ≈ 900 Ω. R also limits the cap-discharge inrush when switch re-closes: I_inrush = V/R, must be < switch rating. Typical mains-AC contact snubber: R = 100–220 Ω 1 W (carbon or metal-oxide, non-inductive), C = 0.1 µF X1/Y1 AC-rated film. Don't use ceramic across AC line. Leakage at line frequency: I_leak ≈ V·2π·f·C; 230 V × 2π·50·0.1 µF ≈ 7 mA — non-negligible across an OFF contact.

Worked example — driving a 12 V solenoid from an N-channel MOSFET

The load

A 12 V SDS-806 solenoid valve, R_coil = 40 Ω, L ≈ 80 mH. Steady-state current = 12 V / 40 Ω = 300 mA. Stored energy at turn-off = ½ · 0.08 · 0.3² = 3.6 mJ.

The MOSFET

IRLZ44N: V_DSS = 55 V, R_DS(on) = 22 mΩ. Switch driven by an MCU GPIO via a level-shifter; rise/fall times around 100 ns.

Without snubber

di/dt = 300 mA / 100 ns = 3·10⁶ A/s V_kickback = L · di/dt = 0.08 · 3e6 = 240 kV theoretical Real: clamped by MOSFET breakdown at 55 V (avalanche). Each switch-off dissipates 3.6 mJ in the MOSFET's avalanche. If switched at 1 Hz: 3.6 mW thermal — survivable but stressful. At 100 Hz: 360 mW — way over the avalanche rating, MOSFET fails.

With freewheel diode (1N4148)

V_clamp = V_supply + V_F = 12 + 0.7 = 12.7 V (well below MOSFET V_DSS) Current decays through coil + diode with τ = L / R_coil = 0.08 / 40 = 2 ms Diode current = 300 mA peak, falling exponentially. 1N4148 is rated 300 mA average forward / 1 A repetitive peak — marginal for 300 mA repeating kickback. Prefer 1N4007 (1 A continuous) or SS14 Schottky for a clean derating margin. Solenoid drop-out time: ~ 6 ms (3·τ) → acceptable for a valve, too slow for a printer dot driver.

With Zener+diode (flyback) clamp — fast drop-out

15 V Zener in series with the freewheel diode → V_clamp = V_supply + V_Z + V_F = 12 + 15 + 0.7 = 27.7 V Decay is ~linear (constant clamp ⇒ constant di/dt = −V_Z/L), not exponential: |di/dt| = V_Z/L = 15 / 0.08 = 187 A/s → drop-out ≈ I/|di/dt| = L·I/V_Z = 0.08·0.3/15 = 1.6 ms (~4× faster than 6 ms diode-only drop-out). MOSFET still safe at 27.7 V (V_DSS = 55 V, ~50 % derating margin).

What to notice

  • Energy = ½LI² must go somewhere. The snubber's job is to absorb it cleanly — not to make it disappear.
  • Freewheel = slow drop-out. The trade-off for free protection is delayed mechanical motion.
  • For PWM (motor drives, switching regulators), the freewheel diode is part of the design — it's not optional. The "low-side" diode on a buck converter or H-bridge IS the freewheel path for the inductor.
  • MOSFETs have an internal body diode — strictly speaking, every MOSFET-switched inductive load already has a freewheel path. But that body diode has bad trr and dissipates avalanche energy in the FET die. Always add an external diode for repeated switching.
  • Schottky for low-voltage, fast switching; standard silicon for high-voltage; TVS for repeated transients.

Where you'll see this

Real uses

  • Every relay coil driver — basic freewheel diode (1N4148, 1N4007, or SS14).
  • Stepper and brushed-DC motor H-bridges — four internal freewheel diodes in the bridge IC (DRV8833, L298).
  • BLDC inverter — six freewheel diodes (or MOSFET body diodes if good enough) across the three half-bridges.
  • Buck converters — Schottky diode is the heart of the topology.
  • Solenoid drivers, fuel injectors, ignition coils — Zener clamp for fast turn-off; HV-tolerant MOSFET.

RULES OF THUMB

  • Always snub inductive loads. Always.
  • Schottky for DC freewheel < ~40 V — fast trr, low V_F.
  • 1N4007 for DC freewheel up to 1 kV reverse blocking — slow trr (~30 µs), only OK for low-rep-rate DC; not usable on AC loads (it would conduct on every line half-cycle).
  • TVS for repeated PWM across the switch or load.
  • RC snubber (or MOV) on AC contact loads — diode-freewheel doesn't apply when polarity flips.
  • Diode rating ≥ steady-state current; surge rating ≥ 10× steady.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Diode polarity — cathode to V+. Reverse = direct short.
  • Too-small diode — 1N4148 (300 mA average / 1 A peak) for a 1 A continuous freewheel = melts.
  • Slow diode + PWM — 1N4007 trr = 30 µs, useless above 1 kHz.
  • Body-diode-only in repeated PWM — fries the MOSFET.
  • Layout: snubber long traces — adds inductance, defeats the snubber.
