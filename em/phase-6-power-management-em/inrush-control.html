Inrush Control

Cold caps and stalled motors look the same to the battery: a short circuit. Inrush limiters keep peak current out of the destructive zone. Regime: turn-on transient, first 1−100 ms after switch closure; steady-state load behavior is on the rail-architecture and budget pages.

Three things draw enormous current at power-on: bulk capacitors charging from zero, brushed motors with no back-EMF and locked rotor (stall) resistance only, and incandescent / heater loads with cold (low) filament resistance. All look like near-shorts to the battery for the first few milliseconds — for caps and incandescents typically 10−100× the steady-state current; for motors typically 3−10× the rated continuous current at stall (limited by winding R only). That blows fuses, dips bus voltage, sags MCUs into brown-out, and chars connector contacts. Inrush control caps the peak, smoothing the transient over tens of milliseconds.

What to notice

  • With no limiter, I_peak = V_bat / R_source (assumes R-dominated; in real boards loop inductance softens the very first nanoseconds but the cap-charging RC envelope still dominates the millisecond-scale pulse). A 12 V battery into 50 mΩ source gives 240 A peak the instant the switch closes — that's what a 2200 µF bulk cap pulls. The exponential tail (τ = RC = 0.05×2200 µF ≈ 110 µs) drops it to ~90 A by 5 τ (~0.5 ms). Fuse I²t energy from that pulse is enough to blow a fast-blow fuse rated for the steady-state load.
  • NTC thermistor: starts cold and high-R, warms and drops — ratio is ~100×. A CL-60 starts ~10 Ω at 25 °C and falls to ~0.1 Ω once self-heated by the steady-state load current. Limits inrush to ~1.2 A peak on a 12 V bus, then steady-state drop is <0.2 V. Trap: if you power-cycle within seconds of shutdown, the NTC is still hot (~0.1 Ω) and provides essentially zero limit on the restart — the next inrush hits the fuse cold. NTCs are also load-current dependent: at low steady-state current they don't self-heat enough and the steady-state drop is too high.
  • Active soft-start with P-FET + RC. An RC on the gate ramps V_GS slowly so the FET enters the linear region; current is gated by V_GS, not by C charging. Most flexible — you choose the ramp time. Adds parts, but no thermal recovery issue.
  • Pre-charge: resistor + relay. For high-power systems (EV traction packs, big DC servos): a resistor in series during pre-charge phase, then a contactor shorts it out. Two switches, sequenced timing, but handles kilowatt-class buses without the thermistor heat headache.
  • Time-to-charge is set by RC. Cap voltage reaches 90% after ~2.3τ. If the system can't tolerate the boot delay, lower the cap, lower the R, or use the FET method to ramp linearly instead of exponentially.
  • I²t is the right fuse metric. A fast 10 A fuse has an I²t rating (e.g., 30 A²s). The inrush pulse has its own I²t (peak² · duration). If pulse I²t > fuse I²t, the fuse blows. Just comparing peak current to fuse rating misses this.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Hot NTC bypassed — restart within seconds of shutdown, NTC is still low-R, no limit.
  • FET in linear region too long — absorbs the inrush energy; SOA derating matters.
  • Pre-charge relay never closes — if logic times out, system runs on the limiter forever and burns up.
  • Slow-blow fuse hides bigger fault — sized for inrush headroom, ignores a slow MOSFET failure.
  • Sizing on 25°C ambient — NTC R drops faster when hot ambient; less limiting on summer day.
