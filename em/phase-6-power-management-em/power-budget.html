A power budget is not optional. Before you choose a battery, before you choose a converter, before you tape out a PCB, you make a table of every load with three columns: continuous, peak, and duty. Continuous determines battery runtime. Peak determines wire gauge and converter rating. Duty (what fraction of time each load is active) determines the average power that actually drains the cell. What to notice

  • Continuous × duty = average. A motor that draws 2 A peak but runs only 20% of the time drains the battery at 400 mA average. The 2 A figure sets your wire gauge and converter rating; the 400 mA figure sets your runtime.
  • Compare averages, not peaks. An ESP32 with WiFi continuously connected averages 100 mA. A motor with 2 A peak at 20% duty averages 400 mA — 4× the ESP32's runtime cost. The motor's peak is 20× larger, but its average (the number that drains the battery) is only 4×.
  • Peak current sizes the converter, not the average. Buy a buck rated for 1.5× peak; a 2 A peak load needs at least a 3 A continuous converter, ideally 5 A to avoid thermal throttling.
  • Don't drain below DoD limit. Li-ion is rated for ~80% depth of discharge. Plan runtime against the usable capacity (0.8 · Q_nom), not nameplate Ah. Going to 100% DoD halves cycle life.
  • C-rate ceiling. A 3 Ah cell rated 10C delivers 30 A max. A 2 A motor draws less than 1C — trivial. A 20 A motor on a 3 Ah pack draws 6.7C — cell heating becomes real.
  • Converter loss is real. 88% efficiency means 12% of every watt becomes heat. A 20 W load consumes 22.7 W from the battery; the missing 2.7 W warms the buck IC.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Quiescent leak ignored — 3 mA of "off" current on a 3 Ah pack = 1000 h = 6 weeks to flat.
  • Datasheet typical, not max — budget for max + 30%, not "typical at 25°C with no load."
  • Inrush forgotten — capacitor charge currents at boot can spike 10× steady-state.
  • WiFi/BT continuous-mode — assume 100 mA average; bursting to 500 mA every transmit is the ↑ case.
