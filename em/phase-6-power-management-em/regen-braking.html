Regenerative Braking

A spinning motor is a generator. When the motor's back-EMF exceeds the bus voltage, the current direction in the H-bridge reverses — energy flows from the motor's mechanical rotation back into the battery (or, if the battery can't accept it, into a brake resistor). This is how electric vehicles recover braking energy, how cobot joints don't crash on power-loss, and why your robot's motor controller needs a brake-chopper.

Back-EMF — the motor's generator voltage

Every brushed-DC and BLDC motor obeys: V_bemf = K_e · ω in SI form, where K_e is the back-EMF constant in V·s/rad and ω is angular velocity in rad/s. Datasheets quote it three different ways:

  • K_e in V/krpm (back-EMF per 1000 RPM, common in industrial servo specs) — what the controls below use.
  • K_v in RPM/V (motor velocity constant — the no-load RPM per applied volt, common in hobby/RC). This is the numerical reciprocal of V/krpm when both are expressed in those units: 100 V/krpm ↔ 10 RPM/V.
  • K_e in V·s/rad (SI, equal to the torque constant K_t in N·m/A for an ideal motor). Convert: K_e [V·s/rad] = 60/(2π) · K_e [V/krpm] / 1000.

Mixing the three conventions is the #1 motor-spec mistake. In the controls below we use V/krpm so V_bemf = ω[RPM]/1000 · K_e[V/krpm] is plain arithmetic.

In normal driving (motoring), the bus supplies V_bus to the motor terminals. Current flows: I = (V_bus − V_bemf) / R_winding. Mechanical power output P_mech = V_bemf · I.

If you suddenly stop driving and shorting the bridge so the motor terminals see V_bus = 0, the back-EMF drives current through the (now-shorted) windings — the motor brakes like a generator into a short. Energy is dissipated as I²R in the windings and as torque against rotation.

The interesting case: leaving the bridge in PWM mode with a duty cycle giving an effective motor terminal voltage = some intermediate value. If V_bemf > V_motor_eff, current reverses direction. The motor pushes current into the battery; energy flows the "wrong" way; the motor experiences a braking torque equal to K_t · |I_regen| (with K_t = K_e in SI units).

The MOSFET H-bridge as a 4-quadrant switch

The same four-MOSFET H-bridge that drives a motor can also rectify current flowing the other way — through the body diodes of the MOSFETs (or actively, through synchronous rectification). This is what makes regen possible at the same cost as driving: no extra components, just smarter control.

Each motor terminal connects to a half-bridge (top MOSFET + bottom MOSFET). The four quadrants of operation:

QuadrantV_motor signI_motor signPower direction
1 — Forward motoring++battery → motor
2 — Forward regen+−motor → battery
3 — Reverse motoring−−battery → motor
4 — Reverse regen−+motor → battery

The body diodes always allow current "backwards" — i.e., from motor's high terminal up to the bus, or from ground up to the motor's low terminal. During regen, instead of letting the body diodes do all the work (and dissipate I²·V_F watts), you actively switch the top/bottom MOSFETs ON when their body diode is conducting. This is synchronous rectification, with I²·R_DS(on) loss instead of I·V_F.

Current reversal — what the trace looks like

Imagine you're driving at constant duty cycle 70 %, motor spinning at the equilibrium RPM where V_bemf ≈ 0.7·V_bus. Now you drop the duty cycle to 30 %. The motor's RPM (and back-EMF) take milliseconds to react, but the effective V_bus is now only 30 %. So V_bemf > V_bus_eff → current reverses → motor brakes regeneratively. The motor decelerates; V_bemf drops; eventually it matches the new equilibrium and steady state resumes.

Steady-state at duty D: V_motor = D · V_bus Steady-state RPM: ω = V_motor / K_e = D·V_bus / K_e Current during transient (D changes from D₀ to D₁ instantly): I = (D₁·V_bus − V_bemf) / R If D₁·V_bus < V_bemf → I is negative → current flows from motor into battery.

Brake chopper — when the battery can't take it

Batteries have a charge-current limit (typically 0.5C to 3C). At high braking power, you'd push more current than the battery accepts. Result: bus voltage rises uncontrollably as the H-bridge keeps pumping current into a capacitor and a battery that won't take it.

The brake chopper (or "brake resistor IC") is a fifth MOSFET in series with a beefy power resistor across the bus. When V_bus exceeds a threshold (say, 110 % of nominal), the chopper switches on, dumping the excess energy into the resistor as heat. The resistor handles the energy the battery can't.

  • Threshold: typically V_bus_nom + 10–20 %.
  • Resistor sized for peak brake power × duty; thermal designed for sustained downhill braking.
  • Hysteresis: switch on at V_high, off at V_low, with a 5–10 % gap so it doesn't chatter.

Worked example — robot arm joint deceleration

The setup

A 6-DOF robot arm joint: BLDC motor with datasheet K_e = 80 V/krpm, R_winding = 0.3 Ω, V_bus = 48 V (13S Li-ion e-bike pack — 13 × 3.6 V ≈ 46.8 V nominal, 13 × 4.2 V = 54.6 V full charge; 4S Li-ion would only be 14.8 V nominal, far too low for this motor and its peak BEMF). At the moment of braking, joint motor is spinning at 4000 RPM under modest load. (Spoiler — this K_e is too high even for the 48 V bus; we'll catch it in the unit-sanity step.) The diagram above shows a brushed H-bridge, but the same energy/current math applies to a BLDC's 3-phase inverter when you reduce it to an equivalent DC model (back-EMF, winding R, effective terminal V); the per-phase factor cancels in the V_bemf·I and I²R balance below.

Get the convention right first

The motor spec uses K_e in V/krpm (volts of back-EMF per 1000 RPM). Hobby/RC suppliers usually quote K_v in RPM/V (no-load RPM per applied volt) — the reciprocal convention in reciprocal units (e.g. 80 V/krpm ↔ 12.5 RPM/V; 4 V/krpm ↔ 250 RPM/V). Mixing them is the #1 motor-spec mistake.

Sanity check: K_e = 80 V/krpm at 4000 RPM cruise would give V_bemf = 320 V — meaningless on a 48 V bus. 80 V/krpm motors belong in 200–400 V drives. For 48 V at 4000 RPM cruise we need a motor where V_bemf at 4000 RPM is comfortably below the bus, so we'll pick a motor spec'd as K_v = 250 RPM/V (equivalently K_e = 4 V/krpm).

Use realistic numbers (K_v = 250 RPM/V, i.e. K_e = 4 V/krpm)

At 48 V, max no-load RPM ≈ 250 · 48 = 12 000. Cruise at 4000 RPM → V_bemf = 4000 / 250 = 16 V.

Brake by setting duty = 0, all 4 low-side FETs on (short-circuit / dynamic brake): I_short = V_bemf / R = 16 / 0.3 = 53 A (very hard brake; all energy dumped in windings as I²R — overheats if sustained) Brake by PWM regen with effective motor terminal voltage V_motor_eff = 10 V (duty D ≈ 10/48 ≈ 0.21): I_motor = (V_bemf − V_motor_eff) / R = (16 − 10)/0.3 = 20 A (flowing out of the motor) Mechanical power extracted: V_bemf · I_motor = 16 · 20 = 320 W out of the rotor Winding I²R loss: 20² · 0.3 = 120 W heat in copper Electrical power back to bus: V_motor_eff · I_motor = 10 · 20 = 200 W recovered into the pack. (Check: 320 W mech = 200 W elec + 120 W copper ✓.) Average bus current: I_bus_avg = P_bus / V_bus = 200 / 48 ≈ 4.2 A — well within a 13S 5-Ah pack at 1 C accept (5 A). No chopper needed here. To overrun the pack you would need a harder brake or a smaller pack.

Brake chopper sizing — harder brake scenario

Push V_motor_eff down to 4 V → I_motor = (16 − 4)/0.3 = 40 A, I²R = 480 W copper, P_bus = 4·40 = 160 W back to bus, I_bus_avg = 160/48 ≈ 3.3 A — still under 5 A accept. Hardest regen short of pure short-circuit: V_motor_eff → 0⁺ → I_motor → 53 A, ALL energy in copper (no bus recovery). The chopper is for when a transient sends I_bus_avg above the pack's accept limit — e.g. a 10-Ah-equivalent inertia decelerating fast enough to push 20+ A average into a pack that accepts 5 A. If a brake event delivers I_bus = 10 A average for 0.5 s into a 5 A-accept pack: 5 A go into the pack, 5 A · 48 V = 240 W must go into the chopper for 0.5 s → 120 J. Use a chopper resistor with thermal mass to absorb 120 J pulses + steady-state for sustained downhill. Cross-check via kinetic energy: reflected inertia J_ref ≈ 0.01 kg·m² at 4000 RPM (ω ≈ 419 rad/s) → E_rot = ½·0.01·419² ≈ 877 J. A 0.5 s 320-W brake removes 160 J of mech energy → motor decelerates from 4000 RPM to 4000·√(717/877) ≈ 3620 RPM. Long downhills accumulate energy faster than this single event.

What to notice

  • Regen is "free" from the bridge's perspective — the same hardware drives and recovers, just with reversed current direction.
  • Synchronous rectification matters for efficiency. Letting current freewheel through body diodes wastes Vf·I (about 2 % at low V, 5 %+ at low currents). Active rectification keeps the losses at I²·R_DS(on).
  • Battery charge-acceptance is the real bottleneck. A 4S 2 Ah pack at 1C accepts 2 A; at 3C accepts 6 A. Regen above that limit needs a brake resistor.
  • Plug-braking (true polarity reversal: V_motor = −V_bus) generates the highest brake torque but also the highest peak current (I ≈ (V_bus + V_bemf)/R, almost 2× short-circuit brake). Too aggressive for most BLDCs. Use short-circuit ("dynamic") brake or PWM-controlled regen instead. The simulator above shows short-circuit brake; plug-brake isn't safe to display without current limiting.
  • Without regen capability, an unloaded motor will spin up to its no-load speed ω_no-load = V_bus / K_e (or RPM_no-load = V_bus · K_v if K_v is in RPM/V). With regen, you can hold any RPM below no-load with finite torque.

Where you'll see this

Real uses

  • Electric vehicles — regen recovers 10–30 % of driving energy on a typical commute.
  • E-bikes — light-touch regen helps with steep descents.
  • Cobot arms — joint motors regenerate when bringing a load down; brake chopper protects bus.
  • CNC spindles — fast spindle stops use regen + brake resistor.
  • Elevators — descending elevators regenerate; the energy heats braking resistors on the roof.

RULES OF THUMB

  • If V_bemf > V_bus_eff: current reverses, regen happens.
  • Body diodes always allow reverse current, but waste V_F·I.
  • Synchronous rectification: I²·R_DS(on) losses instead.
  • Battery accept = 1–3C typical; size brake resistor for the difference.
  • Always include a TVS or chopper for load-dump protection.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • No brake chopper — full battery + regen = bus pumping into overvoltage → caps blow.
  • Body-diode-only regen at PWM rates → diodes overheat.
  • Single-quadrant drivers (cheap PWM ICs) only support 1-quadrant motoring. No regen.
  • Forgetting current limit in firmware — regen current can exceed motor wire rating in microseconds.
  • No deadtime between top/bottom FET — shoot-through during commutation, instant failure.
