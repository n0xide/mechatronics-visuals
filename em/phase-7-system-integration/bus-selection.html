Bus Selection — CAN-FD vs EtherCAT vs RS-485 vs SPI/UART

Picking the bus is a one-time decision that shapes every node in your system. The wrong bus means rewiring at month six.

There is no universally "best" robotics bus. Each one optimizes a different axis: CAN-FD is the workhorse for distributed motor control because it's cheap, deterministic enough, and arbitration handles collisions for free. EtherCAT wins when you need sub-100-µs sync across many nodes and you can afford the master + slave silicon. RS-485 is the chainsaw — crude, robust, dirt cheap, perfect for daisy-chained smart servos but no arbitration so you need a polling master. SPI and UART are not really buses (they're point-to-point links), but you'll use them inside every node for the local sensors. Compare on six dimensions: bitrate, latency, determinism, topology, complexity, cost.

Scenario fit

Hover or click a bus above to highlight its best-fit scenarios.

6-DOF arm, 6 joint controllers, 1 kHz commanded

Need: deterministic enough, cheap, doesn't require a PhD to bring up.
→ CAN-FD (default), EtherCAT only if you've outgrown CAN's latency.
Each joint runs its own fast loop locally; the bus only carries 1 kHz state/cmd. CAN-FD's 100 µs worst-case is fine. EtherCAT is overkill until you're synchronizing >10 joints to <10 µs.

Hexapod with 24× LX-224HV daisy-chained servos

Need: many cheap smart-servos, simple wiring, master sets schedule.
→ RS-485 half-duplex (the servo native protocol).
No arbitration is fine because a single MCU master polls each servo at a fixed cadence. Total bus utilization caps your update rate (24 servos × ~500 µs each ≈ 80 Hz max).

CNC mill, 5 axes synchronized to <1 µs

Need: hard real-time, distributed-clock sync, industrial heritage.
→ EtherCAT (LinuxCNC + EtherLab master).
Distributed Clocks gives sub-µs sync across all slaves — required so all axes start a coordinated motion segment exactly together. CAN-FD jitter (~100 µs) would show as surface marks on the finished part.

Encoder, IMU, ADC — sensors on a single PCB

Need: fast, deterministic, very short distance (cm).
→ SPI from MCU to each sensor.
SPI is point-to-point, master-clocked, full-duplex, 10–50 MHz. Perfect for <30 cm runs. Each peripheral needs its own chip-select line, so plan GPIO budget.

Logging output to a serial terminal, or a GPS module

Need: streaming text or simple framed messages to one other device.
→ UART (115200–921600 baud typical).
Not a bus — just a wire. Anything more than 2 endpoints means switching to RS-485 (multi-drop) or CAN.

Mobile robot — 4 wheel motors + IMU + LIDAR controller

Need: handful of nodes, EMI-rich environment, <200 µs latency.
→ CAN-FD.
Differential signaling shrugs off motor noise; 64-byte FD payloads carry IMU packets in one frame; priority IDs ensure wheel commands beat LIDAR housekeeping.

What to notice

  • "Bitrate" lies — Ethernet/EtherCAT looks fastest at 100 Mbps, but for short small messages the bus overhead (preamble, headers, IFG) dominates and CAN-FD's smaller frames can finish a 10-byte message faster end-to-end. Compare frame turnaround, not raw bitrate.
  • Determinism comes from arbitration, not speed — CAN's CSMA/CR (collision detection + resolution by priority) is what makes it real-time-ish. RS-485 has no arbitration, so it relies on a master scheduling everything — your determinism is only as good as the master's loop.
  • Topology dictates wiring cost — daisy chain (RS-485, CAN linear) means one cable threading the chassis. Point-to-point (SPI) means one cable per peripheral — fine inside a PCB, terrible across joints.
  • Termination is a physical-layer trap on all differential buses — CAN, RS-485, EtherCAT all need correct end-of-line termination (typically 120 Ω). Skip it and the bus appears to work at low speeds, then fails mysteriously when you push the data rate.
  • EtherCAT silicon is the gatekeeper — every slave needs an ESC chip (LAN9252, ET1100, etc.). That hardware tax plus the proprietary master stack is why EtherCAT lives in industrial automation, not hobby builds. Pick CAN-FD until you can't.
  • SPI for sensors, CAN-FD for actuators, Ethernet for the host — common modern split. Each subsystem uses the bus that fits its constraints.

Worked example — 6-DOF arm bus split

The architecture

Industrial 6-DOF arm with separate joint controllers, an IMU on the end-effector, a force/torque sensor at the wrist, and a Linux host running ROS 2 / MoveIt. Three bus tiers:

TierBusWhyBandwidth
Host ↔ controller nodeGigabit Ethernet (ROS 2 DDS)Need MB/s for joint trajectories + images; host has spare CPU1 Gbps available, ~10 MB/s used
Controller node ↔ 6 joint driversCAN-FD at 1 Mbps arb / 5 Mbps dataSingle twisted pair through robot arm; deterministic priority; 8-byte joint command + 8-byte feedback5 Mbps theoretical, ~500 kbps used at 1 kHz update rate
Joint driver ↔ motor encoderSSI / BiSS-C inside the driver PCBLocal, point-to-point, deterministic; encoder is a sensor10 Mbps to encoder, < 1 µs latency
End-effector IMU + F/T → controllerSPI on the local PCB, then via the joint-driver CAN bus to hostSPI is fast + low latency; aggregated by joint driver10 Mbps SPI local, 200 kbps over CAN

Why this split

The host's CPU + Ethernet are abundant. The wire run inside the arm is precious — only 2 conductors of CAN-FD instead of 8+ for Ethernet. The drivers handle their own encoder loop at > 10 kHz locally (SPI/SSI), then report up at 1 kHz over CAN. The Ethernet host doesn't need to see every encoder sample — only the joint state.

Anti-patterns

Don't run Ethernet through robot joints. CAT 5e cable doesn't bend a million times. Bus failures from cable break are 90% of robot maintenance issues.

Don't use I²C for inter-board communication. I²C tolerates 50 cm at 100 kHz indoors; in a robot arm with EMI from motor PWM, you'll get sporadic data corruption that won't reproduce on the bench. Use differential CAN or RS-485 across joints.

Don't oversubscribe a single bus. The CAN-FD bus at 65% utilization works fine; at 90% one bad ECU causes cascade failures. Cross-check Liu-Layland-style schedulability with real frame mix.

Cheat sheet

DECISION TREE

  • 2 chips on one PCB? → SPI or I²C.
  • 2 boards, 1 link? → UART (or USB if you need OS support).
  • Many smart-servos, 1 master? → RS-485 daisy chain.
  • Distributed motor control, <10 joints? → CAN-FD.
  • Industrial sync, >10 axes, sub-µs? → EtherCAT.
  • Host ↔ host? → Ethernet / Wi-Fi / DDS.

Single-ended · < 30 cm · 4 wires + CS each
UART
Single-ended · TTL ≤ 1 m, RS-232 levels ≤ 15 m · TX/RX/GND only

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Confusing RS-232 and RS-485 — RS-232 is single-ended, point-to-point, ±3 V to ±15 V drivers. RS-485 is differential, multi-drop, ±1.5 V to ±5 V differential drivers with -7 V to +12 V receiver common-mode tolerance (TIA-485).
  • Picking I²C for distributed sensors — I²C max ~1 m, 7-bit address collisions are common. Always switch to CAN for cross-chassis.
  • Forgetting bus-load math — classic CAN 2.0 at 1 Mbps: an 8-byte frame is ~135 bits (incl. stuffing + IFS). 6 nodes × 1 kHz = ~80% loaded; 8 nodes saturates the bus. Add anything and you start dropping deadlines.
  • Mixing 3.3 V and 5 V CAN transceivers — they're electrically compatible if both rails are present; double-check with a transceiver datasheet, not vibes.
  • EtherCAT cable order matters — slaves must be physically chained in the order the master expects; swapping cables silently breaks Distributed Clocks.
