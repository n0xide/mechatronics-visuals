Data Flow + Latency Budget — Sensor to Actuator

A control loop is a deadline. Each stage between sensor and actuator eats µs. Add them up. Compare to the period. Either you fit or you redesign.

For any closed loop — say, a current loop on a BLDC motor — the path is: encoder spins → magnetic sensor IC samples → SPI transfer to MCU → ADC converts phase currents → control law runs → DAC/PWM updates → gate driver responds → motor current changes. Each step has its own delay. The sum is your loop latency. The loop also has a deadline: the time between one sample and the next, set by your sample rate. If latency > deadline, you've fallen behind and the loop becomes unstable (or, more often, you missed a deadline silently and the system "just works worse"). What to notice

  • The MCU compute time scales with control complexity — a simple PI loop is ~3 µs on a G4; full FOC with Clarke+Park+SVPWM is ~15–20 µs; an MPC horizon can balloon to 100+ µs. Switch the dropdown to see what fits.
  • Sample rate sets the deadline — 10 kHz means each loop iteration must finish in 100 µs. At 20 kHz you have 50 µs. Crank to 50 kHz and even a simple loop on a G4 starts to miss.
  • Going across a bus is the biggest hit — switch the bus dropdown to CAN. Suddenly serializing a 4-byte command + TX time = 80 µs minimum. This is why you never put the fast loop on the bus — close it locally.
  • Motor electrical time constant L/R is your floor — even with zero compute and zero bus, the motor current can only change at L/R. For typical small BLDC, L/R ~100–500 µs. Your control loop can't beat physics.
  • "Sample mid-PWM" — the ADC must trigger at the midpoint of the PWM period, when switching noise is quietest. This shifts your effective sample time by half a PWM period (25 µs at 20 kHz) but gives clean current readings. Worth it.
  • Always leave headroom — never plan for 95% loop utilization. Target 50–70% peak utilization (30–50% headroom) for interrupt jitter, branch mispredictions, and the surprise function call that turns into 30 µs.

Worked example — current-controlled BLDC with sensor-to-PWM latency

The setup

STM32G474 (Cortex-M4F at 170 MHz) driving a 6-FET BLDC at 20 kHz PWM. ADC samples shunt current at PWM midpoint. FOC update + new PWM duty must finish before the next sample.

PWM-update delay

New duty takes effect at the next timer update event. With 50 µs period and center-aligned PWM, the new duty applies 25 µs after the ISR finishes → effective control delay = 8.8 µs (ISR) + 25 µs (half PWM period) ≈ 34 µs latency from sample to actuation.

Comparison: same loop on a slower MCU

Cortex-M0+ at 48 MHz, no FPU: ISR time ~50 µs (FPU emulation) → can't even close at 20 kHz. Drop to 10 kHz (100 µs period).
Cortex-M4F at 100 MHz, FPU: ISR ~15 µs at 20 kHz. Workable with care.
Cortex-M7 at 400 MHz: ISR ~3 µs; can push PWM to 40 kHz with margin.

For modern servo applications, M4F is the floor. M7 / R52 for high-end. Anything M0-class belongs on stepper motors with simpler step/direction control.

TYPICAL STAGE COSTS

  • Encoder sample — 1–10 µs (SPI transfer).
  • ADC convert — 1–3 µs at 12-bit, <1 µs at 8-bit fast mode.
  • PI law — 1–3 µs on Cortex-M4 with FPU.
  • Full FOC — 10–20 µs (sin/cos table + 2 PIs + SVPWM).
  • PWM update — <1 µs (write timer register).
  • Gate driver prop — 100–500 ns.
  • Motor L/R — 100–500 µs (electrical floor).

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Forgetting sin/cos cost — libm sinf() is 30–100 cycles. Use a table lookup or CORDIC for the FOC inner loop.
  • Polling instead of DMA for SPI — DMA the encoder transfer; let the CPU work in parallel.
  • Floats on a Cortex-M0/M3 — no FPU means every multiply is dozens of cycles. Either upgrade the MCU or go fixed-point Q15/Q31.
  • Putting the loop on a non-RT host — Linux user-space jitter is ms-scale. The fast loop belongs on a microcontroller.
  • Reading the encoder on the same tick you command PWM — actually fine, but only if you sampled the encoder at PWM mid; otherwise you read stale data + new command.
