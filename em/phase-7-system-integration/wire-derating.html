Wire Bundle Derating & Voltage Drop

A wire's ampacity datasheet number assumes a single conductor in open air. Bundle it with other current-carrying wires and the temperature in the bundle rises, dropping the safe current. Add a long run and even a wire that passes ampacity may cause an unacceptable voltage drop at the load. This visual ties both effects together — bundle derating + voltage drop — into one decision tool.

How bundle derating works (physics, not magic)

The handbook ampacity is set by the temperature rise a single wire can endure (typically 30 °C above ambient before insulation degrades). Each wire dissipates I²R as heat. When you bundle wires together, every wire's heat is trapped by its neighbors — heat can only escape from the surface of the bundle, not each wire. Halve the surface-area-to-current ratio and the bundle runs hotter at the same per-wire current; to stay at the same internal temperature, every wire must carry less.

The empirical derating factors below are from MIL-W-5088 / SAE AS50881 (aerospace bundling tables, widely used in robotics for conservative engineering):

Note this is a continuous-duty derating. For intermittent loads (e.g., motor inrush) the per-wire current can briefly exceed derated ampacity as long as the bundle's thermal mass absorbs the heat. Robot wiring harnesses typically size for continuous duty plus a 1.25× margin for transients.

The voltage-drop side

Even when a wire passes ampacity, the I²R loss along the run can drop too much voltage at the load. The classic 2 % rule (drop ≤ 2 % of supply) keeps regulator headroom intact:

1. Wire resistance per meter: R/m = ρ_Cu / A  ·  ρ_Cu = 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m @ 20 °C 2. Round-trip resistance: R_total = 2 · L · (R/m)  ·  two wires carry the current — out and back 3. Voltage drop: V_drop = I · R_total = 2 · I · R · L 4. Drop %: V_drop / V_supply  ·  aim ≤ 2 % for clean rails, ≤ 5 % for non-critical

Resistance rises with temperature. Copper's TCR is ~0.004/°C, so a 50 °C wire is ~12 % more resistive than a 20 °C wire. Hot environments + heavily-loaded wires can drive R up enough to matter; many automotive harnesses use the 85 °C rated values, not 20 °C.

Worked example — robot arm joint motor

The problem

A robot arm motor pulls 8 A continuous (peaks at 15 A). Battery + driver are 1.5 m away. The cable runs in a bundle with 9 other wires (encoder feedback, brake, temperature sensor, etc., 10 total). Supply is 24 V. Acceptable drop: 2 %.

The arithmetic

Max allowed drop: 24 V × 0.02 = 0.48 V at 8 A → max R_total = 0.48 / 8 = 60 mΩ Round trip is 2 × 1.5 = 3 m → max R/m = 60 / 3 = 20 mΩ/m From the AWG table: AWG 16 has R/m ≈ 13 mΩ → drop is 8 × 13 × 3 / 1000 = 0.31 V (1.3 %) ✓ Now derate: 10 conductors in bundle → factor 0.50. AWG 16 single-wire ampacity (power) = 3.7 A. Derated: 3.7 × 0.50 = 1.85 A — far below 8 A continuous

The fix

Step up to AWG 12 (5.9 × 0.50 = wait, still only 2.95 A — no good) or AWG 10. Better: pull the motor power pair out of the bundle into a separate twisted-pair with no other wires nearby. Then single-wire ampacity applies and AWG 14 (5.9 A power) passes 8 A continuous (within 5 A overage but only briefly during peaks) — or AWG 12 (9.3 A) gives a clean margin. Voltage drop on AWG 12 over 3 m at 8 A: 2 × 8 × 5.21 × 1.5 / 1000 = 125 mV = 0.52 %. ✓

Takeaway: in a robot's wire harness, current-carrying wires often need their own (small) sub-bundle separate from low-power signal wires. Otherwise the derating math forces wire gauges 2-3 steps fatter than necessary.

What to notice

  • Bundle effect compounds steeply at first — going from 1 to 3 conductors drops the factor 30 %; going from 3 to 24 drops it another 30 %. Big jump for the first few neighbors, then diminishing.
  • Voltage-drop and bundle-derating constrain different ends — short runs are derating-limited (ampacity is what gets you); long runs are voltage-drop-limited (drop is what gets you). Look at the larger of the two required gauges.
  • Motor leads + sensor leads in the same bundle = both problems — high current heats neighbors, and noise from the switching motor wires couples into sensor lines. Twisted-pair-shielded for sensors, separate harness for power.
  • Use the power column, not chassis for any cable longer than ~0.5 m or any bundle. The chassis column assumes single wire in free air on a chassis, which is rarely the install case.
  • Hot environments derate further — the standard tables assume 30 °C ambient. Subtract another 20 % factor for 60 °C operation (under-hood or near motor housings).

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

  • V_drop = 2 · I · R · L   (round-trip drop)
  • R = ρ/A   (resistance per length)
  • I_derated = I_single · F_bundle
  • Drop % ≤ 2 % for power rails
  • Drop % ≤ 5 % for motors and non-critical loads

RULES OF THUMB

  • 2 wires → factor 0.80
  • 3 wires → factor 0.70
  • 4–6 → factor 0.60
  • 7–24 → factor 0.50
  • 25+ → factor 0.35
  • Hot environment → subtract 20 % more
  • Every 3 AWG steps doubles area, halves resistance

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Using chassis ampacity for a bundled wire — instant overheating. Use power column.
  • Ignoring the round trip — V_drop has a factor of 2 from out + back. Common arithmetic error.
  • Sizing only for steady-state — motor inrush can be 5–10× steady current for 100 ms. Wire passes thermally but voltage may sag.
  • Bundling power and signal — even if derating allows it, EMI coupling can corrupt sensor signals. Separate harnesses.
  • Forgetting temperature rise — Cu resistance rises 12 % at 50 °C ambient. Ampacity drops in hot bays.
