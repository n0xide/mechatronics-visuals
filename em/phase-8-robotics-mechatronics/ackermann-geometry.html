Ackermann Steering Geometry

A bicycle has one steerable wheel; a car has two, and they should turn by different angles. Geometrically, all wheels need to roll without sliding around a common turning center — so the inner wheel turns sharper than the outer. Get this wrong (parallel steering, like a child's wagon) and the tires scrub, generating heat, wearing rubber, and confusing kinematics. This visual shows the bicycle (simple) model, the Ackermann (correct) two-wheel geometry, and the parallel-steer scrub error.

The bicycle model — simplest version

Collapse both front wheels into a single steering wheel on the centerline, and both rear wheels into a single rear wheel. The geometry reduces to two collinear axles separated by wheelbase L, with the front axle turned by angle δ.

For the no-slip condition: tan(δ) = L / R Where R is turning radius (from ICR to rear axle). Rearranged: R = L / tan(δ) For small angles (δ in radians): R ≈ L / δ Connection to robot kinematics: ω = v/R, so v/ω = R = L/tan(δ).

The bicycle model is what every model-predictive-controller for cars and small autonomous vehicles starts with. It's wrong in the details (rear axle is two wheels with their own slip), but right in the topology — and good enough for path-planning above 1 m/s.

Ackermann — the two-wheel correction

When you split the bicycle's front wheel into two wheels on a track-width W axle, each wheel sits at a different distance from the ICR. The inner wheel (closer to the ICR) has a tighter turning radius and must turn through a larger steering angle. The outer wheel is at a larger radius and turns less.

Place ICR on the extended rear axle, distance R from centerline. Inner front wheel is at distance (R − W/2) from ICR; tan(δ_in) = L / (R − W/2) Outer front wheel is at distance (R + W/2) from ICR; tan(δ_out) = L / (R + W/2) → δ_in > δ_out always. Geometrically: extend each front wheel's axle; they intersect at the same ICR if Ackermann is perfect.

Mechanically, Ackermann steering is approximated by a four-bar linkage (Watt linkage variant) on the steering rack. The linkage is tuned so that the inner wheel rotates ~2–4° more than the outer at full lock. This is "100 % Ackermann"; real cars often run 50–80 % Ackermann (a compromise tuned for tire grip during cornering).

Parallel-steering scrub

If both front wheels turn by the same angle (parallel steering, like a wagon or shopping cart), they cannot both roll around a common ICR. The geometry forces one or both to slide sideways — "scrub."

This is fine at very low speeds for non-critical applications (shopping carts, slow forklifts). It's bad for cars: scrubbing tires generate heat (tire wear), make weird noises, and resist turning (steering torque).

Parallel-steer (both wheels at angle δ_avg): Inner wheel's natural angle would be δ_in > δ_avg; setting it to δ_avg means inner wheel slips by angle Δ = δ_in − δ_avg. Outer wheel's natural angle would be δ_out < δ_avg; same magnitude scrub but opposite direction. Scrub angle is small at large R, grows quickly at tight turns.

Worked example — small autonomous delivery robot

The vehicle

Small Ackermann-steered delivery robot. L = 0.6 m wheelbase, W = 0.4 m track width. Max steering angle (centerline equivalent) = 30°.

Minimum turning radius

At δ = 30°: R_min = 0.6 / tan(30°) = 0.6 / 0.577 = 1.04 m Useful for path planning constraint: cannot turn tighter than 1 m.

Wheel angles at full lock

Inner: δ_in = atan(0.6 / (1.04 − 0.2)) = atan(0.6 / 0.84) = atan(0.714) = 35.5° Outer: δ_out = atan(0.6 / (1.04 + 0.2)) = atan(0.6 / 1.24) = atan(0.484) = 25.8° Difference: 9.7° — significant; parallel steer would scrub badly here.

Linkage design

A standard Ackermann linkage: the steering arms angle inward so that extending each one would intersect at the centre of the rear axle, giving an arm angle of arctan((W/2)/L). For the worked example (L = 0.6 m, W = 0.4 m): arctan(0.2 / 0.6) ≈ 18.4°. The resulting trapezoidal four-bar arrangement causes the inner wheel to turn faster than the outer as the tie-rod translates.

Path planner constraint

For RRT/A* path planning, instead of treating the robot as omnidirectional, add a "curvature constraint" κ_max = 1/R_min = 1/1.04 = 0.96 m⁻¹. Reject any candidate path segment with curvature exceeding this.

What to notice

  • Ackermann is geometric, not dynamic. It assumes no slip. At high speed and high lateral g, the wheels actually slide (cornering slip angle), and Ackermann becomes wrong — race cars often use anti-Ackermann (outer wheel turns more) to compensate.
  • The bicycle model is good enough most of the time. Add Ackermann only when (a) the robot turns very tight (R close to W), or (b) you care about tire wear / odometry accuracy.
  • Steering rack vs steer-by-wire — mechanical racks fix Ackermann at design time; steer-by-wire systems can compute and apply individual angles dynamically, even varying them with speed.
  • Minimum turning radius is a hard constraint for autonomous driving. Path planners must respect it; sometimes a 3-point turn is unavoidable.
  • Articulated vehicles (semi-trucks) cheat — the trailer follows its own arc; the truck's effective turning radius is much smaller than a rigid car of the same length.

Where you'll see this

Real uses

  • Every car and truck — Ackermann steering linkage (or modern multi-link variants).
  • Autonomous delivery robots (Starship Tech, Nuro) — Ackermann at 1–4 mph.
  • RC cars and ride-on toys — usually parallel steering; cheap and good-enough at low speed.
  • Lawn tractors — Ackermann linkage.
  • F1 race cars — anti-Ackermann for high-speed dynamic grip.

RULES OF THUMB

  • Use bicycle model for control; Ackermann for analysis.
  • Real cars: ~50 % Ackermann (compromise).
  • R_min ≈ L for typical 30° max steer.
  • Parallel scrub is OK below 0.5 m/s; bad above.
  • R_min is the path-planner curvature constraint.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Degrees vs radians — atan2 returns radians, tan() expects radians.
  • Track measured wrong — center-to-center of tire contact patches.
  • Forgetting rear wheels — they too can be steered (4WS).
  • Curvature constraint ignored in path planner — paths become infeasible at corners.
  • Tire slip ≠ no-slip — model breaks above ~0.5 g lateral.
