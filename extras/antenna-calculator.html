Antenna Geometry Calculator

Frequency in, physical dimensions out. Every common antenna for sub-6 GHz IoT and amateur radio — λ/4 monopole, λ/2 dipole, axial-mode helix, rectangular patch, PIFA — together with the dielectric and end-effect corrections that decide whether your VSWR comes in at 1.2 or 3.5. Tab a geometry; type a frequency or hit a band preset; read the build dimensions and the radiation-pattern sketch.

The lesson under all of these calculations: an antenna is a resonator, not just a wire of a certain length. The classical formulas treat the conductor as infinitely thin, in free space, with no end-effect. Real antennas — finite wire diameter, dielectric coating, ground-plane proximity, nearby metal — resonate shorter than the textbook number. The 95 % factor on a λ/4 monopole isn't a fudge; it's the dominant end-effect correction. The same physics shrinks every PCB trace antenna you'll ever cut.

Wavelength & phase velocity

c = 299 792 458 m/s (exactly) λ = c / f 915 MHz → λ = 328 mm 2.45 GHz → λ = 122 mm 5.8 GHz → λ = 51.7 mm

Phase velocity & velocity factor (for cable)

In a dielectric of permittivity ε_r, the wave slows to v = c / √ε_r. The wavelength inside the dielectric shrinks by the same factor: λ_d = λ₀ / √ε_r. This is why a quarter-wave transmission-line section of coax is physically shorter than a quarter-wave of wire in air.

Microstrip — the ε_eff trick

A trace on a PCB sits between substrate (ε_r ≈ 4) and air (ε_r = 1) — the wave sees an effective permittivity between the two. The first-order Hammerstad approximation for W/h > 1:

ε_eff = (ε_r + 1)/2 + (ε_r − 1)/2 · (1 + 12·h/W)^(−1/2) where h = substrate height, W = trace width → for FR-4 (ε_r=4.3) with W/h ≈ 2: ε_eff ≈ 3.3 → guided wavelength: λ_g = λ₀ / √ε_eff ≈ 0.55·λ₀

Derivation — where λ = c/f comes from

1. A propagating sinusoid travels v meters in 1 second. 2. It completes f cycles in that second. 3. Therefore each cycle spans v/f meters in space. 4. In free space v = c, so → λ = c/f. 5. In dielectric v slows by 1/√ε_r → λ_d = λ₀/√ε_r.

λ/4 monopole (vertical / whip)

915 MHz → λ/4 = 82 mm; real ≈ 78 mm 433 MHz → λ/4 = 173 mm; real ≈ 164 mm 2.4 GHz → λ/4 = 31 mm; real ≈ 29 mm Feed: ~36 Ω over infinite ground Ground plane: ≥ λ/4 radius
Side view — quarter-wave whip over ground plane. The ground plane acts as a mirror; the whip's "image" below provides the missing half of a dipole.
What's happening: a single conductor cannot resonate by itself — it needs a return path. The ground plane provides that path via the image principle: the field above the plane looks identical to a full λ/2 dipole, with the bottom half replaced by the conductive image of the top half. That's why the feed-point impedance is ~36 Ω — exactly half of a dipole's 73 Ω.

The 95 % factor — end-effect correction

The textbook λ/4 length is derived for an infinitely thin conductor in free space, with the current going to zero exactly at the tip. Real wire has finite diameter and capacitance to its surroundings, which causes the current node to push back from the geometric end — equivalently, the effective electrical length is longer than the physical length. To resonate at the desired frequency, the physical length must be shortened:

L_physical = K · λ/4 K ≈ 0.95 ← typical for thin wire (λ/d > 100) K ≈ 0.92 ← chubbier wire or insulated K ≈ 0.98 ← very thin wire or open scaffolding → 915 MHz: 0.95 · 81.94 mm = 77.8 mm

Ground-plane requirement

For a monopole to behave like a λ/4 antenna, it needs a ground plane at its base that extends at least λ/4 radius. Smaller planes raise the feed impedance and tilt the radiation pattern upward — at λ/8 radius you lose ~2 dB and the impedance climbs to ~50 Ω (which can be convenient if you skip the matching network, but the radiation efficiency suffers).

A modern handheld radio uses your hand and body as the counterpoise — the antenna only works because you're holding it. This is why a 433 MHz keyfob has a barely-visible PCB trace antenna: the keyfob's ground pour plus your hand provide enough counterpoise to scrape together ~50 m of range.

Worked example — 915 MHz LoRa monopole on RYLR998

Reyax RYLR998 module on a cyberdeck: 915 MHz LoRa, IPEX antenna connector. The module datasheet calls for a 50 Ω whip; we'll cut one from 0.5 mm enameled copper wire.

λ = 299 792 458 / 915 000 000 = 0.3276 m = 327.6 mm λ/4 ideal = 81.94 mm (geometric quarter-wave) K = 0.95 for thin wire → L_physical = 0.95 · 81.94 = 77.84 mm Round to 78 mm with a slight long bias (easier to trim than to extend) Ground-plane radius ≥ λ/4 = 82 mm (use the cyberdeck's chassis bottom plate) Feed impedance ≈ 36 Ω → use a λ/4 transformer or a quick 50 Ω-to-36 Ω match

In practice for LoRa, you can ignore the impedance mismatch — the 1.4:1 VSWR from feeding a 36 Ω antenna with 50 Ω coax loses ~0.15 dB, dwarfed by the SF12-vs-SF7 trade. Just cut 78 mm and call it good.

λ/2 dipole (centre-fed)

915 MHz → λ/2 = 164 mm; real ≈ 156 mm (each arm 78 mm) 433 MHz → λ/2 = 346 mm; real ≈ 329 mm Feed Z ≈ 73 Ω (free space) Needs balun for unbalanced coax feed
Horizontal centre-fed dipole. Both arms are λ/4. Current is maximum at the centre (feedpoint), zero at the tips. Voltage is the opposite — maximum at the tips, zero at the centre.
Why 73 Ω? The radiation resistance of a thin half-wave dipole — the equivalent resistance that the antenna's radiated power "looks like" from the feedpoint — works out to 73.1 Ω by integrating the far-field intensity over a sphere. Add a small reactive part (+j42.5 Ω at exact λ/2), which is why dipoles are usually built 95 % long: shortening drops the reactance to near zero, making the match a clean 50 Ω with a quick 1.46:1 transformer (or nothing at all — VSWR 1.46:1 is only ~0.32 dB return loss).

Balun — why dipoles need one

A dipole is a balanced structure: equal-amplitude, opposite-polarity currents on each arm. Coax is unbalanced: the inner conductor carries the signal and the shield is the return. Feed a dipole directly with coax and current sneaks down the outside of the shield (it's a third conductor as far as the dipole sees), distorting the pattern and turning the coax run into part of the antenna.

Cure: a balun (balanced-to-unbalanced transformer). Two common types:

  • Choke balun (ugly balun) — coil 6–10 turns of the coax in a 100 mm loop right at the feedpoint. Cheap, lossy at HF, works fine 100–500 MHz.
  • 1:1 transformer balun — ferrite toroid with bifilar winding, transforming common-mode current to a high impedance while passing differential mode. Standard solution for VHF/UHF dipoles.
  • Half-wave coax balun — a λ/2 loop of coax connected feed-to-ground-to-feed, gives a 1:4 impedance step (73 Ω dipole → ~290 Ω, fed by 4 Ω... not usually used at 4:1, but the 1:1 variant is useful).

Helical antenna (axial mode)

Axial mode: C ≈ λ (circumference) Pitch: S ≈ λ/4 Gain ≈ 10·log(K·N·C²·S/λ³) dBi Polarization: circular (great for satellite, drone)
Helical antenna in axial (end-fire) mode — radiation peaks along the helix axis. Counter-clockwise winding gives LHCP, clockwise gives RHCP.

Axial-mode condition

The helix has two modes: normal mode (small helix, broadside radiation, linear polarization — like a coil-loaded antenna) and axial mode (helix dimensions comparable to λ, end-fire radiation, circular polarization). Axial mode is what you want for high gain, and requires:

3/4 < C/λ < 4/3 ← circumference within ~30 % of λ S ≈ λ/4 ← pitch a quarter wavelength α ≈ 12°–14° ← pitch angle = atan(S/C) N ≥ 4 ← below this gain drops & pattern broadens Ground plane ≥ 0.8·λ diameter, λ/8 above the first turn

Gain formula (Kraus)

The classical Kraus formula for axial-mode helical gain:

G ≈ K · N · (C/λ)² · (S/λ) ← linear (numeric) gain G_dB ≈ 10·log₁₀[K·N·(C/λ)²·(S/λ)] ← in dBi over isotropic K ≈ 12 ← classical Kraus value (slightly optimistic, real measurements ~10–12) → for L1 GPS (1575 MHz) with N=10, D=60.6 mm, S=47.6 mm: ~13 dBi

Worked example — L1 GPS helical (1575.42 MHz)

λ = c / f = 299 792 458 / 1.57542 × 10⁹ = 190.3 mm C target = λ = 190.3 mm → D = C/π = 60.6 mm S target = λ/4 = 47.6 mm → pitch angle α = atan(47.6/190.3) = 14.0° For 10-turn helix, axial length L = N·S = 476 mm G ≈ 12 · 10 · 1² · 0.25 = 30 numeric → 14.8 dBi Beam-width (half-power) ≈ 52° / √(N · C/λ · S/λ) ≈ 52° / √2.5 = 33° Feed Z ≈ 140 · C/λ = 140 Ω → match to 50 Ω with λ/4 transformer of √(140·50)=84 Ω stripline

Patch antenna (rectangular microstrip)

W ≈ c / (2·fr · √((ε_r+1)/2)) L ≈ λ_g/2 − 2·Δl Δl ≈ 0.412·h · fringing function Feed Z (edge) ≈ 200–300 Ω — needs inset or λ/4 match
Top view of rectangular patch on substrate. Width W controls feed impedance and bandwidth; length L controls resonant frequency. The two radiating slots are at the W-edges.

Why W and L are decoupled

Width and length play different roles:

  • L sets the resonant frequency — the patch resonates when L ≈ λ_g/2, where λ_g = λ₀/√ε_eff is the wavelength in the dielectric. The guided wavelength is what fits in the patch, not free-space λ.
  • W sets the impedance and the radiation efficiency — wider patches have lower edge impedance (W → ∞ takes Z to ~120 Ω; narrow patches push it to 400+ Ω) and slightly more bandwidth. The classical choice W ≈ c/(2·fr · √((ε_r+1)/2)) gives a reasonable compromise.
  • h sets bandwidth — thicker substrate (more h) widens bandwidth (typ. 1–5 %) but also increases surface-wave loss and decreases efficiency above ~h/λ > 0.05.

Standard design formulas (Balanis)

Step 1. Choose W = c/(2·fr) · √(2/(ε_r+1)) ← compromise width Step 2. ε_eff = (ε_r+1)/2 + (ε_r−1)/2 · (1+12·h/W)^(−1/2) Step 3. Δl = 0.412·h · (ε_eff+0.3)·(W/h+0.264) / [(ε_eff−0.258)·(W/h+0.8)] Step 4. L_eff = c/(2·fr·√ε_eff) ← required electrical half-wavelength Step 5. L = L_eff − 2·Δl ← physical length, shortened by fringing on both ends

Feeding a patch

The patch edge impedance is typically 200–300 Ω — way off from a 50 Ω feed. Three common matching techniques:

MethodHowTrade-off
Inset feedCut a notch into the patch, feed at depth y from the edge: Z(y) = Z_edge · cos²(πy/L)Simple, no extra components
λ/4 transformerQuarter-wave strip of √(Z_edge · 50) Ω feeds the patch edgeAdds length, narrows BW slightly
Coaxial probeDrill through substrate, feed from below at point of matching impedanceBest for low-profile; tricky to manufacture cheaply
Aperture couplingSlot in ground plane couples to feed line below; patch on topWider BW, cleaner pattern, more PCB layers

Worked example — 2.44 GHz Wi-Fi patch on FR-4

f_r = 2440 MHz, ε_r = 4.3, h = 1.6 mm (standard 1.6 mm FR-4) W = (3·10⁸)/(2·2.44·10⁹) · √(2/5.3) = 61.5 mm · 0.614 = 37.7 mm ε_eff = 2.65 + 1.65 · (1 + 12·1.6/37.7)^(−1/2) = 2.65 + 1.65·0.78 = 3.94 Δl = 0.412·1.6 · (4.24·24.83/(3.68·24.36)) = 0.660 · 1.180 = 0.778 mm L_eff = c/(2·fr·√ε_eff) = 61.5/√3.94 = 30.98 mm L = 30.98 − 2·0.778 = 29.4 mm Patch outline: 37.7 × 29.4 mm on FR-4 1.6 mm — Wi-Fi access-point internal antenna size

Compare against a 5.8 GHz patch on the same FR-4: W=15.9 mm, L=12.4 mm — small enough to fit four of them in a 2×2 array on a small PCB.

PIFA — Planar Inverted-F Antenna

Total path: L + W ≈ λ/4 Height above ground: h = 3–10 mm typical BW: 2–8 % (more with thicker h) Every smartphone uses one
PIFA side view — top patch suspended height h above ground plane, shorted at one corner, fed slightly inboard. Length + width sum to ≈ λ/4 because the short-circuit reflects the wave back.

What makes the F-shape "inverted"

A conventional L-antenna is a wire folded once (an "L"). An inverted-F adds a second bend and a shorting pin from the radiator to the ground plane — that's the "F" lying on its side. The shorting pin reflects the standing wave, so the resonant length is halved compared to a λ/4 monopole: L + W ≈ λ/4, not λ/2.

This is the dominant low-profile antenna in modern consumer electronics — phones, tablets, IoT modules. The Espressif ESP32 modules with ceramic chip antennas are pre-tuned PIFA variants packaged in 0805 SMD form.

Tuning levers

  • Length L + W — the main resonance knob. Total electrical path from feed to far corner sets f_r.
  • Feed position — moves edge impedance from 5 Ω (near short) up to 200 Ω (far edge). Sweet spot ~15 % of L from short = ~50 Ω.
  • Short width — narrower short raises impedance; thicker lowers it.
  • h (gap to ground) — wider gap = more bandwidth (better matching tolerance) but more height.
  • Slot in the patch — a single slot cut in L lets you tune two bands at once (e.g., 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi + 5 GHz Wi-Fi sharing one antenna).

Worked example — 2.44 GHz PIFA on a 50×30 mm IoT board

λ = 122.9 mm → λ/4 = 30.7 mm L + W = 30.7 mm → choose L = 18 mm, W = 12.7 mm (rectangular, sits along the board edge) h = 6 mm air gap (lifted by plastic spacers above the ground plane) Feed at ~2.7 mm from the short → ~50 Ω edge impedance Ground plane: 50×30 mm is borderline-OK at 2.4 GHz (≈0.4·λ × 0.24·λ) — gain drops ~1.5 dB vs an infinite plane

For 433 MHz the same geometry would need L + W ≈ 173 mm — way too big for a small board. That's why 433 MHz IoT modules use external wire whips, while 2.4 GHz IoT modules use on-board PIFAs / chip antennas.

Radiation patterns — visual intuition

A radiation pattern is the 3-D distribution of radiated power as a function of direction. Sketches below are the canonical far-field shapes — what you'd measure on an anechoic-chamber rotator. Real patterns get dented by ground reflections, nearby metal, and your hand.

λ/2 dipole — donut (toroid)
Side view: figure-of-8. End view: omnidirectional. Max gain perpendicular to wire axis. Nulls along wire ends. Gain = 2.15 dBi.
λ/4 monopole over ground — half-donut
Side view: upper half of dipole pattern (ground plane reflects). Top view: omnidirectional. Gain = 5.15 dBi over infinite ground (+3 dB vs dipole — same energy into half the volume).
Patch — broadside dome
Side view: hemisphere centred normal to patch face. Top view: circular but narrower along patch length. Gain ≈ 6–9 dBi. Backside is shielded by ground plane (−15 to −25 dB).
Helix (axial) — pencil beam
Side view: narrow cone along axis. Top view: narrow circle. Gain ≈ 10–18 dBi depending on N. Circular polarization (CP) — receives any linear orientation with 3 dB loss.
Yagi-Uda (for context) — directional
Driven dipole + reflector + directors. Forward gain ≈ 10–17 dBi. Front-to-back ratio ≈ 15–25 dB. Not in this calculator, but the canonical "fat arrow" lobe.
Isotropic radiator — sphere
The mathematical reference (does not physically exist). 0 dBi by definition. All other gains are quoted "vs isotropic" (dBi) or "vs a dipole" (dBd, where 0 dBd = 2.15 dBi).

QUICK MENTAL MATH

  • 300 / f_MHz = λ in m — the engineer's shortcut. 300/915 = 0.328 m.
  • 75 / f_MHz = λ/4 in m — 75/915 = 0.082 m = 82 mm ideal whip.
  • Multiply by 0.95 for real-wire end-effect.
  • ISM cheat sheet: 433 MHz=173 mm whip · 868 MHz=82 mm · 915 MHz=78 mm · 2.4 GHz=29 mm · 5.8 GHz=12 mm.
  • Dipole = 2× monopole length; PIFA = monopole bent in half (L+W=λ/4).
  • Patch on FR-4 at 2.44 GHz ≈ 30×38 mm — memorize this one.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • No ground plane = high VSWR — a monopole on bare wire with nothing under it radiates poorly and reflects power back into the PA. Always provide ≥ λ/4 radius counterpoise.
  • Nearby metal detunes the antenna — a metal screw 5 mm from a 2.4 GHz patch can shift f_r by 100 MHz. Datasheet "antenna keep-out" zones are not optional.
  • Antenna ≠ "just a wire that long" — wrong diameter, wrong dielectric, wrong end-effect factor → resonance at the wrong frequency → bad VSWR → wasted TX power and degraded RX.
  • 50 Ω is convention, not law — monopole feed is 36 Ω, dipole 73 Ω, patch edge 200+ Ω. Ignore at low cost in IoT (the loss is tenths of a dB); pay attention in high-gain or PA-limited systems.
  • Polarization mismatch costs 20+ dB — vertical antenna receiving a horizontal signal is essentially deaf. Two CP antennas of opposite handedness lose 20 dB.
  • Trace inductance from the feed pad adds up — at 2.4 GHz, 5 mm of unmatched 50 Ω trace before the antenna looks like ~3 nH of series L = j2.5 Ω. Tolerable; 50 mm is not.

WHEN TO USE WHAT

  • 433/868 MHz IoT — λ/4 whip (cheap, simple). Helical loaded whip if size-constrained.
  • 2.4 GHz IoT — PIFA on PCB or chip antenna SMD. Patch if you want directionality.
  • 5–6 GHz Wi-Fi — chip antenna, PIFA, or patch array. Easy to make small.
  • GPS L1 1.575 GHz — patch (cheap, ceramic) or helix (for handheld). CP either way.
  • Drone telemetry — λ/4 whip on the drone, λ/4 whip or short Yagi on the ground station.
  • LoRa long-haul — λ/4 whip on the node, λ/2 dipole or 6 dBi collinear on the gateway.
  • Satellite comm — circular polarization mandatory: helical, crossed-Yagi, or patch with quadrature feed.

Where this fits in the bigger picture

  • Geometry calculator gets you 90 % there — the formulas in this tool will land you at f_r within ±5 % on the first build. The last 5 % is tuning: trim wire, adjust feed offset, watch VSWR on a NanoVNA.
  • VSWR / matching is the next step — once you've cut the right length, you may still need an L/T-match to land 50 Ω at the PA. See matching-smith.html for the Smith-chart workflow.
  • Link budget closes the loop — TX power − cable loss + TX gain + RX gain − path loss vs RX sensitivity. The antenna gains feed into link-budget.html.
  • For LoRa specifically: a 78 mm whip on a 915 MHz module, fed by 50 Ω coax, with a 100×100 mm metal backplate, will give you a clean 1.3:1 VSWR and ~3 dBi gain at the horizon. Combined with 14 dBm TX and SF12, that's ~10 km outdoor line-of-sight. See lora-modulation.html.
