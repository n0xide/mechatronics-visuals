Voltage discharge curves (1 cell, normalized)

The shape of the discharge curve tells you whether you can use battery voltage as a state-of-charge gauge. LiPo / Li-ion sag smoothly from 4.2 V to 3.0 V — you can map V → SoC with ±5 % accuracy via a 6-point table. LiFePO4 is flat (~3.3 V across 80 % of capacity), so V is useless as a fuel gauge — you need coulomb counting. NiMH is also nearly flat, same problem.

Capacity ↔ runtime calculator

The naive formula is runtime_hours = capacity_Ah / load_A. It's wrong at high loads and cold temps. Peukert's equation is the correction: the higher the discharge rate, the less usable capacity you get. Most acute in lead-acid (k = 1.1–1.3); moderate in Li-ion (k ≈ 1.05); negligible in LiFePO4 (k ≈ 1.02).

Peukert's equation — why heavy loads cheat you

Naive: t = C / I (treating C as fixed capacity) Reality: high current → faster ion transport → polarization → cell internal heating → less usable capacity. Peukert: → t = (C / I^k) · (1 A)^(k−1) — capacity falls off as I rises Equivalently: C_effective = C · (I_ref / I)^(k−1) where I_ref is the rate at which C was specified (usually C/20 for lead-acid, 1C for Li).

For lithium chemistries the effect is small — capacity is rated at 1C or 0.5C and stays within ~5% to 2–3C. For lead-acid the effect is dramatic: a 100 Ah deep-cycle battery rated at 20-hour rate delivers maybe 60 Ah at the 1-hour rate. Cold temperatures make Peukert k worse for all chemistries; below 0 °C lithium loses 20–40 % usable capacity.

CC/CV charging — the two-phase Li-ion algorithm

Every lithium charger IC (TP4056, MCP73831, BQ24074, MAX1555 family) implements the same two-phase profile because the chemistry demands it. Constant Current until the cell hits 4.20 V; then Constant Voltage at 4.20 V until current tails to a small fraction of the initial rate. The 4.20 V target — why exactly that?

Cobalt-oxide cathode (NMC, NCA, LCO) starts evolving oxygen above ~4.25 V at the positive electrode. The 4.20 V target is the standard safe ceiling — every datasheet specifies ±10 mV (so 4.20 to 4.21 max). Some "high-voltage" Li-ion goes to 4.35 or 4.40 V for ~10 % more capacity at the cost of cycle life. Never set the CV target by hand — use a charger IC.

How the duty-cycle average works

Per-component: I_avg = I_active · D + I_sleep · (1 − D) Where D = duty cycle (0 to 1) — the fraction of time the part is in active mode. Total system: I_system = Σ I_avg(i) (sum over all components) Add the regulator overhead: I_battery = I_system / η_reg · (V_out / V_pack) Runtime: → t = C_pack · DoD / I_battery

Battery protection IC — what a BMS actually does

A lithium pack without protection is a fire and a brick waiting to happen. A "BMS" (Battery Management System) is one or more ICs that monitor each cell and disconnect the pack on fault. For a 1S LiPo: a single DW01 or AP9101 + dual N-FET. For 3S–8S: a TI BQ7693x / BQ40z80 / similar with per-cell taps.

OVP Over-voltage protect — cell above 4.25 V UVP Under-voltage protect — cell below 2.5 V OCP Over-current protect — discharge above limit SCP Short-circuit protect — usually < 1 ms OTP Over-temperature protect — NTC monitored Balance bleed current from high cell

3S BMS topology — schematic view

Three cells in series, four wire taps (B−, BAT1, BAT2, B+). Each cell has a voltage divider monitored by the BMS chip. A bleed FET + resistor across each cell can drop the high-cell during balance. A pair of back-to-back N-FETs on the discharge path can open on any fault. The current shunt (10–50 mΩ) is read by the BMS for OCP and coulomb counting.

Balance bleed math

Cell A: 4.18 V — cell B: 4.20 V — ΔV = 20 mV at top of charge. Bleed resistor on cell B: typically 33–100 Ω. With 33 Ω: I_bleed = 4.20 / 33 = 127 mA. To drop B by 20 mV needs ~ΔV · C_cell / I_bleed = 0.020 · 2.5 Ah / 0.127 A = 0.39 h ≈ 24 min. Real-world: balance happens during the CV tail, when charge current is small. Balancing is slow; first few cycles take a week of cumulative balance time.

What a BMS does NOT do

  • Reverse-polarity protection — connecting the pack backwards to a charger will fry the BMS and/or cells. Most BMS chips have no reverse-V tolerance on the charger input.
  • Cell matching at assembly — a BMS keeps cells balanced after manufacture, but if you build a 3S pack out of cells with wildly different IR (internal resistance) or capacity, no BMS rescues you. Capacity-match within 5 %, IR within 20 %.
  • Charge-rate limiting — most simple BMS chips pass through whatever current the charger supplies. The charger IC (e.g., TP4056) is what limits I_charge to 1C.
  • Voltage regulation for the load — the BMS gives you raw pack voltage. A buck/boost regulator separately drops it to 3.3 V or 5 V.

C-rate sanity check — can your pack push the burst current?

"C" is shorthand for current = capacity. A 2500 mAh cell at 1C delivers 2.5 A; at 10C, 25 A; at 0.1C, 250 mA. Each chemistry has a rated continuous and burst C-rate; exceeding it overheats the cell, dents cycle life, or triggers BMS cutoff. What the rating actually means

"50C burst" on a LiPo datasheet means peak current = 50 × capacity, sustained for ~10 seconds, at 25 °C. The fine print: burst rate halves below 0 °C, the rating drops 20 % over the cell's life, and "marketing C-rate" on cheap packs can be 2–3× optimistic. For a serious design, derate by 50 %: a 50C-rated cell should be sized as if it were 25C.

The arithmetic

Subtotal at 3.3 V rail: I_load = 44.1 mA, P_load = 44.1 mA × 3.3 V = 145.5 mW Buck efficiency η = 0.85 typical at this current. I_battery = 145.5 mW / (3.7 V × 0.85) = 46.3 mA Usable capacity: 1500 mAh × 0.80 DoD = 1200 mAh Runtime = 1200 mAh / 46.3 mA = 25.9 hours comfortably above 8h target Margin for "what if" loads (extra Wi-Fi, full-brightness display): even at 100 mA continuous draw, runtime = 1200 / 100 = 12 h Energy stored: 1.5 Ah × 3.7 V = 5.55 Wh = 19.98 kJ ≈ 5 minutes of microwave at full power Charge time @ TP4056 (500 mA = 0.33C): t_cc ≈ 90 min CC + ~40 min CV = ~130 min full cycle

Why this checks out

The dominant load is the Pico SoC plus its display backlight — Wi-Fi is bursty enough to be negligible in the average, but worth tracking because the peak draw during a Wi-Fi burst (~120 mA total system) lands at 0.08 C — far below any C-rate concern even for a low-end LiPo. The 25.9 h headline is conservative because it assumes the display is on 60 % of the time; with a typical 5-minute screen timeout the real-world runtime is closer to 40 h.

ANCHORS TO MEMORIZE

  • 1 LiPo cell = 3.0–4.2 V, 3.7 V nominal
  • 18650 = 2500–3500 mAh, 9–13 Wh, 47 g
  • 1S 1500 mAh = 5.55 Wh ≈ 5 min microwave
  • LiFePO4 cell = 2.5–3.65 V, 3.2 V nominal
  • NiMH = 1.2 V nominal, 1.0 V cutoff
  • Lead-acid cell = 2.0 V nominal, 6 cells = 12 V
  • 1C of 1000 mAh = 1 A (memorize the unit collapse)
  • Charging tail at C/20 termination is standard

COMMON PITFALLS

  • mAh ≠ Wh — always multiply by V_nominal to compare across chemistries. A 1000 mAh 1S LiPo (3.7 Wh) and a 1000 mAh 3S LiPo (11.1 Wh) are not the same battery.
  • Peak ≠ average — sizing the pack on peak load wastes capacity; sizing on average ignores the BMS OCP. Use peak for C-rate, average for runtime.
  • DC bias derating does NOT apply — that's a ceramic-capacitor thing. Battery capacity DOES vary with temperature (–20 % at 0 °C for Li-ion) and load (Peukert) — but not with applied DC voltage.
  • "50C burst" is marketing — derate cheap LiPo by 2× before sizing. Use the data sheet's continuous rating with 50 % margin.
  • Don't forget the regulator — a 3.3 V load drawing 100 mA from a 3.7 V LiPo through an 85 % buck draws ~104 mA from the battery, not 100. Step-up from 3.7 V to 5 V at 70 % efficiency draws ~190 mA — much worse.
  • Self-discharge for NiMH is huge — 20 % per month means a fully-charged NiMH AA holder is at 50 % in 3 months on the shelf. Use low-self-discharge cells (Eneloop) if shelf time matters.
  • Charging in cold weather damages Li — never charge a Li-ion cell below 0 °C; lithium plating is permanent. Discharge to 0 °C is fine, but capacity drops.
