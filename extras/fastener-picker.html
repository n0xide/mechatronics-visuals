How tap-drill diameter is computed

Tap-drill dia (75 % engagement) = D_major − 1.08253 · pitch · (engagement fraction) → for 75 % engagement: D_tap ≈ D_major − 0.812 · pitch → Example M3: 3.0 − 0.812·0.5 = 2.59 mm → nearest stock drill = 2.5 mm Higher engagement (closer to 100 %) gives marginal strength gain but exponential tap-breakage risk. 75 % is the universal default; 65 % is sometimes used in tough stainless to save the tap.
  • Coarse pitch is the default — if a print just says "M6" with no pitch, it means M6 × 1.0 (coarse). Fine pitch is always called out explicitly as M6 × 0.75.
  • Tap drill ≠ clearance drill. A tap drill is for cutting threads — it leaves the minor-diameter hole. A clearance drill is for the bolt to pass through — it must be larger than the major diameter, by 0.1–0.5 mm depending on fit grade.
  • H12 / H13 / H14 are ISO 273 fit grades. H12 = close fit (precision alignment), H13 = normal (most general work), H14 = loose (slotted holes, thermal expansion).
  • Pitch diameter is the diameter at which thread width = gap width — used for strip-strength and torque calculations, not for drilling.
  • Stress area is what you multiply by tensile strength to get bolt capacity — slightly larger than minor-diameter area because the thread root carries some load.

Property class + tightening torque

The little numbers stamped on a bolt head (4.8, 8.8, 10.9, 12.9, or A2-70) are the property class — they set ultimate tensile strength and proof load. The tightening torque T = K · F · d converts your wrench setting into clamp-force preload F, where K is a friction factor (0.2 dry, 0.15 lubed) and d is the nominal diameter.

4.8 → 400 MPa UTS · cheap mild steel 8.8 → 800 MPa · most common structural 10.9 → 1040 MPa (rule: 10×100 = 1000 nominal; ISO 898-1 min = 1040) · high-strength 12.9 → 1220 MPa (rule: 12×100 = 1200 nominal; ISO 898-1 min = 1220) · highest commodity grade A2-70 → 700 MPa · stainless 304

Worked example — M3 × 12 mm class 8.8, dry

Spec: stress area A_s = 5.03 mm². Class 8.8 → proof stress 580 MPa, UTS 800 MPa. Target 75 % of proof.

F_proof = A_s · σ_proof = 5.03 · 580 = 2917 N ≈ 2.92 kN F_target = 0.75 · 2917 = 2188 N ≈ 2.19 kN // 75 % preload is the standard safe target T = K · F · d = 0.20 · 2188 · 0.003 = 1.31 N·m

So torque the M3 8.8 bolt to ~1.3 N·m dry. A factory torque wrench typically reads ±10 %, but assembly variation pushes the actual clamp force into a ±25 % band — that's why you never preload above 75 % of proof.

Pitfalls

  • Dry K on a lubed bolt over-torques by ~33 % → snap or strip.
  • Stainless galling — A2/A4 on itself cold-welds at the thread; always use anti-seize or moly.
  • Titanium galls worse than stainless; mandatory dry-film lube.
  • Re-using stretched bolts for critical joints (head bolts, conrod bolts) — single-use, replace.
  • Class 12.9 in wet environments — high hardness → hydrogen embrittlement; use 10.9 or stainless.

Thread engagement length

The minimum thread engagement is set by the rule that the threads must fail after the bolt body — otherwise the threads strip and the bolt pulls out before reaching its rated strength. The engagement scales with the strength ratio between bolt material and tapped material.

Steel into steel ≥ 1.0 × d Steel into cast iron ≥ 1.25 × d Steel into aluminum ≥ 2.0 × d Steel into brass / Mg ≥ 2.0 × d Steel into plastic use insert!

Why 1× d for steel-into-steel works

Bolt tensile capacity = A_s · σ_uts Thread shear capacity (internal thread, the tapped hole) = π · D_p · L_e · 0.5·σ_shear → set thread shear > bolt tensile, solve for L_e For steel/steel with σ_shear ≈ 0.6·σ_uts (same material), the algebra collapses to L_e ≈ 0.8·d — engineers round up to 1.0·d for safety + manufacturing tolerance. For aluminum tapped (σ_uts_al ≈ 270 MPa) vs steel bolt (σ_uts ≈ 800 MPa) the ratio is 3, so L_e ≈ 2.4·d — round to 2·d minimum, 2.5·d for vibration.
  • Bottoming clearance — when tapping a blind hole, leave 2–3 thread pitches at the bottom unthreaded; otherwise the tap bottoms before reaching full depth.
  • Through-hole engagement is set by stack-up, not by the rule — a nut on the back side gives nut-engagement (specified by ISO standard nut height = ~0.8·d).
  • Tapped hole in a printed part — even with 2× engagement, PLA cut threads fail in fatigue. Use a brass heat-set insert (see Inserts tab).

UNC / UNF imperial thread

US fastener specs: #N-TPI for small sizes ("#4-40" = number-4 body diameter, 40 threads per inch), and fraction"-TPI for sizes ≥ 1/4". UNC is coarse, UNF is fine. Tap-drill diameters come from the same 75 %-engagement rule as metric, but everything is in inches.

Reading the number sizes

Number-size body dia = 0.060" + N · 0.013" → #2 = 0.086" · #4 = 0.112" · #6 = 0.138" · #8 = 0.164" · #10 = 0.190" After #10, sizing switches to fractions: 1/4", 5/16", 3/8", 7/16", 1/2", 9/16", 5/8", 3/4", 7/8", 1". There's no #11 / #12 (rare in modern use); commonly you'll see #2, #4, #6, #8, #10, then 1/4".
  • UNC vs UNF — UNC (coarse) is the default; UNF (fine) has more threads per inch, more strength in tension, less self-loosening resistance. Aerospace tends to UNF, general industrial UNC.
  • SAE numbered drills — the tap drill is often a number- or letter-size, not a fractional inch. #43 (0.0890") for #4-40 tap, F (0.257") for 1/4-20 clearance. Buy a tap-drill set.
  • Imperial-to-metric tap substitutes — #4-40 ≈ M3 (slightly bigger body, finer pitch); #10-32 ≈ M5 fine; 1/4-20 ≈ M6 coarse. Not interchangeable for any structural joint.

For new aluminum designs that will be serviced repeatedly (engine castings, aircraft brackets), spec helicoils from the start — don't wait for the first stripped thread.

When to spec what

  • 3D-printed bracket (PLA / PETG / ABS / PA-CF) → heat-set brass insert, always. Cut threads in PLA strip in 5–10 cycles; PETG is slightly better but still inferior. PA-CF (nylon CF) holds threads better but still benefits from inserts for repeated assembly.
  • Aluminum bracket, new design, repeatedly serviced → spec helicoils up front. Tap with STI tap, install at assembly. Cost is ~3× a plain tap but saves the part from being scrap when threads strip.
  • Aluminum bracket, one-off, threads stripped at field → drill out to STI tap drill, helicoil repair. This is what helicoils were invented for.
  • Sheet-metal box with M3/M4 thread → riv-nut or PEM stud. You can't tap 0.6 mm sheet usefully.
  • Mil-spec / aerospace → Keensert. Key-locked, doesn't back out, FAA-approved for thread repair.
  • Production injection-moulded → ultrasonic / heat-press insert is designed in; the moulded boss is sized for press-in or melt-in.

Washer + nut decision matrix

The default joint is just flat washer + plain nut. Add layers (lock, Belleville, Nyloc, castle) only when a specific failure mode demands it. Most "I added a lock washer to be safe" is folklore that does nothing or makes the joint worse.

Vibration-locking myths

  • Split lock washer is mostly placebo — under proper preload it flattens. NASA banned it for spaceflight.
  • Two flat washers stacked for "more load spread" actually allows them to spin against each other under torque — use one wider washer instead.
  • Loctite + Nyloc = redundant. Pick one; both is overkill and complicates re-assembly.
  • Nord-Lock + Nyloc is overkill; either alone is enough.

Real anti-vibration solutions

  • Higher preload — most vibration loosening is from preload loss. T = K·F·d to ~75 % of proof.
  • Nord-Lock wedge pair — independent of preload; physically locks.
  • Nyloc / all-metal lock nut — adequate for normal vibration; cheap.
  • Castle nut + cotter pin — mechanical positive lock for rotating-shaft retention.
  • Thread-locker (Loctite 243 blue) — bonds the thread; cheap insurance.

Star-pattern (crisscross) tightening sequence

When a joint has multiple bolts on a flange — engine head, motor mount, exhaust manifold, brake caliper — tightening them in sequence around the perimeter twists the flange unevenly and warps the gasket. The crisscross pattern distributes preload symmetrically: tighten opposite or near-opposite bolts in turn so the gasket compresses evenly.

Step 0 — no bolts yet

Bolts shaded green are torqued in the current pass; orange shows the next bolt to tighten; the dashed line traces the sequence so far.

Why multi-pass?

Single-pass full torque on bolt 1 compresses the gasket locally → adjacent area lifts, the next bolt's torque now also closes that gap before it ever pulls the flange flat. Multi-pass low-torque sequence first seats the gasket evenly. Each bolt gets enough torque to pull its area down, but not enough to lock it. → Pass 1 at 30 % seats the gasket. → Pass 2 at 60 % flattens any remaining unevenness. → Pass 3 at 100 % brings every bolt to final preload — they're all pulling against an already-flat surface. → Pass 4 (re-check, after thermal cycle / gasket creep) — engine head, exhaust, anything with hot gasket.

Special cases

  • Engine head bolts — modern engines use stretch bolts (Torque-to-Yield, TTY): tighten to ~30 N·m, then turn an additional 90° + 90° + 90°. Replace bolts each time the head comes off; they don't recover.
  • Wheel lug nuts — 5- or 6-lug, crisscross (1, 3, 5, 2, 4 for 5-lug; star). Re-torque after 80 km / 50 mi to catch the lug-seat bedding-in.
  • Exhaust manifolds — tighten cold, retorque warm; thermal cycling warps cast-iron manifolds.
  • Brake calipers — 2 bolts: just torque both in sequence; calipers don't compress a gasket. But always use the torque spec — caliper bolts are stretch-design.
  • Bearing housings with O-ring seal — crisscross matters; the O-ring needs even compression to seal radially.
