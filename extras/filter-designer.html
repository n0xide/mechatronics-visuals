First-order passive RC

One R, one C, one corner frequency. The whole story is fc = 1 / (2π·R·C). The slider sets the target; we pick the closest E12 R / E12 C and report the realized fc plus the error.

|H(jω)| (dB) ∠H(jω) (degrees) realized fc (−3 dB)

How the formula falls out

1. Cap impedance: Z_C = 1 / (jωC) 2. Low-pass (R series, C shunt) is a voltage divider with Z_C and R: H = Z_C / (R + Z_C) 3. Substitute and clean up: → H(jω) = 1 / (1 + jωRC) 4. Magnitude is |H| = 1 / √(1 + (ωRC)²); the −3 dB point is where ωRC = 1: → fc = 1 / (2π·R·C) 5. Phase: ∠H = −arctan(ωRC). At fc, phase = −45°. At 10·fc, phase ≈ −84°.

Rolloff

−20 dB / decade past fc. That's "first order." Doubling frequency drops gain 6 dB. To go faster you need more poles — Sallen-Key, MFB, or a cascade. Stay in the RC tab if all you need is anti-aliasing for a slow ADC sampling 10× faster than your signal of interest.

Passive RLC bandpass

L and C set f0; R sets the damping. The series-RLC bandpass takes the voltage across R as the output; high Q means a sharp peak, low Q means a gentle dome. Critical damping (Q = 0.5) is the edge between "no peaking" and "peaks above unity."

|H(jω)| (dB) ∠H(jω) (degrees) realized f0

What Q is, geometrically

Q is the ratio of f0 to the −3 dB bandwidth. Q = 10 means the −3 dB bandwidth is 10 % of f0. Q is also (1/R)·√(L/C) — change any one of those and you change selectivity. To pick a real circuit: choose Q (selectivity), then f0, then commit to an inductor value you can actually buy.

f0 = 1 / (2π·√(LC)) → C = 1 / ((2π·f0)²·L) Q = (1/R)·√(L/C) → R = √(L/C) / Q = 2π·f0·L / Q Critical Q (no peak above unity in 2nd-order LP form): Q ≤ 0.5 "Maximally flat" (Butterworth) Q: Q = 1/√2 ≈ 0.707 Bandwidth: Δf = f0 / Q

Real inductors are not ideal

Air-core / chip inductors have parasitic DCR (series resistance) that floors Q. A 100 µH chip inductor with 0.8 Ω DCR at 10 kHz has XL = 6.28 Ω, so the inductor alone gives QL ≈ 7.85 before you add anything. You can't get Q = 20 out of it. Above ~10 MHz, switch to LC tank with parallel mode, or move to an active filter where the L is gone entirely.

Sallen-Key (2nd-order active)

Op-amp configured as a non-inverting buffer with a positive-feedback path through two RCs. No inductor needed — the op-amp synthesizes the second pole. Easy to design with equal R / equal C (set Q purely with the gain) or unity-gain (set Q purely with the R/C ratio).

|H| (dB) ∠H (deg) fc (−3 dB at Q = 0.707)

Equal-R, equal-C derivation (LP)

Set R1 = R2 = R, C1 = C2 = C. Then ω0 = 1 / (RC), so fc = 1 / (2π·RC). Q is set purely by the op-amp gain K = 1 + Rb/Ra: Q = 1 / (3 − K). Invert: K = 3 − 1/Q. So Butterworth (Q = 0.707) needs K = 1.586; gain of ~1.59. Trade: high Q (Q ≥ 5) makes K approach 3, so the op-amp must be picked for closed-loop stability and low offset. Switch to MFB or to a unity-gain Sallen-Key with mismatched R/C if Q > ~10.

Op-amp pick rules

  • GBW ≥ 100 · fc — open-loop rolloff at fc must be at least 40 dB above the closed-loop gain so the in-band response isn't shaped by the op-amp itself.
  • Slew rate ≥ 2π · fc · Vpeak — full-scale sinusoid at fc must not slew. A 5 Vpk sine at 100 kHz needs > 3.14 V/µs.
  • Input offset — for DC-coupled designs (active LP feeding ADC), pick Vos < ½ LSB at your ADC resolution.
  • Stable single-supply candidates: TLV9001 (1 MHz), OPA376 (5.5 MHz), OPA1655 (50 MHz audio). For higher-fc bands use ADA4807 or LM7171.

Multiple-Feedback (MFB) active filter

Inverting op-amp topology with two feedback paths. Lower component sensitivity than Sallen-Key — a 1 % cap mismatch drifts Q by ~½ % instead of ~1 %. Inverts the signal (180° at DC). Preferred for sharp filters, especially Chebyshev sections.

|H| (dB) ∠H (deg) f0

Why MFB beats Sallen-Key for high Q

  • Lower sensitivity — Q sensitivity to capacitor mismatch is ½ for MFB vs. nearly 1 for Sallen-Key with equal-R/C. Real production filters with 5 % caps land where you designed them.
  • Op-amp loading is fixed — output drives R2 alone, not a complex network feeding back to the +input. Easier to predict closed-loop stability.
  • Inverting — a wash for AC; a problem for DC level shifts. Cascade two MFB sections to recover positive polarity if you need it.
  • Worse at very low Q (Q < 0.7) — components get awkward, no Q advantage. Use Sallen-Key for Q ≤ 1.
LP MFB: choose C2, C1 = m·C2 (m ≥ 4·Q²·(1+A) to keep R values real) R2 = 1 / (2π·fc·√(m)·C2 / (2Q)) (roughly; full form in formula box) R1 = R2 / A, R3 = R2 / (m·(1+A) − 4·Q²·(1+A))

Family pick rules

  • Butterworth — flattest passband, monotonic. Default for ADC anti-aliasing when you don't care about phase.
  • Bessel — flat group delay, no phase distortion. Default for square-wave, video, audio crossovers where overshoot must stay clean.
  • Chebyshev — ripple in passband, sharpest rolloff at the corner. Default when you must hit a stopband attenuation spec in minimum order. Higher ripple = sharper. Group delay is terrible.
  • Elliptic / Cauer (not pictured) — ripple in passband and stopband, sharpest possible. Requires more components per section; use when minimum order is mandatory.

Bode plot — direct comparison

All four families at the same fc, same order. The differences show up sharpest right around the corner. Switch the order to see how stopband attenuation grows linearly with N (in dB / decade).

Butterworth Bessel Chebyshev 1 dB Chebyshev 3 dB
  • Rolloff slope = −20·N dB/decade past fc for any family. 4th order = −80 dB/decade. 8th order = −160 dB/decade.
  • Chebyshev ripple vs sharpness — at the same order, 3 dB ripple is ~6 dB sharper at the corner than 1 dB ripple, which is ~12 dB sharper than Butterworth.
  • Bessel rolloff is gentler at the corner than Butterworth, but it doesn't peak. Past ~2·fc it catches up.

Analog → digital biquad (bilinear transform)

Every analog 2nd-order section maps to a digital 2nd-order biquad. Bilinear transform substitutes s ← (2/T)·(z − 1) / (z + 1), with prewarping at fc to keep the corner where you want it. Output: 5 coefficients (b₀, b₁, b₂, a₁, a₂) you drop into firmware.

analog prototype digital biquad Nyquist (fs/2)

The bilinear transform, in 60 seconds

1. Start with analog H(s) of the section you want. 2. Pre-warp fc: ωa = (2/T)·tan(π·fc·T), where T = 1/fs. 3. Substitute s = (2/T)·(z − 1)/(z + 1); algebra to z^(-1) form gives a biquad (b₀ + b₁·z⁻¹ + b₂·z⁻²) / (1 + a₁·z⁻¹ + a₂·z⁻²). 4. RBJ Audio EQ Cookbook gives closed-form coefficients (use them; don't re-derive at runtime). 5. Stability rule: |a₂| < 1 and |a₁| < 1 + a₂. Always true for normalized biquads of stable analog prototypes.

Sample-rate effects

  • Frequency warping — bilinear compresses the upper analog frequency range into 0..fs/2. The corner is exact (because of pre-warp), but the rolloff slope tightens near Nyquist. For fc < fs/10 the warping is negligible.
  • Coefficient quantization — fixed-point biquads with low fc/fs ratio lose poles to roundoff. Use direct-form-II transposed for 16-bit, direct-form-I for floating point.

Worked example — 4-pole Butterworth LPF at 1 kHz (anti-alias for 8 kHz ADC)

Constraints

ADC sampling at 8 kHz (Nyquist = 4 kHz). Signal of interest is 0..1 kHz (industrial accel-sensor envelope). We want anti-aliasing with at least 40 dB attenuation by 4 kHz so no aliasing artifact above −40 dBFS. 4th-order Butterworth at fc = 1 kHz gives −80 dB/decade past corner, so at 4 kHz (2 decades) we're at −80 · 2 = −48 dB. Margin satisfied.

The cascade

Section A: Sallen-Key LP, equal-R/C, Q = 0.541, fc = 1 kHz Section B: Sallen-Key LP, equal-R/C, Q = 1.307, fc = 1 kHz Each section: R = R1 = R2 = 16 kΩ, C = C1 = C2 = 10 nF → fc = 1/(2π·16k·10n) = 995 Hz (0.5 % low) Section A op-amp gain: K = 3 − 1/0.541 = 1.151 → Rb/Ra = 0.151, e.g. Ra = 10 kΩ, Rb = 1.5 kΩ Section B op-amp gain: K = 3 − 1/1.307 = 2.235 → Rb/Ra = 1.235, e.g. Ra = 10 kΩ, Rb = 12 kΩ Total cascade DC gain = 1.151 × 2.235 = 2.572 (+8.2 dB; budget for it or follow with attenuator)

Op-amp pick

OPA1655 (50 MHz GBW, low noise audio op-amp): 50 MHz / 100 = 500 kHz max fc; we use 1 kHz so we have 500× headroom. Slew rate 24 V/µs at 5 Vpk, 1 kHz needs 0.031 V/µs — easy. Two op-amps total (one per section); use a dual package like OPA1656.

Pitfalls

The high-Q section (B, Q = 1.307) is sensitive — 5 % cap tolerance → ~5 % fc drift and ~3 % Q drift. Use 2 % NP0/C0G ceramics or polypropylene film. Mounting matters: long PCB traces add stray capacitance that retunes fc down. Keep loops tight.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Op-amp slew limit ≠ small-signal bandwidth — GBW determines small-signal frequency response; slew rate determines large-signal. Both must be checked. 1 Vpk at 100 kHz fine on most rail-to-rail op-amps; 10 Vpk at 100 kHz needs 6.3 V/µs.
  • Real caps shift fc — 5 % X7R caps drift another 5 % across temperature, 30 % across DC bias. Use C0G/NP0 for fc ≤ 1 % accuracy. Film caps for audio.
  • Parasitic inductance above 1 MHz — film caps SRF ~10 MHz, electrolytics ~100 kHz. Past SRF the cap looks inductive and the filter inverts.
  • High-Q Sallen-Key is fragile — Q sensitivity to op-amp gain is high. Above Q = 10, use MFB or two-amp biquad.
  • Cascade gain stacks — each non-unity section multiplies gain. 4-pole Butterworth at unity needs gain adjustment per section.
  • Chebyshev fc is not −3 dB — for Chebyshev, fc is the corner where the ripple ends; passband is flat (in dB) to fc with ±Rp dB ripple, and −3 dB is past fc.

WHEN TO USE WHICH

  • RC alone — anti-alias for low-resolution ADC with 10× oversampling; smoothing PWM into DC.
  • RLC — narrow-band tuning at HF (FM front-end, NFC); rarely at audio (inductors are bulky).
  • Sallen-Key — Q ≤ 5, simple polarity, ample op-amp choices.
  • MFB — Q ≥ 1, sharp Chebyshev sections, sensitive applications.
  • Cascade — anytime > 2nd order. Higher Q section goes first if signal is large (lower headroom).
  • Digital biquad — anytime sample rate is > 4× the highest fc; cheaper, exact, drift-free.
