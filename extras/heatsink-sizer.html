Heatsink Sizer / Thermal Network Calculator

A power semiconductor in air is a 5 W problem trying to fit through a 2 W hole. Cooling design is just Ohm's law on heat: Tj = Tamb + P · ΣΘ. Six tabs walk the whole chain — junction-to-ambient network, finned heatsink sizing, TIM picking, PCB copper spreading, fan-forced convection, and package derating curves — so a TO-220 at 5 W doesn't smoke at 25 °C ambient.

Thermal network: Tj = Tamb + P · ΣΘ

Heat flows from junction to ambient through a chain of resistances: silicon-to-package (Θjc), package-to-sink interface (Θcs), sink-to-air (Θsa). Each Θ is in °C/W — degrees of rise per watt dissipated. Slide P and the Θ stages; the calculator backs out the required Θsa to stay under Tj_max with a safety derating.

Where the formula falls out (Ohm's law on heat)

Heat dissipation is exactly the electrical-circuit problem you already know — Fourier's heat conduction law has the same form as Ohm's law. Power is the current, temperature rise is the voltage drop, thermal resistance is the resistance.

1. Electrical: V = I · R (volts across a resistor) 2. Thermal analog: ΔT = P · Θ (°C of rise across a thermal resistance) 3. Series stack from junction to ambient: ΔT_total = P · (Θjc + Θcs + Θsa) 4. Add ambient offset: → Tj = Tamb + P · ΣΘ 5. Solve for Θsa given Tj_target: → Θsa_max = (Tj_target − Tamb)/P − Θjc − Θcs

Same algebra you used for voltage dividers, but the "current" is watts of heat and the "voltage" is degrees. KCL still works — if you parallel two heatsinks, their Θ's combine like parallel resistors.

Worked example — STP55NF06L MOSFET dissipating 5 W

The constraints

STMicro STP55NF06L (TO-220) switching a 12 V / 2 A motor load with Rds(on) effective dissipation ≈ 5 W including switching loss. Datasheet: Tj_max = 175 °C, Θjc = 1.6 °C/W. Ambient = 25 °C still air. Thermal paste between case and sink: Θcs ≈ 0.5 °C/W. Safety derate to 80 %.

The arithmetic

Tj_target = 175 °C × 0.80 = 140 °C (safety budget, leaves 35 °C headroom) Allowed ΔT = Tj_target − Tamb = 140 − 25 = 115 °C Required ΣΘ = ΔT / P = 115 / 5 = 23.0 °C/W Required Θsa = 23.0 − 1.6 − 0.5 = 20.9 °C/W (easy — almost any small clip-on heatsink)

Pick a heatsink

A 25 × 25 × 12 mm anodized aluminum clip-on TO-220 sink quotes Θsa ≈ 14 °C/W in still air. With paste (Θcs = 0.5):

Tj = 25 + 5 × (1.6 + 0.5 + 14) = 25 + 80.5 = 105.5 °C (60 % of Tj_max — safe)

What if no heatsink?

Bare TO-220 Θsa ≈ 50 °C/W; ΣΘ = 1.6 + 0 + 50 = 51.6 °C/W Tj = 25 + 5 × 51.6 = 283 °C → exceeds Tj_max by 108 °C → silicon dies in seconds

This is why "rule of thumb: TO-220 alone is good for 2 W in 25 °C still air" exists. At 2 W, Tj = 25 + 2 × 51.6 = 128 °C — borderline. Anything above ~1.5 W and you need a heatsink, however small.

What to notice

  • Θsa dominates the chain — Θjc is 1–3 °C/W, Θcs is 0.1–1 °C/W, but Θsa swings from 0.3 (giant fanned sink) to 80 (bare DPAK). Whatever you do to reduce ΣΘ, it almost always means changing Θsa.
  • The 80 % derating is not a luxury — silicon failure rate doubles roughly per 10 °C rise. Running at 100 % Tj_max gives the part-population a service life measured in hundreds of hours, not years. 80 % is the design-engineering norm; automotive and aerospace use 60–70 %.
  • Parallel devices = parallel Θ — two MOSFETs on the same heatsink each contribute half the power but share the same Θsa. Net effect: each junction sees roughly the same ΔT as a single device of half the power on the same sink. Diminishing returns above 3–4 in parallel.
  • Steady-state vs transient — these Θ values assume the chip has reached thermal equilibrium (seconds to minutes). For pulse loads, use the transient thermal impedance Z(t) from the datasheet — short pulses see far lower effective Θ.

Thermal Interface Material decision matrix

The package-to-sink interface is two rough metal surfaces clamped together. Air trapped in the surface roughness is a 0.024 W/m·K insulator. A TIM fills that air with something better (1–80 W/m·K). Pick by power density, longevity, conductivity, ease of assembly, and risk of electrical conduction.

Reading the Θ_TIM number

TIM datasheets quote thermal impedance in °C·in²/W or K·mm²/W — per unit area. To get °C/W for your device, divide by contact area:

Θcs (°C/W) = Θ_TIM (°C·in²/W) ÷ Contact area (in²) Example: TO-220 tab ≈ 0.16 in² (10.4 × 16 mm). With Θ_TIM = 0.5 °C·in²/W: → Θcs = 0.5 / 0.16 = 3.1 °C/W (poor — needs better paste) Example: TO-247 tab ≈ 0.31 in². With premium paste Θ_TIM = 0.10: → Θcs = 0.10 / 0.31 = 0.32 °C/W (excellent)

Application tips

  • Less is more for paste — TIM is worse than metal-to-metal. Goal: a thin uniform layer that fills surface roughness only. A grain-of-rice dab spread by the screw torque is correct.
  • Liquid metal kills aluminum — gallium attacks Al heatsinks (forms Al-Ga alloy, embrittles the metal). Use only with nickel-plated copper sinks.
  • Don't reuse a pad — phase-change and silicone pads compress on first heat cycle and form their final interface. Removing and re-applying gives 2–3× higher Θ.
  • Mounting pressure matters — bolt-down TO-220/247 packages have a recommended torque (typically 0.5–1.0 N·m). Too little = TIM voids; too much = cracked die or stripped plastic.

PCB thermal pad + via array

SMD power packages (DPAK, D²PAK, PowerPAK, QFN, SOIC-Exposed-Pad) bond their thermal tab to a copper pour on the PCB. Heat then spreads laterally through copper, drops down through plated vias into an inner or bottom plane, and finally escapes to air or to a chassis. Done well, you can drop Θ from 70 °C/W (bare DPAK) to 15 °C/W (large 2-oz copper pour + 25 vias).

How spreading works (the lateral copper resistance)

A power tab is small (DPAK ≈ 6 × 6 mm); the cooling area you want is large (~25 × 25 mm pour). Heat has to spread laterally through the copper before it can radiate. That spreading shows up as a resistance in the chain:

Spreading resistance (circular, simplified): R_sp ≈ 1 / (k · 2 · √(A)) where k = Cu thermal conductivity × Cu thickness (W/K · m) 1 oz Cu = 35 µm; k_Cu = 385 W/m·K → k·t = 385 × 35e-6 = 0.0135 W/K For 1 in² = 6.45 cm² = 6.45e-4 m²: R_sp = 1 / (0.0135 × 2 × √(6.45e-4)) = 1 / (0.0135 × 0.0508) = ~ 1460 K/W per (W/m·K · m) → Effective spreading drops Θ to ~ 20–35 °C/W depending on aspect ratio; ditto with formula simplifications

The exact spreading number depends on tab geometry and pour aspect — this sizer uses a curve-fit to vendor measurements rather than the closed-form integral. For mission-critical designs, run a thermal sim (e.g., 6SigmaET, KiCad ngspice-thermal).

The empirical curve

The shape of Θsa vs airflow is roughly a power-law decay. A good engineering fit:

Θsa(v) ≈ Θsa_0 · (1 + k·v)^(-0.7) where v in m/s and k ≈ 2.5 for typical extrusion At v = 0: Θsa = Θsa_0 (free air) At v = 1 m/s: Θsa ≈ 0.45 × Θsa_0 At v = 3 m/s: Θsa ≈ 0.25 × Θsa_0 At v = 6 m/s: Θsa ≈ 0.17 × Θsa_0 ← diminishing returns

A 60 CFM fan blowing into an 80 mm × 25 mm duct (20 cm²) gives:

Volume = 60 CFM = 60 × 0.000472 m³/s = 0.0283 m³/s Area = 80 × 25 = 2000 mm² = 0.002 m² Velocity = 0.0283 / 0.002 = 14 m/s (very fast — diminishing returns territory)

In practice, ducted velocities at the sink are 2–5 m/s. Anything higher and the fan is louder than it is useful. For CPU coolers, the trade is more fin area at moderate flow vs less fin area at higher flow.

Fan failure design rule

  • Always check the free-air case — if a fan stops (bearing seize, dust, controller fault), the device must survive in still air long enough to shut down safely. Compare the "fan failed" sink temp with Tj_max.
  • Two slow fans > one fast fan — redundancy + lower bearing wear + lower noise. Used in server design universally.
  • Fan curve vs system impedance — a fan's CFM rating is at zero static pressure. When pushing through a densely finned sink, actual CFM is half or less. Get the fan PQ curve and the sink Δp curve to find the operating point.

Power derating curves — package vs case temperature

Every power-device datasheet has a "P_D vs T_case" derating curve. The story is simple: at low T_case you can dissipate the rated power; at Tj_max the device can dissipate zero (all of the temperature budget is gone). The slope is exactly −1/Θjc.

Slope = −1/Θjc W/°C TO-220 (Θjc=2): P drops 0.5 W per °C TO-247 (Θjc=0.7): P drops 1.4 W per °C DPAK (Θjc=3.5): P drops 0.29 W per °C

Reading the curve

The y-axis is the maximum allowed continuous power dissipation. The x-axis is the case temperature (T_case). Three regions:

RegionT_case rangeP_D allowedWhy

Worked example — TO-220 STP55NF06L at 75 °C case

Read the derating

Datasheet: P_D_max = 110 W @ Tc = 25 °C; Tj_max = 175 °C; Θjc = (175 − 25) / 110 = 1.36 °C/W At Tc = 75 °C: P_D_max = (Tj_max − Tc) / Θjc = (175 − 75) / 1.36 = 73.5 W At Tc = 125 °C: P_D_max = 50 / 1.36 = 36.8 W → only 33 % of rating left At Tc = 175 °C: P_D_max = 0 W → device thermally limited to zero

Why "infinite heatsink" rating is misleading

The "P_D = 110 W" headline assumes Tc = 25 °C — which requires an infinite heatsink (no thermal resistance from case to ambient). In a real design at 25 °C ambient with a 5 °C/W heatsink:

At P = 20 W: Tc = 25 + 20 × (0.5 + 5.0) = 25 + 110 = 135 °C At Tc = 135 °C: P_D allowed = (175 − 135) / 1.36 = 29 W → just barely OK At P = 25 W: Tc = 25 + 25 × 5.5 = 162.5 °C → only 9 W allowed → thermal runaway

Thermal runaway is the implicit-equation form: you assumed P_D but the resulting Tc lowers P_D_max, which limits P_D, which lowers Tc... only stable if P × ΣΘ < ΔT_budget. Always cross-check by computing Tc from P + Θ and verifying the derated P_D_max at that Tc.

The two graphs you need from a datasheet

  • Continuous power derating — the one this tab plots; needed for steady-state.
  • Transient thermal impedance Z(t) — a log-log plot of effective Θ vs pulse duration. For a 1 ms pulse, the silicon's heat capacity dominates and Z(t) is much lower than Θjc. Critical for PWM, motor inrush, capacitor charging.
  • Cross-reference: a 100 W rated TO-220 can sink a 500 W pulse for 100 µs without exceeding Tj_max if duty cycle is low — the average is what derating governs, the peak is what Z(t) governs.

SHORTCUTS

  • Bare TO-220 ≈ 50 °C/W to air — good for ~2 W max
  • Clip-on sink 25 mm → ≈ 14 °C/W — good for ~5 W
  • Medium extrusion 100 mm → ≈ 1.5 °C/W — good for ~50 W
  • Add airflow at 2 m/s → halves Θsa
  • 1 in² 1 oz copper under DPAK → Θja ≈ 35 °C/W
  • + 16 thermal vias → drops another 8–10 °C/W
  • Tj derate to 80 % for design margin
  • Per 10 °C Tj rise doubles silicon failure rate

COMMON PITFALLS

  • TIM degrades over years — silicone grease pumps out under thermal cycling. Inspect 5-yr-old industrial gear; expect Θcs to have doubled.
  • Cheap thermal pads lose contact pressure — pad sets a hard gap and screws back off as it cures. Use a torque-spec'd spring clip, not just bolts.
  • Vertical fin orientation matters — natural-convection sinks must have fins vertical, not horizontal. Mounting a board flat with fins facing up reduces Θsa by 20–30 %.
  • "P_D = 110 W" headline misleads — that's at Tc = 25 °C with an infinite sink. Realistic 5 °C/W sink at 25 °C ambient gives you ~30 W usable.
  • Liquid metal on aluminum — gallium attacks Al sinks. Only use with Ni-plated Cu.
  • Forgetting Θcs — TIM resistance is small but non-zero. A 5 mm² die on a 50 µm pad adds ~0.5 °C/W. Sneaks up in QFN designs.
  • Bonded backside vias under SMD pads — open vias wick solder into the via barrel during reflow, voiding the pad joint. Use Cu-filled or epoxy-plug-Cu-cap vias.
