Oscilloscope Techniques

An oscilloscope is the most useful instrument in your lab and the most misused. The probe matters more than the scope. The ground clip matters more than the probe. And triggering is what separates "looking at a wave" from "diagnosing the problem". This visual walks through probe loading, triggering modes, math channels, and the specific captures you'll do every week as an engineer.

The bandwidth rule

Scope bandwidth should be 3–5× your maximum signal frequency. A 100 MHz scope reliably shows up to ~30 MHz signals. For a 1 Mbps UART (rise time ~50 ns equivalent BW ~7 MHz), a 50 MHz scope is plenty. For 200 Mbps USB 2.0 (rise time 5 ns, BW ~70 MHz), you need a 200+ MHz scope.

Probe loading kills high-frequency signals

A 10× probe presents 10 pF of capacitance to your circuit. At 100 MHz, that's an impedance of 1/(2π·100e6·10e-12) = 160 Ω. A 50 Ω source driving a 160 Ω load loses 30% of its amplitude at the probe tip. You're measuring the probe's effect, not the actual circuit.

Solution: active FET probe (typ 2 pF) → 800 Ω load at 100 MHz, much smaller perturbation.

The ground clip — short it!

The longest cable in your scope is your ground loop

A typical scope probe has a 10–20 cm flying ground lead. That lead is an inductor (≈ 10 nH/cm = 100–200 nH). At 100 MHz, that's an impedance of 60–120 Ω. Combined with the probe tip capacitance (10 pF), you've built an LC tank at ~50 MHz.

When you probe a fast edge, this LC tank rings — and you see "ringing" on the signal that isn't actually there. The signal is clean; your ground lead is ringing.

Fix: use a short ground spring (1–2 cm) or solder a tiny ground tab right next to the probed point. For sub-ns edges, this matters even at low voltages.

Common captures you'll do every week

1. Power-supply ripple

Probe across the output cap. AC coupling. 20 mV/div vertical. Bandwidth limit on (20 MHz). Triggering on the switching ripple if visible.

Look for: peak-to-peak ripple, switching frequency, load-step response (touch a 1 Ω resistor across output, watch the dip + recovery).

2. Switching MOSFET Vds + gate

Differential probe across Vds (to handle high-side switching). Standard 10× on gate. Trigger on gate rising edge.

Look for: Miller plateau on gate, ringing on Vds (parasitic LC), overshoot above Vbus (snubber design).

3. Serial bus capture (I²C / SPI / UART)

One probe on clock, one on data. Trigger on clock edge or serial-bus decode mode. Modern scopes can decode the bus in real time and overlay the bytes on the screen.

Look for: clock duty cycle, setup/hold timing, ACK bits, stuck devices, fast-mode timing violations.

4. Eye diagram (high-speed serial)

Set the scope to "persistence" mode (infinite or long), trigger on data, sample many bit periods, view the overlaid eye. Measure eye height + width.

For USB 3 or DDR signals, use a differential probe + a scope with ≥ 4 GS/s.

5. Current waveform with shunt + math

Probe across a known shunt resistor (e.g., 100 mΩ). Apply scope math: V_probe / 0.1 = I in amps. Display the math channel.

Combine with another probe to compute instantaneous power: P = V_load · I_load.

6. FFT of a noisy supply

Use the FFT math function. Window: Hann or Blackman. Look for switching harmonics + their sidebands. A 100 kHz switcher should show clean peaks at 100 kHz, 200, 300; spreading or floor lift indicates EMI / instability.

Probe compensation — do this every session

The 1 kHz square wave test

  1. Plug 10× probe into scope, ground clip to scope's chassis ground.
  2. Touch the probe to the scope's calibration output (usually 1 kHz square wave, 3 V peak).
  3. Observe the displayed square wave.
  4. If the corners are rounded → probe compensation cap is too low. If overshooting → too high. Adjust the small screw on the probe head until corners are square.

Why this matters: an uncompensated 10× probe will show fast edges as overshooting or sagging. Misdiagnosed as circuit problems, lost hours.

Common debugging recipes

"My SMPS output is unstable"

Probe output cap with AC coupling, 20 mV/div, 20 MHz bandwidth limit. Trigger on AC line. Watch for: sub-harmonic oscillation (bursts every 1/2 or 1/3 switching cycle), gradual sag during load steps, big swings during transients.

"My MCU randomly resets"

Probe Vcc with AC coupling. Trigger on a downward edge below the brownout threshold (typically 80% of nominal). Watch for: ground bounce during load events (motor switching, current pulses), brownout dips, slow rail droop.

"My I²C bus is hanging"

Probe SCL + SDA. Use I²C decode trigger on a specific slave address. Watch for: missed ACKs, clock-stretching by a slave taking too long, glitches on SDA during clock-high, slow rise times indicating R_pullup too high.

"My ADC reading drifts"

Use a differential probe across the ADC pin + analog ground (right at the chip, not at the connector). Look for: noise from nearby digital signals coupling in, sampling at the wrong time relative to PWM, ripple from a noisy LDO supply.

Differential vs single-ended — when to switch

  • Single-ended (standard): probe tip on signal, ground clip on board ground. Measures voltage relative to scope ground. Limited to ~30 V_rms safely.
  • Differential: two probe inputs measure the voltage difference. Allows floating measurements (motor windings between phases, mains, current shunts on the high side, isolated supplies).
  • NEVER use single-ended on a high-side switch — the scope ground will short the high-side switch to mains ground through the scope's chassis. Best case: dead scope. Worst case: fire.
  • Common-mode range matters: a differential probe rated 100 V common-mode but 10 V differential won't work on a 400 V motor bus. Get a high-voltage diff probe.

FFT — frequency-domain debugging

The FFT function turns a time-domain signal into a frequency spectrum. Critical for:

  • EMI debug: identify which frequencies are leaking. 100 kHz peaks → SMPS fundamental. 27 MHz → suspicious; might be a clock harmonic.
  • Resonance identification: spot the LC tank resonance on a PCB power rail.
  • Modulation analysis: see the AM/FM sidebands on a transmitter output.
  • Audio noise floor: identify hum at 50/60 Hz, 100/120 Hz harmonics, switching pickup.

Tips that took me too long to learn

  • Save references early: when something works, save the waveform. Future comparison is gold.
  • Use chan colors: dedicate yellow=clock, blue=data, green=power, red=ground/anomaly across all captures.
  • Persistence on, then off: turn on infinite persistence for 5 seconds — anomalies show up. Turn off, use peak-detect mode to find rare glitches.
  • Trigger on the bad event, not the normal one: edge-trigger on a normal pulse to verify it; pulse-width trigger on a too-short pulse to catch the glitch.
  • 10× probe with ground spring for any signal > 10 MHz. The flying ground lead is the enemy.
  • Coupling — DC for the DC component, AC for ripple, GND to find the zero-level. Three different views.
  • X-Y mode: feed two phases of a motor onto X and Y inputs; visualize the rotating field as a Lissajous figure. Beautiful + diagnostic.
