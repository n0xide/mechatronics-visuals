Auxiliary Views

Standard orthographic views show inclined surfaces foreshortened — a slanted face appears as a shorter rectangle, dimensions lie, and any holes or features on it are ellipses you can't measure. The fix: project onto a plane parallel to the inclined surface, not parallel to the principal axes. The result is an auxiliary view: true-size, true-shape, dimensionable.

Why we need them

In a standard third-angle projection, the front, top, and right-side views are projections onto three mutually-perpendicular planes (the principal planes). A surface that is parallel to one of those planes projects true-size to the matching view. A surface that is inclined to all three planes — tilted around one principal axis — is foreshortened in two of the views and shows as an edge (a single line) in the third. A surface that is oblique — rotated around two axes — is foreshortened in all three views.

Construction — primary auxiliary from front view

The recipe is mechanical. Each step is one straight-edge action; combined they recover the true face.

1. Identify the edge view of the inclined face — the view where the face appears as a single line. 2. Draw a reference line (F–A, "front to auxiliary") parallel to the edge view of the inclined face, some distance away. 3. Project each endpoint of the inclined edge perpendicular to F–A. Mark the points where the projectors cross F–A — those are the silhouette of the inclined face in the aux plane. 4. Transfer depth — pick a second principal view (top or side) that shows the depth of the inclined face. Measure each point's distance from a shared reference line (often the back edge of the part). 5. In the auxiliary view, lay those depths off perpendicular to F–A on the appropriate side. → The face appears true-size. 6. Any feature on the inclined face (hole, slot, fillet) projects the same way — center first, then size.

Primary auxiliary

A primary auxiliary projects from one of the three principal views onto a plane that is normal to the inclined face. The auxiliary plane shares one axis with the source view — the axis parallel to the inclined edge — so depths transfer directly.

In the figure above, the front view shows the inclined face as a line of length L at angle θ. The top view shows the same face as a foreshortened rectangle of length L · cos θ. Neither view shows the true length L in the plane of the page. The auxiliary view, projected normal to the inclined edge, shows the face as a rectangle of length L and depth equal to the part's depth — the true size, with no trigonometry needed to read it.

Secondary auxiliary — for oblique faces

If the face is tilted around two axes (oblique), one auxiliary view isn't enough. The first auxiliary turns the face into an edge view; the secondary auxiliary then projects from that edge view onto a plane normal to the second tilt axis. Two projections, true size.

Partial vs complete auxiliary

A complete auxiliary projects every visible edge of the part onto the auxiliary plane. The result is busy — the inclined face is true-size, but every other edge of the part is foreshortened and overlapping, and the auxiliary view ends up cluttered with information the principal views already showed.

A partial auxiliary projects only the inclined face and its features. The rest is omitted. That's what you actually want most of the time — the auxiliary's job is to give you a true-size view of the slanted feature, not to redraw the whole part. Use a partial auxiliary unless the rest of the geometry is also affected by the tilt.

Worked example — bracket with a 35° inclined mounting pad and a Ø10 mm hole

The constraints

You have an L-shaped bracket with a 35° face on the upper end carrying a Ø10 mm mounting hole, 18 mm in from the corner along the inclined face. The principal views show the hole as an ellipse in front and as a single line in the right-side view. You can't dimension an ellipse — you need a true-size view of the inclined face.

The arithmetic

Edge view in right-side: face appears as a line of slope tan(35°) = 0.700 In front view: face shows as a rectangle of length L · cos(35°) ≈ 0.819·L (foreshortened) Projection direction: F–A reference parallel to the edge view → perpendicular to the inclined face's true plane Hole center transferred from front view: 18 mm along inclined edge in true direction Hole projects as a circle of Ø10 mm in the aux view — now dimensionable Title under view: "AUX — TRUE SIZE" with an arrow indicating viewing direction in the source view

What changed by adding the auxiliary

Before: 1 ellipse in front view (can't dimension), 1 edge view in side (no feature visible), 1 foreshortened rectangle in top (can't measure the face). After: 1 partial auxiliary view showing a true-size rectangle with a true circle, and the dimensions are read directly. The CAD package does this automatically — you tap the inclined face and request "auxiliary view from this surface" — but the manual construction is what teaches you to recognize when to ask for one.

What to notice

  • You only need an aux when a feature on the inclined face has dimensions that matter. A slanted face with no holes or slots just needs the inclined edge dimensioned in the principal view (length, angle). Adding an auxiliary is overhead.
  • The aux shares one axis with the source view. Depths transfer directly — measure once in the source view, plot once in the aux. The other axis is the projection direction.
  • Hidden lines on the aux are usually omitted. Aux views are for showing the inclined face true-size; hidden geometry that doesn't lie in the inclined plane is read from the principal views.
  • Oblique faces need two auxiliaries. Primary takes one tilt out; secondary takes the other out. The secondary is usually small — only the face, no surrounding context.
  • Identify the source view first. The aux always projects from a view in which the inclined face appears as an edge. If no principal view shows an edge view of the face, you have an oblique face and need a primary aux first.
  • CAD does this without thinking, you still need to think. Solidworks/Fusion will generate any auxiliary view from a click on a face — but it doesn't choose partial vs complete, doesn't label viewing direction, and doesn't decide which features to dimension where. That's drafting judgment.

Where you'll see this

Real situations

  • Brackets with mounting faces at an angle — control-panel angle pieces, sensor brackets aligned to a robot end-effector axis. The bolt holes need to be true-size.
  • Cylinder gaskets cut at an angle — a flange face mating at 30° to the cylinder axis. The gasket cut needs an aux to dimension the bolt circle.
  • Sheet-metal bend regions — when an inclined web carries weld lines or punched features. The unfolded view is one option, but a partial aux on the bend face is cleaner.
  • Robot end-effector flanges — ISO 9409 robot mounting flange may sit at a tool-tilt angle. Auxiliary view dimensions the bolt pattern in the tool's frame.
  • 3D-printed parts with overhangs — angled walls dimensioned in the print's build-plate orientation. An aux view aligned to the build face dimensions the part as printed.

Cheat sheet

FACTS

  • Inclined face = rotated about one principal axis → 1 aux needed.
  • Oblique face = rotated about two axes → 1 primary + 1 secondary.
  • Aux projects normal to the edge view of the face.
  • Source view depth → aux depth, transferred at right angles to F–A.
  • Foreshortened length in source = L · cos θ; aux recovers L.
  • Partial aux is the default; complete only if surrounding geometry matters.

DRAFTING RULES

  • Label the aux view: "AUX — TRUE SIZE" or "VIEW A–A" with a viewing-direction arrow in the source view.
  • Reference line F–A parallel to the edge view of the inclined face.
  • Place the aux so projectors are perpendicular to F–A (above, below, left, or right of the source view).
  • Omit hidden lines on the aux unless they convey the feature's depth.
  • Dimension the inclined feature only on the aux — don't double-dimension in principal views.
  • Add an angle dimension between the inclined face and a reference in the principal view.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Dimensioning an ellipse — the foreshortened circle in the front view is an ellipse with no honest "diameter." Always add an auxiliary for the inclined hole.
  • Forgetting the viewing arrow — without an arrow in the source view, the reader can't tell which direction the aux looks from. Always add the arrow + view label.
  • Choosing complete when partial is enough — gives a busy, hard-to-read view. Use partial unless the surrounding geometry must be shown.
  • Skipping the depth transfer — the aux only shows one axis from the source view; the perpendicular axis must be measured from a second principal view. Forgetting this gives an aux of the right length but wrong depth.
  • Aux placed where it doesn't project clean — leave the aux in its projection direction, don't drag it to a free corner of the sheet without keeping the projectors aligned.
