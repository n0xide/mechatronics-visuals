Section Views — full / half / offset / aligned / broken / revolved / removed

Hidden lines are honest but unreadable. The moment your part has a counter-bore, a cored hole, or three intersecting pockets, the outside view collapses into spaghetti. A section view imagines the part cut on a plane, throws away the front half, and draws the new face. Solid material gets section lining (hatch) so the eye can separate body from void at a glance.

This visual walks the seven section types you'll actually use on prints (full, half, offset, aligned, broken-out, revolved, removed), shows the cutting-plane line convention (ANSI Y14.2 vs. ISO 128), and lets you flip the hatch pattern between cast iron, steel, and bronze (ISO/ANSI Y14.3). The seven section types you'll meet on real prints

The cutting-plane line — what every arrow means

The cutting-plane line tells the reader where the imaginary saw went. Three pieces of information are encoded:

1. The chain line — thick (0.7–1.0 mm). ISO 128-44 specifies a long-dashed dotted chain (narrow line with the ends and direction-change vertices drawn wide). ANSI Y14.2 uses a thick long-dash short-dash-short-dash pattern (Method 1) or evenly-spaced thick dashes (Method 2). Both standards make the line thicker than any object line. 2. The arrowheads at each end — point in the direction you are looking at the cut face. Same convention as fold-line arrows in orthographic projection. 3. The letter labels (A–A, B–B, …) — same letter on each end of one plane; the section view caption matches. Multiple sections on one drawing get distinct letters (no I, O, Q, S, X, Z — easy to confuse with numerals).

If the section is the only one on the sheet and the cutting plane is obvious (full section, plane on centerline), some practices omit the cutting-plane line entirely. When in doubt, draw it.

Hatch patterns — ANSI Y14.3 / ISO 128-50

The hatch (a.k.a. section lining, cross-hatching) does two things: tells you the area is solid material, and identifies the material class. Patterns are a strong tradition; not all are required by the standard, but you'll meet them on legacy prints.

Two adjacent parts in the same assembly section get different hatch angles (typically 45° on one, 135° on the other) so the eye can separate them. Three parts → 45°, 135°, 30°. Never let two touching parts use the same angle; the section read collapses.

What does not get hatched in a section

The "thin features along the cut" rule. If the cutting plane runs longitudinally through one of these, you do not hatch it — even though the saw notionally went through it — because the un-hatched view reads more honestly:

  • Fasteners — bolts, screws, studs, nuts, rivets. The thread profile is unmistakable; hatching obscures it.
  • Shafts and axles — shown as full circular outlines, not hatched. The cross-section view (perpendicular cut) does hatch.
  • Keys, pins, cotter pins, lockwashers — similar reasoning. Cross-cut: hatch. Longitudinal: skip.
  • Ball / roller / pin bearings — the rolling elements stay un-sectioned. The races may be hatched in good practice.
  • Gear teeth in a section through the axis — the teeth are not hatched because we want to read the pitch and the tooth profile.
  • Webs, ribs, spokes when the cut runs along the rib — do not hatch (otherwise the part reads as a solid disk and the rib disappears). If the cut crosses the rib transversely, hatch normally. This is the most-tested convention.

Aligned vs. offset — the two clarifications you'll need most

Both bend the strict "single flat plane" rule of the full section to tell the truth about the geometry:

Offset section — jog the plane to catch all the interesting features

The cutting plane is allowed to step at right angles to itself. The steps are not shown in the section view (no extra edges where the jogs occurred). A typical pump or valve body needs an offset section to pick up two bores on different centerlines.

Rule: jog at 90°, never at an angle. Each leg of the offset is its own short cutting line in the parent view. In the section: no jog lines appear — the jog is purely a drawing-side trick.

Aligned section — rotate angular features into the cut plane

For parts with features at angles around an axis (e.g., three holes at 120° on a flange), a strict cutting plane through the axis would catch only one. The aligned section rotates the off-axis feature into the cutting plane (typically the vertical) so the dimensions read true.

Rule: rotate about the part's axis of symmetry, never about an arbitrary axis. The rotated feature appears at its true distance from the axis, even though it's drawn in a position where it isn't physically located.

Most common case: bolt circles on flange faces. The section reads "three M8 holes on Ø80 bolt circle" rather than the geometrically literal cut.

Worked example — reading a section view on a cyberdeck case

The print

An aluminum mounting boss inside the fold-p4-pico case has: an M3 threaded hole (8 mm deep), a counter-bored access hole on the opposite face (Ø6 × 3 mm deep), and a side-drilled cross-vent (Ø2.5 mm). The outside views collapse into hidden lines.

The section choice

Step 1: cutting plane bisects the M3 hole — that's the dominant axis. Full section A–A. Step 2: offset the plane by 6 mm to also catch the Ø2.5 vent — offset section A–A. Step 3: do not hatch the threads — show the thread profile (zig-zag) at the M3 hole interior. Step 4: thread depth dimensions read off the section, not the parent view. Tap-drill diameter (Ø2.5 for M3) is implied by the thread callout.

What to notice

  • Hatch identifies material AND separates adjacent parts — modern CAD usually skips material-specific patterns (gray fill + BOM material) but the adjacent-part angle rule is still mandatory.
  • Cutting-plane line is the heaviest line on the drawing — thicker than object lines so it dominates visually. Arrowheads always point in the viewing direction.
  • Half sections cost you a centerline — you only see half the inside, and dimensions that span the centerline can confuse. Use them only for symmetric parts where the saved space matters.
  • Offset and aligned are non-literal — they bend the geometry for clarity. Treat them as a courtesy to the reader, not a fact about the part.
  • Revolved and removed are for cross-sections of long features — a turned shaft's local profile, a structural-shape's web; quicker than a separate full-section drawing.
  • Webs, ribs, spokes → no hatch along the cut — the most common student mistake. The Y14.3 rule exists because otherwise the rib reads as solid material and the part looks twice as heavy.

Cheat sheet

SEVEN SECTION TYPES

Full
plane bisects, all interior shown
Half
1/4 quadrant removed, centerline split
Offset
jogged 90° to hit multiple features
Aligned
off-axis features rotated into plane
Broken-out
partial, irregular freehand boundary
Revolved
cross-section rotated 90° into the view
Removed
like revolved but drawn off to the side

SHORTCUTS

  • Arrowheads point in viewing direction.
  • Letters match at both ends & on the section caption.
  • Two adjacent parts → 45° / 135° hatch.
  • Webs / ribs along cut → no hatch.
  • Fasteners / shafts longitudinally → no hatch.
  • Hatch spacing ≈ 2–4 mm at 1:1.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Hatching ribs along the cut — part reads as a slab.
  • Same hatch angle on touching parts — eye merges them.
  • Aligned section drawn literally — reader can't tell whether the angular feature is rotated or actually misplaced.
  • Section view orientation flipped — section always retains the same up/down as the parent view (no mirroring).
  • Hidden lines inside a section — omit them unless absolutely necessary; the whole point of sectioning was to make them visible as solid lines.
