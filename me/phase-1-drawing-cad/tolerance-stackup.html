Tolerance Stack-Up

Every dimension on a drawing has a tolerance — and tolerances accumulate. Stack four parts in a row and the final gap can be off by far more than any single part. Drag the dimensioning style and the per-feature tolerances; watch the assembly's overall tolerance band grow under worst-case stacking and shrink under root-sum-square. Where the two formulas come from

You have a stack of N parts with individual tolerances ±t1, ±t2, ±t3, ±t4. The question is: how far off can the total length be?

1. Worst-case assumes every part is at the same extreme of its tolerance — all high or all low at once. 2. Maximum total = (L1+t1) + (L2+t2) + … = ΣL + Σt 3. Minimum total = (L1−t1) + (L2−t2) + … = ΣL − Σt 4. Range = 2·Σt → tolerance is ±Σt. → T_WC = Σ|t_i| 5. RSS treats each tolerance as the half-width of a normal distribution at ±3σ (so σ_i = t_i / 3). 6. Variance of a sum of independent normals = sum of variances: σ_total² = Σ σ_i². 7. σ_total = √(Σσ_i²) → expressed as ±3σ band: → T_RSS = √(Σ t_i²) 8. RSS is always < worst-case (since √Σx² < Σ|x| for any non-zero stack).

Chain vs baseline dimensioning

Same parts, two ways to dimension them on a drawing. The choice determines whether tolerances chain together (compounding) or stay independent.

In chain dimensioning, each feature's location is measured from the previous one. If Part 1 is +0.10 long, Part 2's position is already shifted; Part 2's own tolerance then stacks on top. The 4th feature's position has a tolerance band equal to the sum of all four — that's just worst-case stack-up.
In baseline (datum) dimensioning, every feature is measured from a single reference edge. The tolerance on the 4th feature is its own ±t4 — no chain growth. The trade-off is that each dimension carries the full bracket-edge tolerance; the chosen datum had better be a precisely machined surface.

Worked example — Pico 2 W mounting through bracket slot

The problem

You're mounting a Pico 2 W to a 3D-printed bracket. The Pico has four mounting holes at corners spaced 47.0 × 11.4 mm (the standard pattern). The bracket has matching tapped M2 holes. To leave slack for FDM dimensional drift, the bracket holes are slotted.

The dimensions

Pico hole pattern: 47.00 ± 0.10 mm (PCB fab tolerance, JLCPCB) Bracket hole pattern: 47.00 ± 0.25 mm (PA12-CF FDM, ±0.5% typical) Pico mounting hole: Ø2.10 ± 0.05 (clearance for M2) M2 screw shaft: Ø1.95 ± 0.05

Stack-up — will it bolt up?

Position error (worst-case): ±0.10 + ±0.25 = ±0.35 mm (Pico hole vs bracket hole, corner-to-corner) Position error (RSS): √(0.10² + 0.25²) = ±0.27 mm (statistical estimate) Available radial slack: (2.10 − 1.95) / 2 = 0.075 mm (per side, before slot) Verdict: WC 0.35 ≫ 0.075 → bolts will bind without a slot. RSS 0.27 still ≫ 0.075.

The fix

Slot the bracket holes to 2.10 × 2.80 (0.35 mm slack in pattern direction) Slot width 2.10 + ±0.35 + ±0.05 = ±0.40 → 2.10 × 2.90 with safety Verdict (slotted): 0.40 mm slack > ±0.35 WC → bolts up every time

Drawing callout

On the bracket DXF or STEP, dimension all four holes from the bottom-left corner of the PCB cutout (single datum, baseline style). Avoid dimensioning hole-to-hole; the chain-style stack-up would put the upper-right hole at ±0.50 mm even with the same per-hole tolerance.

What to notice

  • WC vs RSS gap widens with N — for 2 parts, RSS is ~71% of WC; for 4, ~50%; for 16, ~25%. The more parts in the stack, the more pessimistic worst-case looks. RSS rewards diversification.
  • One sloppy part dominates RSS — because tolerances are squared, the largest one drives the result. Tightening the worst part by 2× has more effect than tightening four good ones. WC treats every part equally; RSS doesn't.
  • Worst-case is rare in practice — Monte Carlo studies of injection-molded assemblies show worst-case probability is ~0.1% even with 5-part stacks. But "rare" doesn't mean "ignorable" in safety-critical (medical, aerospace).
  • The "1.5σ shift" lurks — Six Sigma quality programs assume tolerances drift by ±1.5σ over time (tool wear, batch variation). That makes RSS slightly optimistic; some shops use 1.25·RSS as a hybrid.
  • Baseline beats chain for assemblies — chain dimensioning is fine for single-feature relationships (slot from edge of slot). Anything that stacks should be datum-referenced. The drawing reads cleaner too.
  • Slot, taper, or counter-bore the loose part — the cheap fix for an over-tight stack-up is to add slack on the floppier part. Don't tighten tolerances if a slot will do.

RULES OF THUMB

  • 2 parts equal tol → RSS ≈ 0.71 · WC
  • 4 parts equal tol → RSS ≈ 0.50 · WC
  • 9 parts equal tol → RSS ≈ 0.33 · WC
  • Baseline always for assemblies of more than 2 features
  • One sloppy part dominates RSS; spend tolerance budget there
  • Add slack early via slots, oversized holes, or float washers

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Chain dimensioning for assemblies — looks fine on one part, blows up the moment you stack four of them. Switch to baseline.
  • Using RSS for N=2 — only saves ~30%; the math overhead isn't worth it. Use WC.
  • Forgetting CTE on long parts — over a 40 °C range, Al expands ~1 mm/m. Add a thermal term to the stack if the assembly spans temperature.
  • Assuming RSS = safe — for safety-critical (brake pedal, EpiPen) use WC. RSS allows 0.3% failure.
  • Trusting FDM ±0.1 — real PLA on a Bambu hits ±0.2 mm easily, PA12-CF up to ±0.5. Check the print actually.
