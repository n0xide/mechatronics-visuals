Composites & Laminate Theory — designing with anisotropic fibers

A carbon-fiber composite is dramatically stronger along the fiber direction than across it. A single unidirectional ply has E_1 ≈ 140 GPa parallel but only 10 GPa transverse — a 14× anisotropy. Lamination stacks plies at different orientations to engineer a "quasi-isotropic" plate, or to deliberately reinforce specific directions. This visual computes ply properties via rule of mixtures, lets you stack a layup, and shows the resulting in-plane stiffness profile (CLT/ABD matrix essentials).

Rule-of-mixtures math (single-ply UD)

Modulus parallel to fiber (Voigt upper bound): E₁ = V_f · E_f + (1 − V_f) · E_m Modulus transverse to fiber (Reuss lower bound): E₂ = E_f · E_m / (V_f · E_m + (1 − V_f) · E_f) In-plane shear modulus: G₁₂ = G_f · G_m / (V_f · G_m + (1 − V_f) · G_f) (similar to E₂) Major Poisson's ratio: ν₁₂ = V_f · ν_f + (1 − V_f) · ν_m Ply stress in arbitrary direction θ uses transformation: [σ_x; σ_y; τ_xy] = T(θ) · [σ_1; σ_2; τ_12] where T(θ) is the 3×3 rotation matrix. For a symmetric balanced laminate, the in-plane stiffness A_ij is: A_ij = Σ_k Q̄_ij(θ_k) · t_k Q̄_ij = transformed reduced stiffness of each ply Effective in-plane modulus: E_x = (A₁₁·A₂₂ − A₁₂²) / (A₂₂ · t_total) E_y = (A₁₁·A₂₂ − A₁₂²) / (A₁₁ · t_total)

Worked example — drone arm tube layup

Spec

5-inch racing drone arm: ~15 mm dia × 1 mm wall × 100 mm long. Must resist bending from prop thrust + torsion from yaw + crash impact. HS carbon fiber + epoxy at V_f = 0.55.

Per-ply properties (rule of mixtures)

E₁ = 0.55 · 230 + 0.45 · 3.5 = 126.5 + 1.58 = 128.1 GPa E₂ = 230 · 3.5 / (0.55 · 3.5 + 0.45 · 230) = 805 / (1.93 + 103.5) = 7.64 GPa G_f = 230/(2·1.30) = 88.5; G_m = 3.5/(2·1.36) = 1.29 GPa G₁₂ = 88.5 · 1.29 / (0.55 · 1.29 + 0.45 · 88.5) = 114 / (0.71 + 39.8) = 2.81 GPa Anisotropy ratio E₁/E₂ = 17 — extreme

Layup choice

Pure [0₄] gives max bending stiffness (E_axial = 128 GPa) but crumbles under crash (transverse only 7.6 GPa). Pure [±45₂] gives torsion stiffness but poor bending. A common choice: [0/±45/0/±45/0] with ~50% axial + 50% angle plies. Result: E_axial ≈ 80 GPa, G_xy ≈ 25 GPa (high). Survives crashes + good thrust response.

Quick-iso comparison: same total thickness, fully QI

QI [0/+45/−45/90]s: E_x = E_y = ~50 GPa, G = ~20 GPa Same stiffness in every direction — best for plates where load direction is uncertain (drone center plate, RC airplane wing skin)

Common pitfalls

  • Forgetting V_f reality — quoted "carbon fiber" parts on hobby sites often have V_f as low as 0.3-0.4 (wet layup, not autoclave). E_1 drops proportionally. For real aerospace quality (V_f > 0.6), need vacuum-bag + autoclave or RTM.
  • Off-axis loading on UD — a unidirectional part loaded at 30° off-axis sees E_30° ≈ 30 GPa, NOT 128 GPa. Always know the load path before specifying a layup.
  • Unsymmetric laminates warp — if the layup is not symmetric about the midplane, curing introduces bending due to thermal mismatch. Use symmetric stacking (mirror through center).
  • Edge / hole effects — drilled holes in laminates concentrate stress 3-7× depending on layup. Use cocured inserts or potted holes for fasteners.
  • Compression-after-impact (CAI) — barely-visible impact damage (5 J energy) can drop compressive strength 50%. Critical for aerospace; design with ≥ 3× safety factor on CAI allowables.
  • Galvanic corrosion at carbon-aluminum interfaces — carbon is cathodic to aluminum; in saltwater the Al dissolves. Use Tedlar barrier or fiberglass interface ply.
  • 3D printing (Markforged, Anisoprint) — "continuous carbon fiber" in PA matrix gives E₁ ≈ 50 GPa, not 130. Different process, different V_f, different bonding — verify with tensile coupon.
  • Tsai-Hill / Tsai-Wu without ply stresses — failure criteria need stress in each ply's local frame (1, 2, 12) — not the laminate-level stress. Always rotate σ from global to ply frame first.
