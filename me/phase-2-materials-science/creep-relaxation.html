Creep & Stress Relaxation — slow strain under constant load

Apply a constant load to a polymer, soft metal, or hot ceramic, and the strain keeps growing for hours, days, or years. That is creep. Fix the strain (eg. tighten a bolt) and the stress drops over time as the material flows; that is stress relaxation. For thermoplastic mechanical parts — printed brackets, 3D-printed jigs, plastic gears, polymer-housed bolted joints — creep is the dominant long-term failure mode and the reason every M3 screw into a heat-set insert needs re-torquing after a thermal cycle.

The math of secondary (steady-state) creep

The flat middle of the curve has a constant slope: the steady-state creep rate ε-dot. For metals, the dominant relationship is Norton's law:

ε-dot_ss = A · σn · exp(−Q_c / R T) A: material constant; n: stress exponent (3–5 for metals, 1–3 for polymers); Q_c: activation energy for creep; R: 8.314 J/(mol·K); T: absolute temperature (K) → doubling stress on a steel at 600°C with n=5 makes the creep rate 32× faster. → Going from 500°C to 600°C with Q_c = 300 kJ/mol multiplies the rate by exp(300000/8.314 · (1/773 - 1/873)) ≈ 210×.

For polymers, use the simpler Findley power law for short-term creep:

ε(t) = ε0 + m · tn ε0: instantaneous elastic strain at load application; m: stress-dependent coefficient; n: time exponent (0.1–0.3 for many thermoplastics — sub-linear, the strain rate slows over decades).

Stress relaxation — the dual problem

Same material, different boundary condition. Instead of constant stress, the strain is held fixed (eg. a bolt tightened to a fixed elongation). The stress decays:

σ(t) = σ0 · exp(−t / τ) — single-Maxwell-element approximation τ (relaxation time) is the time at which σ falls to 1/e ≈ 37 % of σ0. τ is highly temperature-dependent (Arrhenius). → Bolt into PA12 boss: τ at 25°C might be 30 days; at 60°C might be 30 hours. Heat-cycle a printed assembly and the preload is gone after the first hot day.

Real polymers do not follow a single exponential; they follow a stretched exponential or a sum of relaxation modes. The single τ is a useful first-order estimate.

Homologous temperature — predict creep without testing

The homologous temperature is T / T_m, both in kelvin. Creep becomes significant above:

Metals (BCC, like steels)0.3–0.4Steel (T_m 1810 K) creeps above ~540 K = 270°C

For thermoplastics, T_g is the practical limit: above T_g, the polymer behaves rubbery and the creep rate is enormous. PLA at 60°C is at T_g and will deform under any load. A car interior in summer hits 70°C; PLA parts inside soften and creep.

Worked example — bolt preload loss in a PA-CF printed boss

The setup

Cyberdeck enclosure: M3 screw through a printed PA-CF (carbon-filled nylon) boss into a heat-set brass insert. Tightened to recommended 0.6 N·m preload (~1500 N axial). The boss compresses elastically by ~0.05 mm. Ambient between 10–45°C.

The relaxation prediction

PA-CF T_g ≈ 55°C. Service temp 25°C → T/T_g = 0.91 (absolute K) Relaxation time τ at 25°C ≈ 50 hours (literature; PA-CF datasheets list ~30–100 h) After 24 h: σ/σ0 = exp(−24/50) = 0.62. Preload dropped from 1500 N to 930 N. After 1 week (168 h): σ/σ0 = exp(−168/50) = 0.035. Preload essentially gone.

The mitigation

a) Re-torque after 24 h and again after 7 days. This is why every assembly into plastic recommends "torque check after first heat cycle". b) Use shoulder bolts — metal-on-metal preload bears on a steel shoulder; the plastic boss just carries lateral load. c) Use thread-locker — chemical bond replaces some preload as the friction lock. d) Switch to PEEK or PC for the boss — T_g 145°C; τ at 25°C > 10 years.

Worked example — shelf made of PLA print

The part

Wall-mounted PLA shelf, 200 mm cantilever, 20 mm wide, 5 mm thick. Loaded with 1 kg (10 N) at the tip. Maximum bending stress at the wall:

M = F · L = 10 · 0.2 = 2 N·m σ = M·c/I = 2 · 0.0025 / [(0.02 · 0.005³)/12] = 2 · 0.0025 / 2.08e-10 = 24 MPa

The instant strain (Hooke)

PLA E ≈ 3.5 GPa. Elastic deflection δ = F L³ / (3 E I) = 10 · 0.008 / (3 · 3.5e9 · 2.08e-10) = 36 mm? Recalc: 10 · 0.2³ / (3 · 3.5e9 · 2.08e-10) = 0.08 / 2.18 = 36 mm. Yes, that big. 36 mm is the instantaneous (elastic) tip droop. Already too much for a flat shelf.

The creep prediction

PLA at 24 MPa, 25°C: Findley with m ≈ 5e-4, n ≈ 0.25 gives ε(t) ≈ 0.007 + 5e-4 · t0.25 (t in hours) After 1 year (8760 h): ε = 0.007 + 5e-4 · 9.7 = 0.012. Strain doubled vs. instantaneous. Tip droop after 1 year ≈ 72 mm. After 5 years ≈ 100 mm. The shelf is now a slide. Conclusion: PLA at 24 MPa is not a long-term structural material. PETG would reduce creep ~30 %. PA-CF would be predictable. Metal bracket would not creep meaningfully.

What to notice

  • Polymers creep at room temperature — metals only creep at homologous T > 0.3. Treat every plastic load-bearing part as creep-limited unless the load is far below long-term design stress.
  • Long-term design stress ≠ yield stress — polymer datasheets list "1000-h creep stress" or "creep modulus at 100 MPa · 1 yr". Design to those, not to the tensile yield.
  • Stress relaxation is creep's twin — identical molecular process, different boundary condition. Constant stress → growing strain (creep). Constant strain → falling stress (relaxation).
  • Bolted preload into plastic is doomed — the only safe answers are metal-on-metal (shoulder, washer, insert), thread-locker, or periodic re-torque.
  • Tertiary creep ends in fracture — necking, voids, and acceleration. The "creep life" you read on a datasheet is usually the time to onset of tertiary, not failure.
  • Larson–Miller parameter packs T and time-to-rupture into a single number: LMP = T(20 + log₃₀ t). Extrapolate short, hot tests to long, cool service.
  • Temperature is the lever — doubling T (in K) typically multiplies creep rate by 10–100×. Cooling a polymer part 20°C can extend life 10×.

SHORTCUTS

  • Primary: decelerating creep, work-hardening.
  • Secondary: steady rate, design region.
  • Tertiary: accelerating → rupture.
  • Doubling stress (n=5 metal) ≈ 32× faster.
  • Polymers: design to 1000-h or 1-yr creep modulus.
  • Bolts in plastic: re-torque after first heat cycle.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Using short-term E for long-term polymer design — off by 2–10×.
  • Forgetting T effect — a part qualified at 25°C fails at 60°C in months.
  • Extrapolating linearly — creep is sub-linear in time and super-linear in stress.
  • Designing to yield only — creep limits are usually lower for long-term service.
  • Treating relaxation as decay-to-zero — some polymers (semicrystalline) plateau above zero due to crystalline anchoring.
