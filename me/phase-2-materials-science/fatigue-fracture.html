Fatigue and fracture: cyclic, mean, and notches

Static failure is the easy case — fatigue is sneakier. Parts that survive a single peak load break later under cyclic loads well below UTS. Steel has an endurance limit (a safe stress for infinite life). Aluminum doesn't — it just keeps degrading. Notches amplify nominal stress by K_t, so a tiny hole can move you from "infinite life" to "fails Tuesday."

What to notice

  • Steel's "knee" at ~10⁶ cycles is the endurance limit. Stay below it and the part survives forever (in theory). It's the reason rotating-machinery design uses fatigue strength as the limit, not yield.
  • Aluminum has no knee. Drag the slider low — aluminum cycles climb but never go infinite. For aluminum, design to a target life (e.g., 10⁷ cycles at 0.4·UTS).
  • The Goodman line shrinks the safe region. A part loaded with σ_mean = 0.5·Sy can only sustain σ_alt ≈ 0.5·Se before fatigue failure. Mean stress is not free.
  • Sharper notch = higher K_t. The slider drops r toward zero — σ_max blows up. Press-fit corners, sharp-internal-corners on machined parts, and unfileted bolt threads are real fatigue origins.
  • Use K_t on σ_alt only, not σ_mean (in basic Goodman). Notches concentrate cyclic stress; static stress redistributes via local yielding. Goodman-Soderberg variants refine this.
  • FDM print fatigue is dominated by layer interfaces. Stress concentration at any layer line acts like a built-in notch — orient critical loads in-layer, not across.

ENDURANCE ANCHORS

  • steel — Se ≈ 0.5·UTS at 10⁶ cycles (true knee).
  • aluminum — NO true endurance limit; design to N.
  • FDM — worse fatigue from layer interfaces.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • below UTS ≠ safe — cyclic loads still cause failure.
  • surface finish — rough surface dramatically cuts fatigue life.
  • K_t on σ_alt only — basic Goodman; static stress yields locally.
