Galvanic Corrosion

When two dissimilar metals touch in the presence of an electrolyte (humid air counts), the less-noble one corrodes preferentially — fast. The further apart they sit on the anodic-index chart, the worse. Pick a pair below; the index gap tells you whether you can bolt them together bare, need an insulator, or should just pick a different metal.

0–0.15 V: safe contact in any environment 0.15–0.30 V: safe indoors / dry; insulate outside 0.30–0.50 V: always insulate > 0.50 V: avoid; pick different metals
Verdict: Pick two metals to compare.

Why dissimilar metals corrode (the battery you didn't want)

A galvanic cell needs three things: an anode, a cathode, and an electrolyte path. Bolt aluminum to stainless and you've built one.

1. Every metal has an electrode potential measured against a standard reference (the saturated calomel electrode, SCE). Tabulated as the anodic index. 2. In contact + electrolyte, the metal with the more negative potential becomes the anode and donates electrons (oxidizes, corrodes). 3. The driving voltage is the index difference: ΔV = |V_A − V_B|. 4. Larger ΔV = stronger cell = faster corrosion. Above ~0.5 V, the rate is destructive on any reasonable timescale. 5. → Picking metals close on the chart minimizes the driving voltage. 6. Or you break the loop: break the metal-to-metal path (insulating washer/sleeve) or break the electrolyte path (sealant, paint, dry environment).

Area effect (the cathode-area amplifier)

Galvanic rate scales with the ratio of cathode area to anode area. A small anode in contact with a huge cathode rusts fast; a big anode in contact with a small cathode loses material slowly across its surface.

1. Total galvanic current is set by the driving voltage and the system resistance. That current must leave through the anode surface. 2. Current density at the anode = I_total / A_anode. → Small A_anode = high current density = fast pitting. 3. Disaster case: small zinc rivet through huge stainless plate (small anode, big cathode) — rivet eats away in months. 4. Forgiving case: large galvanized panel with stainless screws (big anode, small cathode) — galvanic loss is spread thin; panel is OK for decades. 5. Rule of thumb: keep cathode-to-anode area ≤ 1 to ≤ 10:1 max. Large galvanic ratios eat fasteners alive.

Worked example — aluminum heat sink, stainless screws, garage workbench

The pair

Aluminum 6061: anodic index ≈ 0.90 V (active) Stainless 304: anodic index ≈ 0.50 V (passive) ΔV = 0.40 V → "always insulate" band Environment: indoor garage with occasional humidity → still acts like outdoor Area ratio: M3 SS304 screw vs Al heatsink → Al much larger anode → favorable, but ΔV still bites at the screw hole over years

Where it bites

Around the screw threads, moisture wicks in, oxide layer breaks down on aluminum near the steel, white pitting appears in 1–3 years. Once the screw is loose, conduction (thermal) drops too — common failure on outdoor amplifier chassis and solar inverters.

Fix #1 — insulator (cheap, retrofit)

Nylon shoulder washer (M3) under screw head: $0.05 each Nylon flat washer above aluminum: blocks electrolyte path Result: contact is steel→nylon→aluminum, no direct steel-aluminum metal contact

Fix #2 — coating (production)

Anodize the aluminum (Type II or Type III). The oxide layer is dielectric. Then it doesn't matter what touches it — the oxide is the insulator. See: Anodize Dimensional Allowance for the thickness budget.

Fix #3 — pick a compatible fastener (best)

Aluminum screw (6061 or 7075): ΔV = 0 → no galvanic cell at all Or 316 stainless if marine context (still 0.4 V but more passive) Or anodized aluminum + anodized screw → both surfaces dielectric

What to notice

  • Magnesium loses to everything — Mg is at the bottom of every chart. Pair it with anything and the magnesium goes. Used deliberately as a sacrificial anode on boats and water heaters (replace the rod every few years; it saves the steel hull).
  • Gold and platinum win — at the top, they almost never corrode in air. That's why high-reliability connector contacts are gold-flashed: even if humid, gold doesn't oxidize, and the gold layer doesn't form a galvanic cell with the underlying nickel (nickel undercoat is standard).
  • Stainless isn't immortal in salt air — 304 will pit; 316 (with Mo) resists better but still suffers if mated with mild steel. Marine fasteners want 316, ideally with Tef-Gel or zinc-chromate sealant in the joint.
  • Solder is a hidden anode — SnPb (0.7 V) and lead-free SAC (~0.65 V) are more active than copper (~0.35 V), so the solder joint is the anode against the copper pad. Conformal coating on PCBs partly solves this.
  • Galvanized steel is "intentionally" galvanic — the zinc layer is sacrificial. Galvanic action eats the zinc, not the steel underneath, until the zinc is gone — then the steel goes.
  • Anodize before assembly — anodizing inside a stack-up is impossible; do it on individual parts before they're pressed/bolted. Mask threads if needed.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

Driving voltage
ΔV = |V_A − V_B|
Severity band
indoor: ΔV < 0.15 safe
outdoor: ΔV < 0.25 safe
marine: ΔV < 0.15 safe
Anode rate
∝ I × A_cathode / A_anode
Break loop
insulator or seal electrolyte

SAFE PAIRS

  • Al + Al — ΔV = 0, always
  • SS + SS — ΔV = 0, always
  • Al + SS — 0.4 V, insulate
  • Cu + brass — < 0.1 V, safe
  • SS + Ti — < 0.1 V, safe
  • Steel + Zn-plate — sacrificial, intentional

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Carbon-fiber composite + Al — carbon is very noble (top of chart). CF panels with Al fasteners corrode fast in salt air. Use Ti fasteners or isolate.
  • Brass fittings on Al plumbing — common in solar panels, blame for ~30% of leaks after 5 years. Use 316SS or Al fittings.
  • Small Zn rivet, big SS sheet — rivet evaporates in months. Area ratio matters as much as ΔV.
  • Crevice corrosion in 316 — even "safe" stainless pits inside O-ring grooves where O2 can't replenish. Specify Cr-rich super-duplex if you have such joints in seawater.
  • Aluminum + concrete — fresh concrete is alkaline; eats Al window frames if not painted/anodized.
