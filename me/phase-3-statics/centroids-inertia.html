Centroids & Moment of Inertia — Composite Shapes

Build a cross-section from rectangles, circles, and triangles. The composite-area method gives the centroid (x̄, ȳ); the parallel-axis theorem gives the moment of inertia about the centroidal axes. Holes go in as negative-area shapes — they pull the centroid away from where they sit.

What to notice

  • Centroid = area-weighted average of sub-centroids. x̄ = ΣA_i·x_i / ΣA_i, ȳ = ΣA_i·y_i / ΣA_i. The bigger a sub-shape, the more it pulls the centroid toward itself.
  • Holes are negative areas. Subtract A·x and A·y for the hole's region. The centroid shifts away from the hole — material moves opposite the missing chunk.
  • Axes of symmetry contain the centroid. If the shape has a vertical axis of symmetry, x̄ is on that axis. If horizontal, ȳ is. Use symmetry to skip integrations whenever possible.
  • I about centroidal axis = sum of (I_cg + A·d²) for each sub-shape. The parallel-axis theorem transfers each sub-shape's inertia (taken about its own centroid) over to the composite centroid using the squared offset d.
  • I scales with the cube of dimension perpendicular to bending. A rectangle's I_x = b·h³/12 — height h enters cubed. Double the height: 8× the stiffness. Width only enters linearly. That's why beams are tall, not fat.
  • The A·d² term often dominates for I-beams. The flanges' own centroidal I is small, but they sit far from the composite centroid — A·d² is huge. That's the I-beam's secret.
  • Polar moment J = I_x + I_y. For circular cross-sections in torsion: J = π·d⁴/32. Combines bending stiffnesses about both axes.

MEMORIZE

  • symmetry — centroid always lies on any axis of symmetry.
  • holes — treat as NEGATIVE area in the composite sum.
  • circles — J = I_x + I_y (perpendicular-axis theorem).

COMMON PITFALLS

  • which axis? — I depends on axis; always specify which one.
  • units — centroid in length, A in length², I in length⁴. Don't mix.
