Distributed Loads — Equivalent Resultant

A distributed load w(x) is mathematically just an integral of force per unit length. For equilibrium purposes you can replace it with a single equivalent concentrated load: magnitude equal to the area under w(x), located at the centroid of that area. Switch profiles and watch the resultant magnitude and position update — along with the support reactions.

What to notice

  • R = area under the load curve. Doesn't matter how the load is distributed — for ΣF and ΣM you only need the area (total) and its centroid.
  • Triangular load: resultant at L/3 from the larger (peak) end. If the triangle's peak is at the right end, the centroid is at 2L/3 from the left — i.e., L/3 from the right (the peak). This is the most-tested fact about distributed loads.
  • Trapezoidal = rectangle + triangle. Split it, find the resultant of each piece, sum the two forces, and use weighted-average for the combined centroid. Or use the closed-form formula above.
  • The resultant only works for external equilibrium. When you compute internal shear and bending moment inside the beam, you must use the actual distributed load, not the lumped resultant.
  • Support reactions follow from ΣF = 0 and ΣM = 0. Sum moments about one support to eliminate that support's reaction from the equation — the other support's reaction falls out in one line.
  • Moving the resultant closer to a support shifts load onto that support. A right-heavy triangular load puts more weight on the right reaction. Drag the start/end of the profile to see this happen live.
