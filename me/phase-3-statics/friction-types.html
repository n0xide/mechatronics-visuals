Friction Types — static, kinetic, rolling, belt, wedge

Five distinct phenomena hide under the single word "friction". Each has its own formula, its own coefficient, its own design intuition. This visual builds them from a shared physics base (the Coulomb model), then layers in the special cases — rolling resistance for wheels, the capstan (belt) equation for rope around a post, the wedge for self-locking ramps, and the friction-angle trick that turns ratios into geometry.

Steel on steel (dry) μ_s ≈ 0.6, μ_k ≈ 0.4 Steel on steel (lubed) μ_s ≈ 0.1, μ_k ≈ 0.05 PLA on PLA μ_s ≈ 0.3, μ_k ≈ 0.2 Rubber on dry asphalt μ_s ≈ 0.9 Rolling resistance C_rr 0.002 (steel/steel) — 0.015 (rubber tire) Capstan: 3 turns at μ=0.3 → 290× force multiplication

The Coulomb model — one formula, two regimes

Two surfaces in contact resist relative motion. The friction force opposes (or would oppose) sliding and is proportional to the normal force, with a coefficient that depends on whether they are currently slipping:

Static (not yet slipping): |F_friction| ≤ μ_s N. → The friction force matches whatever applied force is trying to move the body, up to the limit μ_s N. Kinetic (already slipping): F_friction = μ_k N, opposing the velocity. Constant, independent of speed (in the basic model). Always μ_s > μ_k — this is why a static object takes more force to start moving than to keep moving. Stick-slip oscillation lives here. → Friction is independent of contact area in the Coulomb model. (Surprising but consistent with experiment for hard, dry surfaces. Soft elastomers and ploughing contacts violate this.)

The block on an incline — live experiment

Slowly tilt a plane under a block. Increase the angle θ. Two questions:

  1. At what angle does the block begin to slide? (Answer: tan θ_s = μ_s. The angle of repose.)
  2. What angle will keep it sliding at constant velocity once moving? (Answer: tan θ_k = μ_k.)

The friction angle — the geometric trick

Define φ = arctan μ. Then:

The reaction force on the block (sum of normal N and friction F) leans away from the surface normal by angle φ in the direction of impending motion. If the load on the block (gravity, applied force) lies inside the friction cone of half-angle φ, the block doesn't move. If it points outside the cone, the block slides. → The "stick" condition becomes a pure-geometry test, no algebra. → On an incline: gravity is along the vertical. It lies inside the friction cone iff θ < φ. Threshold θ_s = φ = arctan μ_s.

Five friction phenomena, side by side

Capstan (belt) friction — the wrap-angle multiplier

A rope or belt wrapped θ radians around a fixed cylinder, with tension T_1 on the loose end and T_2 on the holding end. The cylinder is held; the rope is on the verge of slipping toward T_2 (the larger tension). Coulomb friction along every infinitesimal arc gives:

Element dN = T · dθ (the wrap force, per arc element) Friction at the verge: dT = μ dN = μ T dθ Integrating: ln(T_2 / T_1) = μ θ T_2 / T_1 = eμθ — the capstan equation → Note: exponential in wrap angle. A few extra turns dominate.

Wedge equilibrium — self-locking ramps

A wedge with a half-angle α under a load W. To lift the load, push horizontally with force P. Two contact surfaces, each with friction angle φ = arctan μ.

Force to lift: P_lift = W · tan(α + 2φ) (both surfaces resisting the push) Force to hold (load wants to push wedge back out): P_hold = W · tan(α − 2φ) Self-locking condition: P_hold ≤ 0 ⇔ α ≤ 2φ → If wedge angle is smaller than twice the friction angle, the wedge stays in place without continuous force. Door stops, tapered shafts, woodworking wedges all rely on this.

Rolling resistance — the wheel's penalty

A rolling wheel does not slide, so kinetic friction does not apply. But energy is still dissipated — in elastic deformation of the wheel/surface (hysteresis), micro-slip at the contact patch, axle bearings. Lumped together: rolling resistance coefficient C_rr.

F_rolling = C_rr · N — same form as Coulomb, much smaller coefficient. Power lost rolling: P = F_rolling · v

Worked example — lab cart on a printed PETG ramp

The setup

3 kg rolling lab cart (4 nylon wheels, ball-bearing center) on a printed PETG ramp at 8°. Will it roll on its own? If yes, what is its terminal-ish speed?

The static check (Coulomb does NOT apply here)

Rolling wheels — not sliding. Use C_rr, not μ_s. Nylon-on-PETG with ball bearings: C_rr ≈ 0.01 (conservative) Driving force (gravity along ramp): W sinθ = 3 · 9.81 · sin 8° = 4.10 N Resistive force: C_rr · W cosθ = 0.01 · 3 · 9.81 · cos 8° = 0.291 N 4.10 > 0.291 — cart accelerates. Net force 3.81 N → a = 1.27 m/s²

If it were sliding instead (block, not wheels)

PETG-on-PETG static μ_s ≈ 0.35 → φ = 19.3°. 8° < 19.3° → no slip, block sits. Need to tilt to 19.3° before the block moves at all.

Lesson

Wheels turn an 8° "stuck block" problem into an "accelerating cart" problem. C_rr at 0.01 vs. μ_s at 0.35 — 35× reduction. This is why wheels were invented.

What to notice

  • Static > kinetic > rolling — for the same materials, in this order, each one typically 2–10× less than the previous.
  • Friction angle φ = arctan μ — converts the algebraic condition into a geometric one. Vectors inside the friction cone don't slide.
  • Capstan is exponential — small μ and modest wrap angle give large force multiplication. Three turns of rope around a bollard with μ = 0.3 multiplies by e0.3 · 6π = e5.65 = 285×.
  • Wedge half-angle test α < 2φ for self-locking — rule of thumb: 7° wedge with μ = 0.3 (φ = 16.7°) self-locks easily.
  • Lubrication kills static-kinetic gap — oiled steel-on-steel is nearly the same coefficient sliding or stuck. Stick-slip dies out.
  • Independence of contact area is a hard-surface approximation — rubber tires, soft plastics violate it; real contact area scales with load (plastic deformation of asperities), restoring the μ-independent-of-area result by a different mechanism.
  • Rolling resistance is NOT friction in the slip sense — it's hysteresis + micro-slip + bearing loss. Lumped into a "coefficient" because the math looks the same.

SHORTCUTS

  • μ_s > μ_k — always, hence stick-slip.
  • φ_steel-dry ≈ 31° (μ 0.6).
  • φ_steel-lubed ≈ 6° (μ 0.1).
  • 3 turns, μ=0.3: 285× force boost.
  • Bike tire: C_rr 0.003.
  • Wheels ≈ 35× less friction than sliding.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Using μ_s when sliding — once moving, switch to μ_k.
  • Assuming F = μN always — static is ≤, not =.
  • Forgetting contact-area independence for hard pairs is OK; for rubber/plastic it isn't.
  • Tip-over vs. slide — tall blocks tip before sliding; the friction calc is irrelevant.
  • Forgetting both wedge surfaces — "α ≤ 2φ" has the factor of 2 because of two contact surfaces.
  • Treating belt as Coulomb — belt-on-pulley is capstan, exponential, not linear.
