Hydrostatics — pressure, buoyancy, submerged surfaces

A fluid at rest exerts pressure equally in every direction, and that pressure grows linearly with depth. Three formulas (P = ρ g h, F_b = ρ g V, F = ρ g h_c A) cover most engineering questions: how deep before a thin-wall tank bursts, how much buoyancy a hull provides, what force the gate of a dam sees, and what to write on a manometer reading. The fundamental equation — P = ρ g h

Consider a column of static fluid, area A, height h. The weight of the column equals the pressure-times-area at its base:

Weight = (ρ V) g = (ρ A h) g Pressure at base = weight / A = ρ g h Independent of cross-section shape, independent of total fluid volume, only depends on the vertical distance from the free surface. → A 1-cm-wide straw and an Olympic pool both produce the same pressure at the same depth.

The total (absolute) pressure at depth h includes the pressure above the free surface (usually atmospheric):

P_abs(h) = P_atm + ρ g h Gauge pressure P_g = P_abs − P_atm = ρ g h Engineering numbers are almost always gauge unless they say "absolute". Vacuum is below atmospheric, gauge negative.

Buoyancy — Archimedes' principle

A submerged or floating body displaces fluid. The buoyancy force equals the weight of the displaced fluid:

F_b = ρ_fluid · g · V_displaced Direction: straight up (opposing gravity). → Floating: body floats with the fraction submerged equal to ρ_body / ρ_fluid (the "draft" ratio). → Submerged: net force = weight − buoyancy = (ρ_body − ρ_fluid) V g.

Force on a submerged vertical surface

A flat surface (a tank wall, a dam, a lock gate) submerged vertically. Pressure varies linearly with depth, so the total force and its point of application both follow from the centroid math:

Total force: F = ρ g h_c A — where h_c is the depth of the area's centroid and A is its area. Point of application (center of pressure): h_p = h_c + I_xx / (h_c A) — below the centroid, because pressure is heavier at the bottom. For a rectangle of width b and height d, fully submerged with its top at depth h_top: → h_c = h_top + d/2, A = b d, F = ρ g (h_top + d/2) (b d) → If top is at the free surface (h_top = 0): F = ρ g (d/2) (b d) = ρ g b d² / 2 → Point of application: h_p = d/2 + (b d³ / 12) / (d/2 · b d) = d/2 + d/6 = 2d/3 from top (= d/3 from bottom)

Manometers — reading pressure with a U-tube

A U-tube partially filled with a known-density manometer fluid (often mercury or water). Pressure at the two free surfaces sets the height difference:

Connect one side to the system (P_sys); leave the other open to atmosphere. If P_sys > P_atm, the manometer fluid drops on the system side and rises on the open side. Height difference Δh. P_sys − P_atm = ρ_manometer · g · Δh (gauge pressure of the system). Mercury (13.6 g/cc) is preferred for high pressures: 1 atm reads as only 760 mmHg, compact column. Water for low gauges (HVAC, gas burners): same 1 atm = 10.33 m of water, impractical column.

Worked example — bottom of an aquarium

The aquarium

Hobby reef tank: 1.2 m long, 0.6 m wide, 0.6 m deep. Filled with seawater (ρ = 1025 kg/m³). How much force does the bottom support? How much force is on each end wall?

Pressure at the bottom

P_bottom = ρ g h = 1025 · 9.81 · 0.6 = 6033 Pa = 6.03 kPa (gauge) Force on bottom = P · A = 6033 · (1.2 · 0.6) = 4344 N = 443 kg-force Sanity check: weight of water = 1025 · (1.2 · 0.6 · 0.6) · 9.81 = 4343 N ✓

Force on each end wall (0.6 m wide × 0.6 m tall, submerged from the top)

h_c (centroid depth) = 0.3 m F = ρ g h_c A = 1025 · 9.81 · 0.3 · (0.6 · 0.6) = 1086 N Point of application: h_p = 0.3 + (0.6 · 0.6³/12) / (0.3 · 0.36) = 0.3 + 0.1 = 0.4 m (= 2/3 of height from top) Sanity: standard answer for a wall extending from free surface to depth d is the resultant at d/3 from the bottom ✓

Worked example — cyberdeck enclosure under hydrostatic pressure (waterproofing)

The scenario

You drop a fold-p4-pico cyberdeck (PETG enclosure) into 2 m of fresh water. Wall thickness 3 mm, internal volume 1 L. Three questions: how much net buoyancy? what pressure on the seal? does the case implode?

Buoyancy

Mass of cyberdeck: 400 g. Weight: 0.4 · 9.81 = 3.92 N (downward) External volume ≈ 1.2 L = 1.2e-3 m³ (internal 1 L plus ≈ 0.2 L of wall and bezel material at 3 mm thickness; air-filled interior, so the case displaces its full external volume when submerged) Buoyancy (if fully submerged): 1000 · 9.81 · 1.2e-3 = 11.77 N (upward) → net 7.85 N up — the case floats; submerged fraction ≈ 400 / 1200 = 33 %

Pressure on the seal at 2 m depth

P = 1000 · 9.81 · 2 = 19.62 kPa (gauge) Force on a 100 mm × 60 mm seal face: 19620 · 0.006 = 117.7 N pushing on the seal If using a 2 mm-wide O-ring rated for ~3 bar = 300 kPa: well within limits. Sealing is governed by seal design, not pressure magnitude.

Wall stress

Thin flat plate of 100 mm × 80 mm under 20 kPa: max stress ≈ βPa²/t² ≈ 0.3 · 20000 · 0.08² / 0.003² ≈ 4.3 MPa. PETG yield ~50 MPa — 12× margin. Conclusion: case survives easily, floats, seal is the failure point if any.

What to notice

  • Shape doesn't matter for pressure — only the vertical depth. A skinny straw and a vast lake both give ρgh at depth h.
  • 1 m of water ≈ 10 kPa ≈ 1.45 psi — useful shortcut. Every 10 m of water adds another atm.
  • Buoyancy = displaced fluid weight, NOT body weight. A 1 kg steel ball in water displaces ~0.13 L → ~0.13 kg-force of buoyancy (net 0.87 kg-force pulls it down, so it sinks). A 1 kg balsa block (ρ ≈ 150 kg/m³) fully submerged would displace ~6.7 L → 6.7 kg-force of buoyancy — far more than its weight — so it pops to the surface and floats with only ~15 % submerged, where the displaced-water weight finally matches the 1 kg block weight.
  • Float fraction = ρ_body / ρ_fluid — an iceberg (ρ 917) in seawater (ρ 1025) shows 11 % above water. A wood log (ρ 700) in fresh water shows 30 % above.
  • Submerged-surface force resultant acts BELOW the centroid — because pressure is heavier at the bottom. For a wall from surface to depth d, it acts at d/3 above the bottom.
  • Atmospheric pressure cancels in many problems — both sides of a tank wall see 1 atm pushing equally. Only the gauge contribution matters for net force.
  • Hydrostatic paradox — the force on the bottom of a flared vessel can exceed the weight of the water inside it (the slanted walls add downward components). Force-on-bottom = ρgh · A_bottom regardless.

SHORTCUTS

  • 10 m water = 1 atm = 100 kPa.
  • 1 atm = 760 mmHg = 14.7 psi.
  • Iceberg 89 % submerged.
  • Surface to depth d wall: F=ρgbd²/2, line of action 2d/3 down.
  • Submarine at 100 m: 11 atm absolute (10 atm gauge).
  • Plumbing: each story (3 m) = ~30 kPa = 4.4 psi.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Mixing gauge / absolute — pump curves use gauge; gas-law calcs need absolute.
  • Treating buoyancy as body weight — it's the displaced fluid's weight.
  • Forgetting trapped air — a sealed container holds more buoyancy than a solid block of the same outer shape.
  • Wrong centroid depth h_c — for tilted plates, measure from free surface to the centroid of the plate's projection.
  • Mixing column units — "9 inches of vacuum" on a gauge means 9 inHg below atm = 30.5 kPa below 1 atm.
