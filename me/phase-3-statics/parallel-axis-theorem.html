Parallel-Axis Theorem

Move the rotation axis away from a body's centroid and the moment of inertia grows by A · d². That's parallel-axis. Drag the offset and watch Itotal grow quadratically — the basis for I-beam design, why a flange far from the neutral axis dominates stiffness, and why hollow shafts beat solid ones per unit mass.

Where the theorem comes from

The moment of inertia about an axis is the integral of distance² over the area. Shift the axis by a constant d, and that constant pops outside the integral.

1. About centroid C: I_C = ∫ y² dA where y is measured from C (centroid axis). 2. Define new axis O parallel to C, offset by d (axis O is d units away from C in the +y direction). Distance from any element dA at coordinate y to the new axis: y' = y − d. (The element's position is measured from C; subtract the offset to re-zero on O.) 3. About O: I_O = ∫ (y − d)² dA = ∫ y² dA − 2d ∫ y dA + ∫ d² dA 4. Middle integral vanishes: ∫ y dA = 0 by definition of the centroid (first moment about its own centroid is zero). 5. Last integral: ∫ d² dA = d² · A (constant d). 6. → I_O = I_C + A · d² 7. This only works when one axis is the centroidal axis. You can't apply parallel-axis between two arbitrary axes.

Composite sections — I-beam as flanges + web

The real power of parallel-axis: any complex shape can be broken into simple sub-shapes whose centroidal inertias you know. Compute each piece's contribution about the overall centroid by parallel-axis, then sum.

In an I-beam, the web's centroidal axis sits on the neutral axis (d = 0 → I_web contributes only its own I_c). The two flanges are offset by half the beam height, so each contributes ~A_flange · d² → dominant terms. Why an I-beam is efficient: it puts most of its material at large d.

Worked example — composite L-section

The shape

An angle (L-section): horizontal leg 100 mm wide × 20 mm thick, vertical leg 80 mm tall (overall) × 20 mm thick. The two legs share a 20 × 20 corner block. To avoid double-counting the overlap, model the horizontal leg as the full 100 × 20 and split the vertical leg into only the part above the horizontal — i.e., 20 wide × 60 tall (the 80 mm overall height minus the 20 mm of horizontal-leg thickness). Find I about the section's horizontal centroidal axis.

Step 1 — find the section centroid

A_h = 100 × 20 = 2,000 mm², centroid (50, 10) horizontal leg spans y = 0 to 20 A_v = 20 × 60 = 1,200 mm², centroid (10, 50) vertical-leg piece spans y = 20 to 80, midpoint y = 50 A_total = 3,200 mm² ȳ = (2000 · 10 + 1200 · 50) / 3200 = (20,000 + 60,000)/3200 = 25.0 mm

Step 2 — centroidal I of each piece

I_c,h (own centroid) = 100 · 20³ / 12 = 66,667 mm⁴ (bh³/12 about horizontal axis) I_c,v = 20 · 60³ / 12 = 360,000 mm⁴

Step 3 — parallel-axis to section centroid

d_h = ȳ − 10 = 15 mm → A_h · d² = 2000 · 225 = 450,000 mm⁴ d_v = 50 − ȳ = 25 mm → A_v · d² = 1200 · 625 = 750,000 mm⁴ I_total = (66,667 + 450,000) + (360,000 + 750,000) = 1.63 × 10⁶ mm⁴

Cross-check via "where's the mass?"

Most of the moment of inertia comes from the parallel-axis terms (1.20 × 10⁶), not the centroidal terms (0.43 × 10⁶). The Steiner translation dominates — typical for thin sections far from the centroid. Confirms why beam designers spread material vertically.

What to notice

  • I grows with d², not linearly — double the offset and you quadruple the contribution. Triple it and you 9×. That's why I-beams put the flanges at the extreme top and bottom; even a thin flange contributes more than a thick web.
  • I_O ≥ I_C always — parallel-axis only adds, never subtracts. The centroidal axis has the minimum moment of inertia of any parallel axis.
  • Use only with the centroidal axis as reference — the derivation depends on the linear-moment integral being zero. Between two arbitrary parallel axes A and B, you'd have to go A → centroid → B with two parallel-axis transfers (with d² of each leg).
  • Radius of gyration k = √(I/A) — a single number summarizing the "effective" offset. For a centroidal axis, k is small; for offset axes, k grows with d. Used in column buckling: slenderness ratio L/k.
  • Hollow shaft beats solid — same mass, you can spread the material away from the centroid and crank I way up. Bicycle frames, drive shafts, gold-bar safe doors — all hollow.
  • I-beam's web carries shear, not bending — bending is resisted by flanges (huge d → huge I); web carries the vertical shear flow. So you can drill holes in the web (near neutral axis), but never near the flanges.

RECIPE

  • 1. Break composite into known sub-shapes
  • 2. Find each centroid + total centroid
  • 3. Get I_c about each sub-shape's own centroid
  • 4. Add A · d² for each (d to overall centroid)
  • 5. Sum all contributions

PITFALLS

  • Wrong axis reference — the +A·d² is from CENTROID to new axis. Not between two arbitrary axes.
  • Wrong sign on d — d is squared, so sign doesn't matter, but be consistent in finding centroid first.
  • Confusing I with J — I_x (rectangular section, bending) vs J (polar, torsion). Same parallel-axis form but different I_c.
  • Forgetting cutouts subtract — for a beam with a hole, I_hole gets a NEGATIVE A·d². The hole area is treated as negative.
  • Mixing units — mm⁴, m⁴, in⁴ differ by factors of 10¹², 416,231. Always check.
