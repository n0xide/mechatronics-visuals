Beam Deflection

Four classic deflection formulas (Euler–Bernoulli, linear-elastic, small-deflection, constant EI). Pick one, slide the parameters, watch the deflected shape. The same EI shows up in every formula — bending stiffness is what fights deflection. Most importantly: δ scales with L³ (point load) or L⁴ (distributed) — small length increases turn into big sag.

What to notice

  • Cantilever vs simply-supported, same load: a cantilever with end load deflects 16× more than a simply-supported beam with the same midspan load (PL³/3EI vs PL³/48EI). Cantilevers are inherently floppy.
  • UDL is gentler than concentrated load. A SS beam with UDL of total wL = P deflects 5wL⁴/384EI = 5/8 of PL³/48EI = 5PL³/(384EI), compared to PL³/48EI = 8PL³/384EI — UDL deflects ~5/8 as much.
  • Height h enters cubed. Doubling beam height divides deflection by 8 AND quarters the stress. Doubling width divides deflection by 2 only. Always go taller before wider.
  • EI is "bending stiffness." Same EI = same deflection for the same load. A steel beam and an aluminum beam of equal EI deflect equally — but the aluminum one is fatter and lighter.
  • Don't trust thin 3D-printed cantilevers. A 100 mm PETG bracket (E≈2 GPa, ~100× softer than steel) deflects ~100× more under the same load. Most "warped" prints aren't manufacturing defects; they're elastic deflection.
  • The deflected shape is rescaled to fill the figure. Real elastic deflection is usually 0.1–2% of the span — visually negligible. The image rescales δ_max to a fixed pixel size so you can see the shape; the true δ is printed below the figure.

MEMORIZE

  • δ ∝ L³ for point loads, L⁴ for distributed.
  • Superposition works — linear elastic loading is additive.
  • E·I rules stiffness — material AND geometry, not mass.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Adding mass ≠ adding stiffness — doubling rect height gives 8× stiffness (h³). Mass doubles, stiffness 8×.
  • Superposition fails past yield — only valid in linear elastic regime.
