Indeterminate Axial Members

Two unknowns, one equilibrium equation — equilibrium alone doesn't solve the problem. The trick: add a compatibility equation that ties the deformations of the two members together. A bolt-in-sleeve preload, a heated bar between fixed walls, a composite shaft loaded at its ends — same recipe in all of them: equilibrium + compatibility + material laws.

Why "indeterminate" — and what's missing

A statically determinate axial problem has one equilibrium equation and one unknown: solve ΣF = 0, done. An indeterminate problem has two members carrying a shared load (or two reactions to a single load) — that's two unknowns, but still only one equilibrium equation. The extra equation must come from how the geometry connects.

Determinate: one force unknown, ΣF = 0 → solve. Example: a single bolt pulling on a flange. Indeterminate (degree 1): two force unknowns, one equilibrium equation. Need one compatibility equation. Example: bolt + sleeve in compression, both attached to the same joint. Indeterminate (degree 2): three unknowns, one equilibrium → need two compatibility equations. Three-bar truss, etc.

The three-equation recipe

Every indeterminate axial problem reduces to:

StepEquationWhat it says
1. EquilibriumΣF = 0Internal forces in the members balance the applied external load.
2. Compatibilityδ_1 = ±δ_2 (or some geometric relation)The members' deformations are tied by how they're physically connected.
3. Materialδ = F·L / (A·E)Hooke's law for axial members — relates force to deformation.

Three equations, two force unknowns + the geometric coupling. Solve simultaneously.

Case 1 — bolt-in-sleeve preload

The classic. A bolt passes through a sleeve (or stack of washers, or a plate). When the nut is torqued down, the bolt stretches (tension) by an amount the sleeve gets squashed (compression). Both share the same final length — the bolt and sleeve are clamped at the same points.

Initial misfit: bolt is δ_0 shorter than needed (or the sleeve is δ_0 too long), so the nut must close the gap. Equilibrium after assembly: F_b = F_s (no external force; bolt pulls, sleeve pushes back equally) Compatibility — final lengths match: δ_b + δ_s = δ_0 (bolt stretches + sleeve shortens = gap closed) Materials: δ_b = F·L / (A_b · E_b) · δ_s = F·L / (A_s · E_s) Sub in: δ_0 = F·L · (1 / (A_b·E_b) + 1 / (A_s·E_s)) → F = δ_0 / (L · (1/(A_b·E_b) + 1/(A_s·E_s)))

Notice the form: F is proportional to δ_0 (preload comes from the initial misfit) and falls with L (longer joint, more compliant). The bolt and sleeve appear as parallel springs in the compatibility equation; their effective stiffness is the sum of individual stiffnesses, and the preload force is the effective stiffness times the gap.

Case 2 — heated bar between fixed walls

A solid bar fits exactly between two rigid walls. Heat it up by ΔT. The bar wants to expand by α·L·ΔT but it can't — the walls don't move. The wall reactions develop an axial compression that exactly cancels the would-be thermal expansion.

Thermal expansion if free: δ_T = α · L · ΔT Mechanical shortening by wall force: δ_M = −F · L / (A · E) (compression) Compatibility — walls don't move: δ_T + δ_M = 0 Solve: → F = α · ΔT · A · E · σ = α · ΔT · E Stress is independent of length and cross-section — only material properties and ΔT.

The thermal stress σ = α·E·ΔT is one of the most useful results in mechanics. For steel (α ≈ 12·10⁻⁶ / °C, E = 200 GPa), a 50 °C rise gives σ = 12·10⁻⁶ · 200·10⁹ · 50 = 120 MPa — already 25% of yield for a typical structural steel, and the bar hasn't moved at all. This is why heated structures need expansion joints: relieve the constraint, eliminate the stress.

Case 3 — composite bar (steel sleeve + aluminum core, end-loaded)

Two materials bonded along their length, loaded together at the ends. The total load P is shared between them; the shared end-displacement gives the compatibility equation.

Equilibrium: P = F_1 + F_2 Compatibility: δ_1 = δ_2 (both stretch together — they're bonded) → F_1·L / (A_1·E_1) = F_2·L / (A_2·E_2) → F_1 / F_2 = (A_1·E_1) / (A_2·E_2) Stress ratio: σ_1 / σ_2 = E_1 / E_2 (stiffer material carries more stress per unit area)

The stiffer member always carries more load. For a steel-aluminum composite, the steel carries ~3× the stress that the aluminum sees — even at the same strain. Designers exploit this in composite shafts: wrap a low-stiffness core (aluminum, plastic) with a high-stiffness skin (steel, CF) to direct load into the skin while keeping the part light.

Worked example — M6 steel bolt in aluminum sleeve, ΔT raise

The setup

M6 steel bolt (A_b = 20.1 mm², E_b = 200 GPa, α_b = 12·10⁻⁶ /°C) passes through an aluminum sleeve (A_s = 100 mm², E_s = 70 GPa, α_s = 23·10⁻⁶ /°C). Length L = 100 mm. Initial assembly preload F_0 = 5 kN (bolt tensile, sleeve compressive). Heat to ΔT = +50 °C.

Differential thermal expansion adds a compatibility term

δ_b_free = α_b · L · ΔT = 12·10⁻⁶ · 100 · 50 = 0.060 mm (bolt wants to grow) δ_s_free = α_s · L · ΔT = 23·10⁻⁶ · 100 · 50 = 0.115 mm (sleeve wants to grow more) Δδ_thermal = δ_s_free − δ_b_free = +0.055 mm (sleeve wants 0.055 mm more length than bolt)

Compatibility with combined loads

Mechanical lengths must match (they're clamped together): δ_b_mech − δ_s_mech = −Δδ_thermal F·L · (1/(A_b·E_b) + 1/(A_s·E_s)) = Δδ_thermal (ΔF added by thermal misfit) k_eff = 1 / (L · (1/(A_b·E_b) + 1/(A_s·E_s))) = 1 / (100 / (20.1·200·1000) + 100 / (100·70·1000)) = 1 / (0.0249·10⁻³ + 0.0143·10⁻³) = 1 / (0.0392·10⁻³) = 25.5 kN/mm ΔF = k_eff · Δδ_thermal = 25.5 · 0.055 = 1.40 kN (thermal preload increase)

Result

Final bolt tension: F = 5.0 + 1.4 = 6.4 kN (28% higher than at assembly) Bolt stress: σ_b = 6400 / 20.1 = 318 MPa (below the 8.8-class proof of 580 MPa, OK)

This is why heated steel-on-aluminum joints overload bolts — aluminum expands twice as much as steel, the joint tries to grow, the bolt resists, and the bolt sees a thermal preload increase on top of the assembly preload. Use a Belleville washer stack or a longer-grip bolt to soften the joint and absorb the thermal excursion.

What to notice

  • Stiffness ratio decides load split. Two parallel members share a load in proportion to A·E. The stiffer one always carries more. Same idea as parallel springs — pull both, the stiff one pulls back harder.
  • Thermal stress is geometry-independent. σ = α·E·ΔT carries no length or area term. A 1 mm bar fully constrained between rigid walls sees the same stress as a 1 m bar. Only the material and the ΔT matter.
  • Compatibility = "fit it back together". The pre-deformation step is "let everything do what it wants" — measure those displacements. The post-step is "now force them to share endpoints" — that's where the internal forces come from.
  • Bolt-in-sleeve preload falls if joint relaxes. Bolted joints lose preload to embedding (sleeve creep, paint settling, thread bedding-in) — about 5–15% in the first hours. Re-torque after run-in for safety-critical joints.
  • Two-material joints in changing temperature need compliance. If steel-on-aluminum joints are clamped tight at room temp, hot operation overloads the bolts; cold operation under-clamps. Add a wave washer, Belleville, or extended-grip bolt to soften the joint.
  • The compatibility equation depends on how the parts connect. "δ_1 = δ_2" (both stretch the same), "δ_1 = −δ_2" (one stretches, the other compresses, gap closes), "δ_1 + δ_2 = δ_total" (series) — different geometries give different equations. Draw the deformation first.

Where you'll see this

Real situations

  • Bolted flanges — pressure-vessel and pipe-flange bolts share load with the gasket/flange compression. Indeterminate. Standard design tables (ASME) already integrate the math.
  • Press-fit bushings — interference fit creates initial misfit δ_0; equilibrium + compatibility gives the contact pressure between bushing and bore.
  • Composite shafts / CF wraps — carbon fiber wrapped on aluminum core; the CF carries most of the torsion stress because of higher stiffness.
  • Heated machine frames — long aluminum extrusions bolted to steel base plates can warp or crack under sun exposure. Either let them slide (slotted holes) or accept the thermal stress.
  • Bridge expansion joints — every span over ~30 m has one. Without it, summer-vs-winter thermal stress would crack the deck.
  • PCB through-hole solder joints — copper via, FR-4 substrate, BGA ball. Different α, same temperature cycle, repeated stress → fatigue cracks. The compatibility equation predicts the strain range.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

  • δ = F·L / (A·E) axial deformation
  • σ = α·E·ΔT thermal stress, fully constrained
  • δ_T = α·L·ΔT free thermal expansion
  • F_1/F_2 = (A_1·E_1)/(A_2·E_2) load split, equal δ
  • k = A·E / L axial stiffness (spring)
  • k_parallel = k_1 + k_2 bolt + sleeve

NUMBERS TO MEMORIZE

  • α_steel ≈ 12·10⁻⁶ /°C, α_aluminum ≈ 23·10⁻⁶ /°C
  • α_copper ≈ 17·10⁻⁶, α_CFRP ≈ 1·10⁻⁶ (axial)
  • E_steel = 200 GPa, E_aluminum = 70 GPa
  • Steel σ_yield typical ≈ 250–500 MPa; ΔT = 100 °C constrained → 240 MPa stress in steel.
  • Bolt preload loss to embedding: 5–15 % in first hours.
  • For composites: stress in stiff phase ≈ (E_stiff / E_avg) × σ_avg.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Forgetting the sign in compatibility. Bolt stretches positive, sleeve compresses negative; δ_b + δ_s = δ_0 means add magnitudes since both directions close the gap.
  • Treating thermal as a load instead of a strain. ΔT does not add a force directly; it adds a free expansion. The reaction force only exists if displacement is constrained.
  • Using nominal area for stressed bolt. Bolt tensile area (A_t, root area) is smaller than nominal — use it for stress calcs.
  • Ignoring sleeve/washer creep in long-term joints. Plastics, gaskets, and even soft aluminum creep at constant load — preload decays. Use spring washers or Belleville stacks for long-term constancy.
  • Composite load-split with mismatched lengths. Equal-strain compatibility only holds if both members have the same gauge length. For nested sleeves, the rule is by stiffness ratio, not length ratio.
