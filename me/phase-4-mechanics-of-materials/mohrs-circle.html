Mohr's Circle

Stress isn't a scalar — it's a tensor whose value depends on the orientation of the plane you measure. Mohr's circle is the geometric trick that lets you rotate a stress state by hand. The transformation equations (and why a circle)

If you rotate the x-y axes by θ to new axes x'-y', the stress components transform as:

σ_x' = (σ_x + σ_y)/2 + (σ_x − σ_y)/2 · cos 2θ + τ_xy · sin 2θ σ_y' = (σ_x + σ_y)/2 − (σ_x − σ_y)/2 · cos 2θ − τ_xy · sin 2θ τ_x'y' = −(σ_x − σ_y)/2 · sin 2θ + τ_xy · cos 2θ

Let σ_c = (σ_x + σ_y)/2 (the center) and let a = (σ_x − σ_y)/2 (half-difference). Then σ_x' − σ_c and τ_x'y' parametrize as:

σ_x' − σ_c = a · cos 2θ + τ · sin 2θ τ_x'y' = −a · sin 2θ + τ · cos 2θ Square and add: (σ_x' − σ_c)² + (τ_x'y')² = a² + τ² = R² → circle of radius R centered at (σ_c, 0) in (σ, τ) space. Each plane orientation θ corresponds to one point on the circle. Rotate the element by θ in physical space → walk around the circle by 2θ in Mohr space.

Reading the circle

  • Principal stresses σ₁, σ₂ — the two points where the circle crosses the σ axis (τ = 0). σ₁ on the right is the maximum normal stress; σ₂ on the left is the minimum.
  • Maximum shear — the top and bottom of the circle (τ = ±R). The two planes of max shear are 45° rotated from the principal planes (which makes geometric sense: max shear = 45° from max tension in pure tension).
  • Sign convention for τ — most textbooks plot τ_xy positive downward on the Mohr plane (or, equivalently, negate τ). This makes rotating the physical element CCW correspond to walking CCW on the circle. We use the "downward positive" convention; if your textbook does it the other way, the principal angle direction just flips sign.
  • Pure shear case — σ_x = σ_y = 0, τ ≠ 0: circle is centered at origin. Principal stresses are ±τ. A bar in pure torsion has 45° spirals of tension/compression (why brittle shafts crack at 45°).
  • Uniaxial tension — σ_x > 0, σ_y = 0, τ = 0: circle goes from 0 to σ_x. τ_max = σ_x/2 at 45° (why ductile rods fail in shear bands at 45° in tension).

Worked example — pressure vessel + axial load

The stress state

Thin-walled cylinder (r = 100 mm, t = 5 mm) under internal pressure 2 MPa. Additional axial load adds 30 MPa tension. Wall element experiences:

σ_hoop = pr/t = 2 · 100 / 5 = 40 MPa σ_long = pr/(2t) + 30 = 20 + 30 = 50 MPa (axial sum) τ_xy = 0 (no torque on the vessel)

Mohr's circle directly

σ_avg = (40 + 50) / 2 = 45 MPa (center) R = |(40 − 50) / 2| = 5 MPa (half-difference, τ = 0) σ₁ = 50 MPa, σ₂ = 40 MPa (longitudinal and hoop, already principal) τ_max,in-plane = 5 MPa (small — vessel is benign in shear)

3D consideration — out-of-plane

σ_radial ≈ 0 (free surface) → σ₃ = 0 τ_max,3D = (σ₁ − σ₃)/2 = 25 MPa (this is what fails ductile materials)

Lesson: in-plane Mohr's circle gave 5 MPa max shear, but the true max shear is 25 MPa because the radial direction is at zero. Always check the 3D max shear when one principal stress is zero.

SHORTCUTS

  • Physical rotation θ = Mohr rotation 2θ
  • Principal planes = 0 shear on faces
  • Max-shear planes = 45° from principals
  • Brittle fails on σ₁: tension perpendicular to crack
  • Ductile fails on τ_max: slip at 45° from tension
  • 3D check when any σ = 0

PITFALLS

  • Sign of τ in plot — different conventions; pick one and stick with it.
  • Forgetting 2θ — physical 30° rotation = 60° around Mohr circle.
  • Skipping 3D — in-plane τ_max isn't 3D τ_max if σ_z ≠ σ₁ or σ₂.
  • Plane-stress vs plane-strain — different boundary conditions; doesn't change the circle but changes σ_z.
  • Mohr for strain — same circle, different axes (ε_x, γ/2); divide γ by 2 to plot.
