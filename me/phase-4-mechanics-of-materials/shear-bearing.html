Shear, Bearing, and Pressure-Vessel Stresses

A pin in a clevis carries its load by shearing across one or two planes. A pin bearing against the side of its hole develops a contact stress. A thin-walled cylinder under internal pressure feels biaxial stress, with the hoop direction taking twice as much as the longitudinal. Three classic σ = F/(some area) problems where picking the right area is the whole game.

Double shear (clevis)

Bearing stress on the hole

Where the pin presses against the inside of its hole, the contact area is the projected rectangle t × d (NOT the curved surface). Bearing stress σ_b = F/(t·d) crushes both the pin and the plate locally — usually plate is the weaker side.

Thin-walled pressure vessel

A pressurized cylinder (gas tank, hydraulic accumulator, propane bottle) is in a biaxial stress state. Cut around the circumference: balance gives axial σ_l = pr/(2t). Cut along the axis: balance gives hoop σ_h = pr/t. Hoop is twice axial — that's why pressure vessels fail by cracking lengthwise.

What to notice

  • Single vs. double shear: for the same load F, double shear cuts the shear stress in half because the load splits between two planes. τ_single = F/A, τ_double = F/(2A).
  • The shear plane is perpendicular to the load. It's the cross-section of the pin that the two plates would slide along if the pin failed. Visualize: hold a pencil in one hand and a sleeve over it in the other — pull them apart; the failure surface is where the sleeve slides off the pencil.
  • Bearing area is projected, not curved. The contact is over a half-cylinder, but the *projected* area t·d is what carries the load in the standard formula. Distributing F over the full curved area would underestimate the local crushing.
  • Hoop = 2× longitudinal. Two free-body cuts give it. (1) Slice the cylinder along its axis (long-seam cut): the projected pressure area 2r·L is balanced by hoop stress on two thin strips of area t·L each → σ_h = pr/t. (2) Slice across the axis (end-cap cut): the pressure on πr² is balanced by longitudinal stress on the ring of area 2πr·t → σ_l = pr/(2t). Take the ratio: σ_h / σ_l = (pr/t) / (pr/2t) = 2 exactly, independent of p, r, t — that's why pressure vessels split along their length, perpendicular to the higher hoop stress.
  • Thin-wall assumption: r/t ≥ 10. Below that, the through-thickness stress gradient matters and you need thick-cylinder (Lamé) equations. A 50 mm radius vessel needs t ≤ 5 mm for the formula to hold.
  • Bigger pin or thicker plate help shear and bearing differently. Doubling pin diameter quarters shear stress (A scales with d²) but only halves bearing stress (area scales with d). Thicker plate doesn't affect shear at all but halves bearing.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

shear
τ = V / A
bearing
σ_b = F / (t · d)(projected area)
hoop
σ_h = p · r / t
axial (vessel)
σ_l = p · r / (2t)

MEMORIZE

  • Double shear halves τ — two planes share V.
  • Bearing area = t · d — pin diameter × plate thickness (projected).
  • Hoop = 2× axial in a thin-walled cylinder.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Vessels fail along length — hoop is the bigger stress, splits open lengthwise.
  • Pin double-shear needs symmetric loading — asymmetric clevis loads one plane harder.
