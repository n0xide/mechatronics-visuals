Bend Allowance & K-Factor

Bend a flat sheet and three things happen simultaneously: the outside stretches, the inside compresses, and somewhere between them sits a neutral fiber that neither stretches nor compresses. The K-factor is the position of that neutral fiber as a fraction of thickness. Pick a material/thickness; the bend-allowance formula tells you exactly how much flat material the bend consumes.

Where the K-factor comes from

The K-factor parametrizes a real, measurable thing: how close to the inside surface the neutral fiber sits during a bend.

1. In pure bending, the inside compresses (negative strain) and the outside stretches (positive strain). At one fiber across the thickness, strain = 0 → no stretch, no compress. 2. If the material were perfectly elastic, the neutral fiber sits dead center (K = 0.5). It would also have to be exactly at the centerline before AND after bending. 3. Real metal bending is past yield — much of the bend is plastic deformation. Material thins on the outside (more than it thickens on the inside) → neutral fiber shifts toward the inside. 4. Tighter R / t → more thinning on the outside → K is lower. 5. Softer / more formable material → less thinning → K closer to 0.5. 6. Air-bending: K ~ 0.45. Bottom bending / coining: K ~ 0.33–0.40 (more squeeze). 7. → Always determine K by bending a sample, measuring the result, and back-calculating. Tables are starting points only.

Bend allowance formula — the arc length of the neutral fiber

The neutral fiber sits at radius (R + K·t) from the bend center. For bend angle α (degrees), the arc length is BA = (π/180) · (R + K·t) · α. That's exactly how much linear flat material gets "consumed" by the bend (because the neutral fiber neither stretched nor compressed — it has the same length flat and bent). Flat blank length: L_flat = L₁ + L₂ + BA. Where L₁, L₂ are the flat legs measured from the start/end of the bend arc (not from the corner). Alternative: bend deduction BD = 2(R+t) tan(α/2) − BA. Used when dimensions are taken to the mold-line corner: L_flat = L1_outside + L2_outside − BD. Both methods give the same blank. BA-method is more natural for CAM; BD-method matches drawing dimensions.

Worked example — sheet-metal Pico bracket

The bracket

Mount a Pico 2 W to a wall with a sheet-metal L-bracket. CRS (cold-rolled steel), 1.5 mm thick. Inside bend radius 1.5 mm. Bend angle 90°. Total mold-line dimensions: 40 mm (vertical) × 30 mm (horizontal).

K-factor

Mild steel air-bend at R/t = 1.0 → K ≈ 0.42 (interpolated)

Bend allowance

BA = (π/180) × (R + K·t) × α = (π/180) × (1.5 + 0.42·1.5) × 90 = 0.01745 × 2.13 × 90 = 3.346 mm

Bend deduction

BD = 2(R+t) tan(α/2) − BA = 2 × 3.0 × tan(45°) − 3.346 = 6.0 − 3.346 = 2.654 mm

Flat blank

Mold-line: 40 + 30 = 70 mm; subtract BD: 70 − 2.654 = 67.35 mm flat Naive cross-check (subtract only R, not R+t): 38.5 + 28.5 + 3.35 = 70.35 mm — 3.0 mm over. Reason: BA-method uses tangent-line legs, not mold-line; subtract (R+t)·tan(α/2) from each mold-line dimension. At α=90° this offset is just (R+t). Tangent legs: 40 − 3.0 = 37.0 and 30 − 3.0 = 27.0; flat = 37.0 + 27.0 + 3.346 = 67.35 mm ✓

Tool selection

Use a press-brake punch with 1.5 mm tip radius for the R = 1.5 mm bend. Die V-opening should be 6–8× material thickness for air-bending → 8 mm minimum. The minimum flat-to-bend distance from the die is ~ V/2 = 4 mm; ensure your bracket geometry leaves enough flat to grip.

What to notice

  • Sharp inside radius cracks — if R < t, the outside stretches past UTS and you get tearing. Minimum bend radius for ductile material ≈ t; for harder material (spring steel, full-hard CRS), 2–4t.
  • Springback — sheet metal partially elastic returns when released. You over-bend by 1–5° to compensate. Spring steel can spring back 5° at 90° nominal — hardest to control.
  • Bending across the grain is weaker — for severe bends, orient the bend line perpendicular to the rolling direction. Cracking is more common parallel to grain.
  • Bottoming and coining shift K — air-bending: K = 0.40–0.45; bottoming (touches die floor): K = 0.38; coining (high force, pressed flat): K = 0.33. Coining gives the most repeatable result but eats tooling.
  • Tangent-line vs mold-line dimensioning — the two methods (BA vs BD) come from different drawing conventions. BA-method needs flat legs out to the start of the bend arc; BD-method works with overall L-shape outside dimensions. Pick the one your drawing uses.
  • Empirical K beats catalog K — bend a sample, measure the actual flat blank required, back-calculate K. Use that K for the rest of the run. Catalog values are ±0.05 from reality; that's ±0.1 mm on the flat for a typical bend.

K-FACTOR QUICK TABLE

  • Soft Al / Cu: 0.45–0.50
  • Al 6061: 0.40 air, 0.38 bottom
  • Mild steel CRS: 0.45 air
  • Stainless 304: 0.43
  • Spring steel: 0.33 air
  • Bottom-bend: subtract 0.02 from air-bend K
  • Coined: subtract 0.07–0.10 from air-bend K

PITFALLS

  • Wrong tangent vs mold-line — measure flat legs to start of arc, not to corner.
  • Catalog K ≠ shop K — bend a sample and back-calc.
  • R < t → cracking. Use bigger die or different material.
  • No springback compensation → 90° drawing nets 87°.
  • Bending into a feature — leave ≥ V/2 + 2t from any cutout edge to the bend line.
  • Grain direction — cracks form parallel to rolling for tight bends.
