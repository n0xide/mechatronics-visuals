Hand tools — tapping & finishing

Cutting a thread into a hole by hand is mostly about: (1) pre-drilling to the right tap-drill size, (2) keeping the tap square to the surface, and (3) breaking the chip every quarter-turn before it binds. The wrong drill size or technique snaps small taps — and broken M3 taps are notoriously hard to extract.

Rule of thumb: D_drill ≈ D_major − pitch gives ~75% thread engagement, the standard for most aluminum and steel applications. 50% engagement makes tapping easy but threads strip earlier; 100% gives the strongest threads but breaks taps in hard materials.

Tapping technique: ¼ turn forward, ⅛ turn back

The tap cuts on the forward stroke; the back stroke breaks the chip free. Skip the back stroke and the chip grows until it wedges the tap — at which point an M3 tap snaps off in your hole and ruins the part. The reversal is especially critical on small (≤M4) taps where torque tolerance is tiny.

What to notice

  • D − p is the only formula you need. Tap drill size ≈ major diameter minus pitch for 75% engagement. M3 × 0.5 → 2.5 mm drill. M6 × 1.0 → 5.0 mm drill.
  • 75% engagement is the standard. Going to 100% (slightly smaller drill) snaps small taps in steel; going to 50% (slightly larger drill) makes tapping easy but threads strip earlier.
  • Back-stroke is non-negotiable on M4 and smaller. The chip is brittle and short; without breaking it every ¼ turn, it wraps around the flutes and binds the tap. M3 in aluminum can snap with just 0.5 N·m of binding torque.
  • Aluminum loves WD-40 for tapping. Don't use water-based coolant — it galls and re-welds chip to tap. Stainless needs heavy sulfurised oil; brass needs nothing.
  • Deburr before tapping. A burr at the hole entrance tilts the tap on first engagement, making the thread cross-threaded for its entire length.
