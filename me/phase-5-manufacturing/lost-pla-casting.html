Lost-PLA Casting

FDM print a pattern in PLA. Encase it in plaster. Bake the plaster: the PLA boils out, leaving a hollow mold of your exact shape. Pour molten metal in. Break the plaster. One-off bronze/brass/aluminum parts in days, not weeks — no machining, no green-sand mold-making, no expensive billet. The desktop maker's metal casting.

Stage 1 — Pattern (FDM print)

The PLA print becomes the negative of your mold. Every defect, every layer line, every overhang artifact is replicated in the cast metal. Treat the print as the final part's surface: print fine layers (0.10–0.16 mm), high infill or solid walls in the visible surfaces, and orient features for the best surface finish.

Print parameterSettingWhy
Layer height0.10–0.16 mmTrade-off: tighter layers = better surface, longer prints.
Infill10–25 %, gyroid or honeycombLower infill = less PLA to vaporize = cleaner burnout.
Walls2–3 perimetersNeed a continuous skin so the plaster doesn't push through during investing.
MaterialPLA (not PETG, not ABS)PLA decomposes cleanly at ~350 °C with minimal residue. PETG leaves char; ABS leaves carbon and acrid smoke.
Sprue + ventsAdd at the model levelPrint the sprue as part of the pattern — metal will follow the print's geometry.
Draft angle1–3° on visible facesHelps the plaster release the molten PLA evenly. Still applies even for one-shot molds!
Draft angle still matters: even for a one-shot lost-PLA mold, slight draft on vertical faces helps the burnout gases vent and the metal flow during pouring. Without draft, surface tension can trap gas pockets against the mold wall — cosmetic defects (gas porosity) at the surface.

Stage 2 — Invest in plaster

"Investment" = pour a plaster slurry around the pattern and let it set. The plaster will become the mold; it has to be strong enough to survive the burnout temperature without cracking, and refractory enough to hold the molten metal without softening.

Plaster + silica sand50/50 by volume, water to slurry~900 °CDIY mix. Sand adds refractoriness; plaster alone cracks.
Plaster + grog50/50 plaster + crushed firebrick~1100 °CFor higher-temperature pours (bronze, silver).
Sodium silicate sandSand + waterglass, CO₂ cure~1300 °CIndustrial process. Excellent for iron/steel but trickier setup.

Mixing: water then plaster (or per manufacturer's recipe). Mix to a heavy-cream consistency; vacuum-degas (60 seconds, 28 inHg if available) to remove air bubbles that would otherwise leave pits on the casting. Vibrate or tap the flask to help the slurry penetrate undercut features. Set time depends on plaster — usually 30 minutes to an hour before handling, 24 hours before burnout.

Stage 3 — Burnout in a kiln

The plaster mold goes into a kiln, sprue-end down (so the melting PLA flows out and air can enter). The temperature is ramped slowly to drive off PLA, water of hydration, and any volatiles. Going too fast cracks the mold from steam pressure.

PhaseTemperatureHold timeWhat's happening
1. DryRT → 150 °C1–2 h rampFree water boils off. Steam pressure cracks the mold if you go fast.
2. Soften150 → 200 °C30 min ramp + 30 min holdPLA softens, glass transition (T_g ≈ 60 °C) long passed, starts to flow out.
3. Melt+drain200 → 350 °C1 h holdPLA melts and drains through the sprue. Most of the polymer leaves now.
4. Burn off350 → 600 °C1–2 h holdResidual PLA decomposes; carbon burns to CO₂ in oxygen-rich air. Critical for clean cavity.
5. Heat-soak600 → 700–750 °C1–2 h holdDrive off chemically bound water; bring mold to pour temperature so metal doesn't freeze on contact.
6. Cool to pourdown to pour temp − ΔT30–60 minMold should be just below metal's pour temp to minimize thermal shock.

Total burnout cycle: 8–14 hours for a 1–2 kg flask. Skipping the slow ramp causes mold cracks; skipping the high-temperature soak leaves carbon in the cavity that creates surface defects on the casting.

Stage 4 — Pour molten metal

Melt the metal in a crucible (graphite, silicon carbide, or clay-graphite). Skim slag/dross from the surface. Bring the mold up from burnout to its pour temperature, then pour steadily and continuously down the sprue — pausing or thin streams cause cold-shut defects where two metal fronts meet but don't fuse.

Stage 5 — Break out and finish

After cooling (mold quenches in water once metal solidifies — for safer handling, or air-cool overnight for delicate parts), break the plaster mold off with a hammer and chisel. The casting comes out with the sprue still attached and possibly some sand/plaster bits stuck to it.

  • Cut off sprue with a hacksaw or bandsaw. The sprue stub on the casting is removed by filing or grinding.
  • Sandblast to remove residual plaster and any oxide skin. Glass beads, aluminum oxide grit.
  • File / grind the parting line, sprue stub, and any flash.
  • Machine features that need tight tolerance (bearing bores, threaded holes). Lost-PLA gives ±0.3 mm at best; tighter than that requires post-machining.
  • Polish or patina as desired.

Worked example — knuckle for the arm-6dof prototype, bronze

The setup

Single-piece bronze knuckle for a 6-DOF arm prototype: ~60 mm × 40 mm × 30 mm, two bearing bores, lugs at 90°. Direct-machining from billet bronze is expensive and slow. FDM-prototype + lost-PLA gives a usable first-article in days.

The numbers

Pattern volume: ~25 cm³ 25 cm³ × 1.24 g/cm³ = ~31 g if solid; ~6–8 g at 20 % gyroid infill (what you actually print) Plaster volume: ~250 cm³ (10× pattern + sprue, includes flask) Bronze required: 25 cm³ × 8.8 g/cm³ = 220 g + 30 g for sprue/runner waste = ~250 g Burnout cycle: 11 hours total, soak at 700 °C for 2 hours Pour temperature: 1100 °C, mold at 650 °C Cool to RT before breakout: 4 hours Finishing: 30 minutes (sprue removal + sandblast); bearing bores reamed to H7 after

What changes from the FDM original

The cast knuckle is ~1.3–1.5 % smaller than the print in all dimensions (solidification shrinkage of tin bronze). Layer lines are visible on the casting unless the print was vapor-smoothed (PLA cannot be vapor-smoothed; print in ABS-equivalent → lost-PLA equivalent process for that, or sand the pattern). Bearing bore tolerance is the dimensional weakness — always print the bore +0.5 mm undersize, then ream the cast bore to spec.

What to notice

  • Lost-PLA is a one-shot mold. Each pour destroys the plaster (broken to release the casting) and the pattern (vaporized during burnout). For quantities > ~5 of the same part, switch to a reusable mold (silicone for cosmetic resin, plaster/sand for metal).
  • PLA, specifically — not PETG, not ABS. PLA depolymerizes cleanly at ~350 °C with most product being CO₂ and water. PETG leaves char that fouls the cavity surface. ABS produces toxic styrene + acrid carbon.
  • The plaster mold is the slow step. Mixing, setting, drying, ramping to burnout temperature takes 24 hours minimum. The pour itself is 30 seconds. Cycle time is dominated by the kiln.
  • Cold-shut defects are the most common surface failure. Metal flow front cools before reaching the far end of the cavity → two fronts meet but don't fuse → visible seam. Fix by raising pour temperature, raising mold temperature, or shortening the path.
  • Gas porosity from incomplete burnout. Residual PLA leaves carbon; on pour, carbon oxidizes in the hot metal and produces gas pockets. Symptom: tiny bubbles or "swiss cheese" near the surface. Cure: longer high-temperature soak.
  • Solidification shrinkage is the pattern-maker's allowance: ~1.3–1.5 % for tin bronze, ~1.3 % for aluminum, ~1 % for grey cast iron, ~2 % for cast steel. The classic shop rule is the shrink rule per foot: 1/8" (1.0 %) for grey iron, 5/32" (1.3 %) for aluminum and bronze, 1/4" (2.0 %) for steel. Print the pattern slightly oversize if final-dimension tolerance matters. Most cast features get a finishing pass anyway.
  • Sprue design matters. Sprue large enough to deliver metal fast (no cooling on the way in), runners that reach the far points of the cavity, and risers at the highest points to feed shrinkage. The sprue is part of the PLA print and follows the same investment + burnout path.

Where you'll see this

Real uses

  • Robot arm knuckles & mounts — bronze or aluminum prototypes for SO-ARM100 / PA612 builds before committing to machined billet.
  • Custom electrical busbar terminals — brass terminals for high-current battery packs; cast and then drilled for tap.
  • Cyberdeck handles & bezels — aluminum or bronze accents on portable computer projects.
  • Jewelry & small-batch art bronze — the home of the technique. Wax patterns and PLA patterns both work, but PLA-via-FDM is faster than wax.
  • Replacement parts for vintage equipment — broken cast-iron lever from a treadle sewing machine, broken brass knob from an antique radio. CAD the replacement, FDM it, cast it.
  • Resin/PLA hybrid casts — resin printers (DLP/SLA) produce smoother patterns. "Castable resin" exists specifically for lost-resin casting; same five stages.

Cheat sheet

NUMBERS

  • PLA: 1.24 g/cm³, decomposes ~350 °C, T_g ≈ 60 °C
  • Bronze: 8.8 g/cm³, pour ~1100 °C, mold ~650 °C
  • Aluminum: 2.7 g/cm³, pour ~740 °C, mold ~350 °C
  • Brass: 8.5 g/cm³, pour ~970 °C, mold ~550 °C
  • Burnout cycle: 8–14 h, soak ≥ 2 h at peak
  • Solidification shrink (pattern oversize): bronze ~1.5 %; Al ~1.3 %; grey iron ~1.0 %; steel ~2.0 %

RECIPE

  • 1. Print pattern in PLA, sprue + vents included, light infill.
  • 2. Invest in plaster slurry, vacuum-degas, vibrate, set 24 h.
  • 3. Burnout: ramp slow, soak ≥ 2 h at 600–700 °C.
  • 4. Pour: metal at pour temp, mold at burnout temp, steady continuous stream.
  • 5. Break out, sprue off, sandblast, finish.
  • Reusable jewelry flasks (perforated stainless rings) make hands-on easier.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Skipping the burnout soak → carbon residue → gas porosity at surface.
  • Ramping too fast in the dry phase → steam pressure cracks the mold.
  • Pouring too cold → cold-shut seams where the flow front froze before meeting.
  • No sprue/vents in the pattern → metal can't displace the air, casting won't fill.
  • Using PETG or ABS instead of PLA → toxic fumes + carbon residue in the cavity.
  • Plaster too thin around the pattern → mold cracks under metallostatic pressure during pour.
  • Mold cold at pour → metal freezes on contact, surface defects, no fill of thin walls.
