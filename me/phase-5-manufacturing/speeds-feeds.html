Speeds & Feeds

Two numbers run every milling and turning operation: RPM (how fast the tool spins) and IPM/MMPM (how fast it moves through the material). They derive from surface speed (SFM, the cutting speed at the tool edge) and chip load (how much each flute bites). Pick a material/tool combo; sliders set diameter and depth; the calculator gives the four numbers you punch into the controller.

Why the formula has π in it

SFM (Surface Feet per Minute) is the speed of the cutting edge as it moves across the workpiece — a tangential velocity. RPM × (tool circumference) = tangential velocity.

1. Circumference = π · D (units of length) 2. Surface speed Vc = π · D · n where n is revolutions per unit time. 3. In imperial: Vc [ft/min] = π · D [in] / 12 · n [RPM] 4. Solve for n: n = Vc · 12 / (π · D) 5. In metric: n [RPM] = Vc [m/min] · 1000 / (π · D [mm]) 6. Feed is independent: chip load is the per-tooth bite (mm/tooth). Each rev, each tooth bites once, so feed = cl · n_flutes · RPM. 7. → Two formulas, two settings on the machine. Both must be right for the chip to be the right size.

What chip load actually does

Chip load is the most important number after SFM, but the least talked about by hobbyists. It governs:

  • Tool life — too low (< 0.01 mm/tooth) and the tool rubs instead of cutting. Heat builds in the edge, not in the chip; edge work-hardens; tool dulls fast. Counter-intuitive: too slow a feed kills tools faster than too fast.
  • Chip evacuation — chips carry heat away. Bigger chips = more cooling per cut. Tiny chips just powder and stick to the tool (built-up edge in aluminum).
  • Surface finish — too high a chip load leaves scallop marks; too low leaves rub marks. Sweet spot is material-specific (0.02–0.10 mm/tooth typical for end-mills).
  • Cutting force — F ∝ chip area (cl × DOC). Doubling chip load doubles force; finally the tool deflects or chatters.
  • Heat partition — at correct chip load, ~80% of heat leaves with the chip. At low chip load, 20% chip / 80% tool — recipe for instant failure.

Worked example — slotting 6 mm pocket in aluminum with carbide

The setup

Material: Al 6061, SFM 1000 typical with carbide Tool: 6 mm 2-flute carbide end-mill Operation: slotting (full-diameter engagement) DOC (axial depth): 3 mm (50% of D, conservative)

Compute

Vc = 1000 SFM × 0.3048 = 305 m/min RPM = 305 × 1000 / (π × 6) = 16,180 RPM (or your spindle's max, whichever is lower) Spindle limit: most desktop CNCs cap at 12,000 RPM → use 12,000 Chip load: 0.05 mm/tooth (Al + 2-flute carbide table) Feed = 0.05 × 2 × 12,000 = 1,200 mm/min MRR = feed × DOC × WOC = 1200 × 3 × 6 = 21,600 mm³/min = 21.6 cm³/min

Power check

Specific cutting energy Al ≈ 0.7 W/(mm³/min) Power = 21,600 × 0.7 / 1000 = ~15 W of cutting power Plus friction, idle, motor inefficiency → ~150–300 W draw → fine for a 0.5 kW spindle

If it chatters

Drop DOC first (it has the biggest moment arm). Then drop RPM by 20% to shift away from resonance. Keep feed proportional (so chip load stays the same) — never drop feed without dropping RPM.

What to notice

  • Smaller tool = higher RPM, lower feed — RPM scales as 1/D. A 1 mm endmill at the same SFM needs 6× the RPM of a 6 mm one. Many spindle motors can't go that fast.
  • More flutes = more chips per rev, more feed — a 4-flute at 12,000 RPM and 0.025 mm/tooth gives the same feed as a 2-flute at 0.05. The 4-flute leaves better finish; the 2-flute clears chips better (less rechip-cut).
  • Aluminum wants 2 flutes — chips are big and gummy; flutes need room. Stainless wants 4+ to spread the cut load thin.
  • Carbide is brittle — interrupted cuts (e.g., re-entry into a pocket) shock-load the edge. Carbide chips out if the cut isn't continuous; HSS plows through.
  • Adaptive ("trochoidal") clearing uses small WOC (radial bite, 5-15% of D) and full DOC. Same MRR but cooler tool; doubles tool life. Worth learning.
  • Always check spindle limit — a router spindle may run 24,000 RPM; a Tormach mill maxes at 10,000; a Bridgeport tops at 4,200. The math gives a target; the machine sets the ceiling.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

RPM
n = Vc × 1000 / (π × D)
RPM (imperial)
n = SFM × 12 / (π × D)
Feed
F = cl × flutes × n
MRR
F × DOC × WOC
Chip thinning
cl_actual = cl × WOC/D (radial)
SFM ↔ m/min
m/min = SFM × 0.3048

SHORTCUTS

  • Carbide ≈ 4× HSS SFM
  • Adaptive: DOC = D, WOC = 0.1D
  • Slot: DOC = 0.5–1.0 × D
  • Profile: DOC = 1–2 × D, WOC = 0.5D
  • Drill: feed = 0.5× endmill feed
  • Climb mill for finish (chip thick → thin)

PITFALLS

  • Feed too low — rubs, work-hardens, dulls tool fast. Counter to intuition.
  • Forgetting chip thinning — at WOC < 50% of D, actual chip load is less than nominal; compensate or it's effectively a rub.
  • Mixing units — SFM (ft/min) ≠ m/min ≠ mm/s. Always convert before plugging into RPM.
  • Climb vs conventional — climb (downcut) is preferred for finish but eats backlash; needs rigid machine.
  • Spindle stalls — exceeding spindle torque limit cuts deeper than it can sustain; chip welds, snap.
  • No coolant on Ti — actually wrong: Ti needs flood coolant; smear-built-up edge is the failure mode without.
