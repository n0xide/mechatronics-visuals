Metric thread system — anatomy & engagement

A metric thread is geometry, not magic: M[D]×[P] says "major diameter D, pitch P, 60° flank." Engagement length is what holds load — too short and the threads strip before the bolt yields. What to notice

  • Pitch shrinks faster than diameter, then plateaus. M2×0.4 is 20% pitch/diameter; M10×1.5 is 15%. Smaller threads have proportionally larger root features.
  • 60° flank is the universal metric profile (ISO 68). The angle is fixed across all metric sizes — only D and P scale. Inch UTS is also 60°; old Whitworth was 55°.
  • Engagement rule is substrate-driven, not bolt-driven. Steel-on-steel is 1×D because the threads in both halves are equally strong. Aluminum needs 1.5×D because aluminum shears at ~half steel's strength. Plastic-direct (2×D) usually still fails because the boss splits in hoop, not the threads.
  • 6g/6H is the "store-shelf" tolerance. 6g screw + 6H nut gives small clearance and easy assembly. 4g/4H is precision (instrument-grade) and harder to make. 8g/7H is loose, for galvanized or contaminated threads.
  • Coarse pitch is the silent default. When you write M4 with no pitch, you mean M4×0.7. If you need fine-pitch (M4×0.5), you MUST write it — the screw won't engage a coarse nut.
  • The "stress area" is >π·d1²/4. It's averaged between the minor and the pitch diameter (a few percent above the pure-minor cross-section) — what you use in F = σ·As for the tensile capacity of the bolt.

DECISION — ENGAGEMENT

  • 1.0×D — steel into steel
  • 1.5×D — steel into aluminum
  • 2.0×D — steel into plastic (no insert)

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Callout is M[D]×[P] — coarse is default, omit P.
  • Fine pitch must be explicit (e.g. M4×0.5) or you'll buy the wrong nut.
  • Coarse and fine don't mate — never mix in a stack.
