Vibration Loosening

Threaded joints don't loosen by axial vibration — they loosen by transverse vibration. Once the joint slips sideways under the head, the friction grip releases, the thread helix unwinds, and the preload bleeds off. The Junker test (1969) showed that even small transverse motions can completely unload a properly torqued bolt in seconds. Threadlockers, lockwashers, and prevailing-torque nuts are all bets on different mechanisms to stop the slip.

The Junker test (DIN 65151)

Gerhard Junker showed in 1969 that the classic engineering belief — "tight bolts don't loosen" — was wrong. His test fixture applies a controlled transverse displacement between two plates while a bolt is preloaded and monitored. Plain bolts lose 100 % of preload in fewer than 100 cycles at modest amplitude. The same bolt subjected to axial vibration (perpendicular to the joint, parallel to the bolt axis) lost essentially nothing.

1. Friction at the head and under the nut holds the bolt against the screw-out torque generated by the thread helix. 2. Transverse motion of the joint plates → relative slip between the bolt head/nut and its bearing surface → friction force drops to kinetic friction (lower than static). 3. Loss of friction grip → the helix's screw-out torque (always present under preload) is now free to rotate the bolt. 4. Each cycle of transverse slip nudges the rotation a tiny angle. After hundreds-to-thousands of cycles, accumulated rotation = full unwinding. Preload → 0.

Why transverse loosens but axial doesn't

The net screw-out torque on a preloaded bolt is approximately T_o ≈ F · (p/2π − μ_t·r/cos(α/2)) (V-thread; α = 60° flank gives 1/cos(30°) ≈ 1.155) for pitch p, mean thread radius r, and thread friction μ_t. For typical metric-coarse hardware the friction term wins (μ_t·r/cos(α/2) > p/(2π)) so the thread is self-locking under static friction — T_o is negative, the bolt won't back out on its own. But the preload still generates an unwinding tangential force F·tan(λ) at the thread helix (λ = lead angle), and that force only stays opposed by friction as long as the head/nut bearing surfaces and the thread interface remain in static contact. Transverse vibration slips the head sideways → friction drops from static to kinetic (lower) → the unwinding tangential force overcomes residual friction → bolt rotates a tiny angle each cycle. Axial vibration cycles the bearing pressure but doesn't slip the head, so static friction is preserved and the helix can't unwind.

Threadlockers — Loctite grades

Anaerobic threadlockers cure in the absence of air when between metal threads. They fill the helical gap with a polymer that resists the screw-out torque. The grade controls cured strength: low for serviceable joints, high for permanent or safety-critical.

243 is the modern default. Oil-tolerant means the formula cures correctly on threads that have residual cutting oil or assembly grease — true for almost any factory part. Use 263 for vibration-critical or safety-critical applications.

Threadlocker also seals threads against fluid and corrosion. Loctite 567/577 are PTFE-loaded thread sealants for pipe threads (NPT, BSPT); they're not strictly anti-loosening but they prevent the corrosion that would seize a joint or leak fluid through it.

Nyloc nuts and prevailing-torque hardware

A Nyloc nut has a polyamide (nylon) ring inserted into one end. As the bolt threads into the ring, the nylon deforms — creating a prevailing torque that resists unscrewing even with zero preload. The first installation cuts threads into the nylon; subsequent installations don't restore the original grip, so Nyloc nuts are single-use.

  • Prevailing torque typically 5–15 N·m for M8 hardware, decreasing with size.
  • Temperature limit ~120 °C for standard nylon; above that the insert melts/creeps and the lock fails.
  • Re-use degrades the lock by ~50 % each install. Always use a new Nyloc.
  • For higher temperatures, use all-metal locknuts (DIN 6927, "stover" lock nut) — the threads are crimped so the bolt is friction-locked at the deformed section. Reusable to a limit.

Worked example — propeller-shaft flange on the arm-6dof prototype

The setup

Six M6 bolts hold a flange to the output shaft of an arm joint. The shaft cyclically reverses direction during pick-and-place — this is transverse loading at the bolt heads (the flange shears against the shaft face each cycle). Frequency ~2 Hz, amplitude ~0.1 mm at the bolt circle. Service life target: 10⁷ cycles (~50,000 hours = ~5 years of operation).

Choose the protection

Plain bolt + flat washer → fails in < 10³ cycles. Unusable. Split lockwasher → marginal improvement. Still fails by 10⁵ cycles. Unusable. Tooth lockwasher → fails ~10⁶ cycles. Order-of-magnitude short. Loctite 243 blue → survives 10⁷ cycles in Junker test data. Choose this. Loctite 263 red → safer (more vibration margin) but needs heat to remove for service. Reserve for safety-critical. Nord-Lock washer pair → also passes but more expensive (~3× per joint). Reserve for joints with no chemical-cleaning access.

Installation procedure

  1. Clean threads with brake cleaner; threadlocker won't bond to oily, painted, or dirty threads.
  2. Apply 1 drop of Loctite 243 to the male thread, 3–4 threads in.
  3. Assemble + torque to spec (~10 N·m dry / K=0.2, or ~7 N·m light-oil / K=0.14, for class-8.8 M6 → 75 % proof preload ≈ 8.7 kN with σ_proof = 580 MPa, A_s = 20.1 mm²).
  4. Wait 24 hours for full cure; light handling load OK after 30 minutes.
  5. Mark each fastener with a witness mark across head/joint so future inspection catches rotation early.

What to notice

  • Transverse, not axial. Memorize this: vibration loosening is a shear phenomenon at the head/nut bearing surface. If the joint isn't slipping sideways under the head, it's not loosening.
  • Preload matters more than locking devices. A properly-torqued bolt to 75 % of proof load is much less likely to loosen than an under-torqued bolt with a fancy washer. Get the preload right first; add locking only if you can't get the preload high enough or the application is critical.
  • Split lockwashers are decorative. Multiple independent studies (NASA, DOE, Bosch) found split washers offer minimal vibration protection. Industry still uses them because they're cheap and tradition is hard to kill. Use 243 instead.
  • Threadlocker needs clean threads. Oil, grease, paint, plating all prevent bonding. Brake cleaner + air-dry before applying. Galvanized or zinc-plated threads need primer (Loctite 7649) for full strength.
  • Nord-Lock is reusable, threadlocker isn't. If the joint will be serviced repeatedly, Nord-Lock washers are cheaper-per-cycle. If once-and-forget, 243/263 is cheaper.
  • Witness marks save inspections. A paint mark from head to joint catches a rotated fastener at a glance. Industrial practice; cheap insurance.
  • Heat is the threadlocker enemy. 243 fails above ~150 °C. For engines, exhausts, or anything hot, use 271/272 (higher heat) or all-metal mechanical locks.

Where you'll see this

Real situations

  • Robotic arm joints — every revolute joint has transverse vibration from end-effector motion. Loctite 243 on every M3+ in the arm-6dof builds.
  • Stepper motor mounts — small amplitude but high cycle count. Mounting bolts on NEMA-17/23 motors should always have 243.
  • PCB standoffs — long-term vibration in a portable device. Nyloc or 222 (purple, very light).
  • 3D printer hot-end + extruder — high vibration, daily cycles. Nyloc on bed mounts, 243 on hot-end mounting screws.
  • Bike crankarm bolts — pedal force is mostly axial, but bearing wobble creates transverse component. Manufacturer torque + 243 or 263.
  • Car wheel lug nuts — designed with conical seat that wedges into the wheel hub; the cone is the anti-loosening mechanism, no threadlocker needed.
  • Aerospace flight-critical hardware — castellated nut + cotter pin or self-locking metal nut. Threadlocker is too unreliable for life-critical.

Cheat sheet

FACTS

  • Loosening is transverse vibration, not axial.
  • Junker test (DIN 65151): typically 12–25 Hz × 0.5–1.0 mm transverse for ~2000 cycles.
  • Plain bolt fails in < 100 cycles.
  • Split lockwasher offers almost no additional vibration resistance.
  • Loctite 243 cures fully in 24 h; light load OK in 30 min.
  • Loctite 243 max service temp ≈ 150 °C.
  • Nyloc nuts: single-use; max temp ~120 °C.

SELECTION TREE

  • Indoor, low vib, serviceable → 222 (purple) or no threadlocker.
  • General industrial, moderate vib → 243 (blue).
  • High vibration, safety-critical → 263 (red) or Nord-Lock.
  • Already-assembled (can't disassemble) → 290 (green, wicking).
  • Hot environment → all-metal locknut or castellated + pin.
  • Re-serviceable many times → Nord-Lock (re-usable).
  • Aerospace / flight-critical → mechanical lock + cotter pin.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Putting 243 on dirty threads — paint, plating, oil, residue prevents cure.
  • Re-using Nyloc nuts — second install grips ~50 % as well; safety-critical fail.
  • Split lockwasher under a soft-coated surface — wears the coating and weakens preload.
  • Threadlocker on plastic / nylon — anaerobic adhesives don't cure on plastics. Use a mechanical lock instead.
  • Tightening through a Nyloc after partial install — friction reading is wrong (prevailing + clamping); torque to spec misses the preload.
  • Stainless on stainless without anti-seize — galling, not loosening, but kills the joint. Use Loctite 567 or copper-based anti-seize.
  • Forgetting witness marks — without them, partial loosening is invisible until catastrophic.
