What to notice

  • Try s + l = p + q exactly. Use a₁ = a₃ and a₂ = a₄ (parallelogram). The coupler stays parallel to the ground and traces a circle — useful for keeping a payload level (pantograph, scissor lifts, drafting lamps).
  • If you can't get a full revolution, you're outside the Grashof region. Many useful mechanisms (wipers, oscillating fans) are intentionally non-Grashof triple-rockers driven through a separate crank slider.
  • Toggle position appears when the crank and coupler become collinear: torque transmission is huge, velocity transmission is zero. Locking pliers and kickstands exploit this.
  • Coupler curve is the famous "magic" of four-bars — by choosing a point on the floating link you can trace approximate straight lines (Watt, Chebyshev), figure eights, eggs, or dwells.
  • Crank-rocker is the workhorse: a motor rotates the short crank continuously, and the rocker oscillates back and forth. Sewing-machine needle bar, beam-pumping oil derrick.

Cheat sheet

GRASHOF RULES

  • s+l ≤ p+q → Grashof (some link rotates fully)
  • Shortest = crank → crank-rocker
  • Shortest = ground → drag-link (double-crank)
  • Shortest = coupler → double-rocker
  • s+l > p+q → triple-rocker (no full rotation)

DECISION POINTS

  • Toggle position: crank + coupler collinear → max mechanical advantage, zero velocity transfer
  • Transmission angle µ between coupler & rocker; keep 40°–140° for smooth force flow
  • Parallelogram (a₁=a₃, a₂=a₄): coupler stays parallel — payload leveler

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Non-Grashof = triple-rocker — no link makes a full revolution; can't drive with a continuous motor on the input
  • Coupler curve highly sensitive to length tolerances — ±1% can shift the path significantly
  • Change-point (s+l = p+q) flips configuration; lock with a guide or branch-resolving link
