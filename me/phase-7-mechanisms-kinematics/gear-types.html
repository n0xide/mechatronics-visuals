Gear Types Side-by-Side

Four gear families do all the work: spur (cheap, noisy, parallel shafts), helical (quiet, parallel shafts, with thrust), bevel (right-angle, no reduction in ratio range), and worm (very high reduction, often self-locking). Each one has a use case the others can't fill cleanly. Pick a gear; see the geometry, the thrust direction, the typical reduction range, and whether it back-drives.

Spur — the workhorse

Two parallel shafts, teeth cut straight (axially). Each tooth engages in line contact along the full face — simple, cheap, but the sudden engage/disengage of each tooth creates impact and noise.

Ratio: i = z_gear / z_pinion. Speed reduces by i; torque increases by i (minus loss). Pitch circle diameters: d = z · m, where m = module (mm/tooth). Center distance: a = (z₁ + z₂) · m / 2. Efficiency 96–99% per stage (very high). Why noise? — tooth-passing frequency × impact at each engage. Use when: cost matters more than noise; precision allows for backlash; loads aren't crushing.

Helical — the quiet one

Same as spur but the teeth are cut at an angle (helix angle β, typically 15–35°). Multiple teeth are always in contact (overlap ratio > 1), so load is shared smoothly. Noise drops 5–15 dB compared to spur.

Tangential force: F_t = T / r (same as spur) Radial (separating) force: F_r = F_t · tan(α_n) / cos(β), where α_n is normal pressure angle (typically 20°). Axial thrust: F_a = F_t · tan(β). → Helical gears generate an axial force on each shaft (direction set by helix hand × rotation × driver-vs-driven). You need thrust bearings on each shaft. Double-helical ("herringbone") gears cancel thrust internally by having two opposing helices on the same gear. Used for big industrial drives. Use when: noise is an issue (transmissions, household appliances); shaft alignment is good; you can support thrust loads.

Bevel — 90° between shafts

Cone-shaped pitch surfaces. Two flavors: straight bevel (teeth radiate from apex, basically a spur on a cone) and spiral bevel (curved teeth like a helical, smoother + quieter).

Shaft angle Σ = 90° is standard; the pitch cone half-angles satisfy γ₁ + γ₂ = Σ. For Σ = 90°: tan(γ₁) = z₁ / z₂, tan(γ₂) = z₂ / z₁. Differential in cars uses bevels; rear-axle reduction is hypoid (offset-axis spiral bevel) for ground-clearance reasons. Use when: shafts must cross at 90°; reduction not too extreme (single stage usually ≤ 5:1).

Worm — extreme reduction, often self-locking

A worm is essentially a screw thread; the wheel is a helical gear with teeth matching the worm's lead. One revolution of the worm advances the wheel by one tooth (single-start) — that's where the huge reductions come from.

Reduction: i = z_wheel / z_starts. With z_wheel = 60 and single-start, i = 60:1 in one stage. Lead angle λ on the worm: tan(λ) = lead / (π · d_worm) = z_starts · p / (π · d_worm). Self-locking: the worm wheel can not drive the worm if friction prevents back-drive. Threshold: μ > tan(λ). Single-start worm with λ ≈ 3° → μ_static ~ 0.05 (steel/bronze pair, well-lubed) is enough to self-lock. Most single-start worms self-lock. Efficiency drops with low lead angle: η = tan(λ) / tan(λ + φ_f) where φ_f = friction angle. A self-locking worm has η < 50% — by definition. Use when: massive reduction in one stage; need self-locking (lift gates, satellite trackers, hose clamps); willing to accept high losses + heat.

Worked example — robot arm joint actuator

Requirements

Arm joint torque required: 10 N·m Joint speed: 30 deg/s (5 RPM) Motor: small BLDC, 6000 RPM no-load, 0.05 N·m peak Required reduction: 6000 / 5 = 1200:1 motor side, but only ~150:1 needed when motor isn't at no-load → say 100:1

Option A — 3-stage planetary spur

3 stages of 5:1 each: i = 125, η = 0.97³ = 0.91, torque to load: 0.05 × 125 × 0.91 = 5.7 N·m → INSUFFICIENT Need 4 stages of ~3.5:1 → i = 150, η = 0.97⁴ = 0.89, torque = 0.05 × 150 × 0.89 = 6.6 N·m → still short Need bigger motor + harmonic drive (160:1 in one stage, 90% η)

Option B — worm + spur backup

Worm 100:1, η = 0.50 → effective reduction: 0.05 × 100 × 0.50 = 2.5 N·m. INSUFFICIENT. Worm 60:1 + spur 2:1 = 120:1. Worm η = 0.55, spur η = 0.98 → combined 0.54 Output torque = 0.05 × 120 × 0.54 = 3.2 N·m → still insufficient. Worms waste half the energy.

Option C — Cycloidal / harmonic drive

Single-stage harmonic 100:1, η = 0.85 → torque = 0.05 × 100 × 0.85 = 4.25 N·m Bigger motor (0.1 N·m peak) + harmonic 100:1 → 8.5 N·m torque Not self-locking, but the high gear ratio means back-driving needs (i × T_friction) at the output — effectively low back-drivability for arm-payload loads. Brake still required for safety on vertical axes. For robot arms, harmonic is the standard.

Lesson

A worm gear's self-locking property is amazing for safety (arm doesn't fall on power-off), but you pay for it with efficiency. For battery-powered robots, harmonic/cycloidal beats worm despite higher complexity, because energy is precious.

WHEN TO USE WHAT

  • Spur: cheap, parallel shaft, indoor
  • Helical: noise-sensitive, higher load
  • Bevel: 90° crossing, ≤ 5:1
  • Worm: huge ratio, self-locking
  • Planetary: compact 3–10:1 per stage
  • Harmonic: 50–200:1, zero backlash

PITFALLS

  • Spur in robotics — backlash is a kinematic error you can't get rid of. Helical or anti-backlash needed.
  • Helical without thrust bearings — shaft will slide out. Cone-roller or angular-contact bearings.
  • Bevel mesh misalignment — sensitive to angular misalignment. Pre-load with shims.
  • Worm overheat — 50% efficiency means 50% of input is heat. Calculate thermal limit.
  • Worm + high frequency — wear from continuous sliding. Use bronze wheel, hardened-steel worm.
  • Plastic gears — fine for low torque + clean environment; UV and oil swell them.
