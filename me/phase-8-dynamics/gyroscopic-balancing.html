Gyroscopic precession & rotor balancing

Two ideas, same root cause — what spinning mass does in the presence of an unwanted load. Top: a flywheel under applied torque precesses at Ω_p = T/(I·ω). Bottom: an off-center mass on a rotating shaft creates a centrifugal force F = m·ω²·r that the bearings have to absorb every revolution. Prop balance and gyro stabilization are two faces of the same coin.

A. Gyroscopic precession

Angular momentum vector H⃗ = I·ω⃗ along the spin axis. Apply an external torque T⃗ perpendicular to H⃗ → H⃗ precesses around the torque axis at rate Ω_p = T / (I·ω). A spinning top doesn't fall because gravity's torque drives precession, not falling.

B. Rotor imbalance — static & dynamic

Static imbalance: an off-center mass on a spinning shaft generates a centrifugal force F = m·ω²·r rotating with the shaft. Even a tiny mass at high RPM is a hammer-blow at the bearing — once per revolution (at 1× shaft frequency), the casing sees a rotating force vector pushing outward through the imbalance direction.

Dynamic imbalance: two equal masses 180° apart cancel statically but create a couple if they're on different planes along the shaft. Two-plane balancing is required for long rotors.

Hobby propeller balancing

At 8000 RPM (a typical quadcopter motor speed), even 0.1 g offset by 5 mm produces ≈ 0.35 N of rotating force at the bearing — small in absolute terms, but cyclic at 133 Hz. Over a multi-minute flight that's millions of load cycles into the bearing, the motor housing, and the IMU's accelerometer. Hobbyists use a magnetic or two-rail balancer to bring the prop's heavy side to neutral, often by sanding or taping correction mass on the light side. The math is the same as industrial two-plane balancing; only the precision is different.

What to notice

  • Precession rate Ω_p = T/(I·ω). Big I or big ω → tiny precession from a given torque. That's why fast-spinning rotors feel "stiff" to turn — they precess instead of yawing.
  • Direction rule (right-hand): H⃗ × T⃗ direction → precession direction. Drone pilots feel this as pitch/roll coupling when they yaw.
  • Gyroscopic stiffness helps and hurts. Helps for camera gimbal stability; hurts for fast intentional maneuvers — the motor has to fight the precession.
  • F = m·ω²·r is brutal at speed. Doubling RPM quadruples force. Going from 4000 to 8000 RPM with the same imbalance makes the bearing see 4× the force.
  • The force rotates with the shaft. The bearing sees the load at 1× shaft frequency — same as the rotation rate. Match this to mechanical resonance and you have a disaster.
  • Static balance ≠ dynamic balance. A shaft can be statically balanced (CG on axis) yet still wobble because two off-axis masses on different planes create a rocking couple. Drones rarely need two-plane; long shafts (drive shafts, drum rotors) always do.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

Ang. momentum
H = I · ω_spin
Precession
Ω_p = T / (I · ω_spin)
Centrifugal F
F = m · ω² · r

RIGHT-HAND RULE

  • spin axis × applied torque = precession axis
  • Torque direction by right-hand rule (curl fingers along T, thumb gives vector)
  • Higher ω_spin → slower precession for same T (gyro stiffness)

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Static balance ≠ dynamic balance — long rotors need two-plane balancing
  • 0.1 g imbalance at 8000 RPM is a significant bearing force (F ∝ ω²)
  • Gyro stiffness fights intentional maneuvers — motors must overcome precession reaction
  • Force rotates with the shaft at 1× shaft freq — avoid matching mechanical resonance
