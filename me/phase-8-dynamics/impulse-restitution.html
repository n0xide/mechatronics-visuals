Impulse & Coefficient of Restitution

When two bodies collide, momentum is always conserved — but kinetic energy generally isn't. The coefficient of restitution e bridges them: e ∈ [0, 1] by definition — e = 0 is a perfectly plastic collision (objects stick), e = 1 is perfectly elastic (no energy lost). Why momentum is always conserved but energy isn't

Momentum conservation comes from Newton's 3rd law: the force one body exerts on the other is equal and opposite. The impulses cancel; the system's total momentum is unchanged by the collision.

1. During collision: F_12 = −F_21 (Newton 3). 2. Integrate over collision time: ∫F_12 dt = −∫F_21 dt → J_12 = −J_21. 3. Δp_1 = J_21 and Δp_2 = J_12, so Δp_1 + Δp_2 = 0. → Σp constant. 4. Energy is different: during collision, both bodies deform. If deformation is elastic, all stored energy returns as KE. If plastic (sound, heat, permanent dent), it doesn't. 5. The coefficient of restitution e is the ratio of relative separation speed to relative approach speed: e = (v₂' − v₁') / (v₁ − v₂) (note: signs!) 6. Combined with momentum conservation, e fixes the after-velocities: v₁' = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ − m₂·e·(v₁ − v₂)) / (m₁ + m₂) v₂' = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ + m₁·e·(v₁ − v₂)) / (m₁ + m₂)

Worked example — pendulum impact tester (idealised: not a real Charpy)

The setup

A pendulum-style impact rig drops a 5 kg head from height h = 1 m, swings down, hits a 0.3 kg test piece sitting on a frictionless rail (so it's free to fly off — unlike a real Charpy test where the specimen is broken and the energy gets absorbed into deformation). The pendulum swings back to height h' = 0.4 m. What is e?

Why this isn't Charpy: a real Charpy specimen is clamped at both ends and absorbs energy by fracturing, so the simple 2-body collision math doesn't apply — the standard Charpy reading is the energy difference (m·g·(h − h')), not a coefficient of restitution. This worked example uses Charpy-like geometry to teach impulse + restitution on a free-flying target.

Velocities

v₁ before = √(2 g h) = √(2 · 9.81 · 1) = 4.43 m/s v₁ after (on rebound) = ±√(2 g h') = ±√(2 · 9.81 · 0.4) = ±2.80 m/s Since pendulum swings back: v₁' = +2.80 m/s (toward where it came from); call right + so v₁' = −2.80 m/s in our convention. Test piece: v₂ before = 0; v₂' unknown.

Conservation + e

Momentum: 5 · 4.43 + 0.3 · 0 = 5 · (−2.80) + 0.3 · v₂' 22.15 = −14 + 0.3 v₂' → v₂' = 36.15 / 0.3 = 120.5 m/s Hmm, that's not physical for a 0.3 kg piece hit by 4.43 m/s. Check. Actually: if the test piece is light, it gets a kick. But 120 m/s exceeds the pendulum speed → check e. e = (v₂' − v₁') / (v₁ − v₂) = (120.5 − (−2.80)) / (4.43 − 0) = 123.3 / 4.43 = e > 27 → impossible.

Correct interpretation

The Charpy hammer doesn't release the test piece — it absorbs energy by deforming/breaking it. Energy difference = absorbed energy (the standard Charpy reading).

E_absorbed = ½ · 5 · 4.43² − ½ · 5 · 2.80² = 49 − 20 = 29 J This is the Charpy impact energy (kJ/m² with sample area). Use it directly; don't try to model as 2-body collision.

Energy-loss formula in two-body collision

For two free bodies (no external constraint):

1. Reduced mass: μ = m₁m₂ / (m₁ + m₂) 2. Relative velocity before: v_rel = v₁ − v₂ 3. ΔKE = ½ · μ · v_rel² · (1 − e²) 4. At e = 0: ΔKE = ½ μ v_rel² — all relative-motion energy is absorbed. 5. At e = 1: ΔKE = 0 — no energy lost (elastic). 6. Bullet (μ → m_bullet for huge m_target): ΔKE ≈ ½ m_b v_b². Almost all the bullet's energy goes into the target.

What to notice

  • Momentum survives, energy doesn't — heat and sound are where the energy goes. A loud collision is energy fleeing as sound; a hot dent is energy as heat.
  • e is material × geometry — same ball on harder surface has higher e (steel on steel > rubber on rubber). Tennis ball pressure changes e (de-pressurized ball has e ~ 0.65 vs new ~0.85).
  • e decreases with impact speed — at higher speeds, materials deform plastically (yield → permanent damage). Above the elastic limit, e drops fast.
  • Coefficient of restitution is path-independent — you can drop a ball off a 1 m roof or 10 m roof; the height ratio (bounce / drop) gives the same e. e = √(h_bounce / h_drop).
  • Newton's cradle isn't perfect — even the best executive toy has e ~ 0.98. After 30 swings, motion is visibly damped. Steel on steel really is 95%+ elastic.
  • Impulse equals Δp — useful even when force-time history is complex. A 0.05 kg golf ball going 70 m/s post-impact had Δp = 3.5 N·s applied by the club, regardless of how the impulse was distributed over the 1 ms contact.

e VALUES

  • Steel on steel: 0.55–0.95
  • Glass marbles: ~0.95
  • Tennis ball: 0.85 (new)
  • Basketball: 0.75–0.80
  • Bowling pin: ~0.7
  • Wood block: ~0.4
  • Soft clay: ~0

PITFALLS

  • Sign of velocities — relative speed in same direction or opposite changes the equations.
  • Forgetting 2nd equation — momentum alone has 2 unknowns; e equation closes it.
  • e isn't constant — drops with speed and with surface deformation.
  • Coefficient of restitution vs efficiency — different concepts; e applies to a single collision, η to a continuous process.
  • 3D collisions — e applies along the normal of contact; tangential motion is governed by friction.
  • Embedded impact — when bullet sticks, e = 0; use kinematics, not energy balance, to find common velocity.
