Logarithmic Decrement — Identifying ζ from Free Decay

Whack a structure and let it ring. Measure two successive peaks. Their ratio gives the damping ratio ζ — directly, with one line of arithmetic. This is how modal-testing engineers extract damping from real hardware: tap, record, fit. The plot below shows a decaying sinusoid; pick which two peaks to use, see ζ pop out, and compare to the catalog values for common materials.

δ = (1/n) ln(x₁/x_{n+1}) ζ = δ / √(4π² + δ²) ζ < 0.1: lightly damped (typical) ζ = 1: critical damping

Method: measure x at 1st peak, count n peaks ahead, measure that peak. Take ratio, ln, divide by n → δ. Then ζ.

Where the formula comes from

A damped 2nd-order system: m ẍ + c ẋ + k x = 0. Standard substitution x = e^(rt) gives characteristic roots

r = −ζω_n ± ω_n √(ζ² − 1) For ζ < 1 (underdamped, the case of interest): roots are complex. Solution: x(t) = x₀ · e^(−ζω_n t) · sin(ω_d t + φ) where ω_d = ω_n √(1 − ζ²) (damped natural frequency) Envelope decays exponentially at rate ζω_n. Look at two peaks: at t = t_1 and t = t_1 + n·T_d (n cycles later). x₁ = X · e^(−ζω_n t_1) ; x_{n+1} = X · e^(−ζω_n (t_1 + n T_d)) Ratio: x₁ / x_{n+1} = e^(ζω_n n T_d) Take ln: ln(x₁/x_{n+1}) = ζω_n · n · T_d = ζω_n · n · (2π / ω_d) = 2πn · ζ / √(1−ζ²) Per-cycle: δ = (1/n) ln(x₁/x_{n+1}) = 2π ζ / √(1−ζ²) Invert: ζ = δ / √(4π² + δ²). → Two measurements give ζ.

Why we use multiple cycles (n > 1)

  • Noise robustness — if x₁ has measurement noise σ, then ln(x₁/x₂) has noise ~σ/x. Averaging over n cycles: ln(x₁/x_{n+1}) is n times larger; noise on the ratio is √n higher → SNR improves by √n.
  • Catches non-exponential decay — if the system has nonlinear damping (Coulomb friction, for instance), the per-cycle log ratio drifts. Comparing many cycles lets you spot this and fit a different model.
  • Practical rule: pick n so that x_{n+1} is around 10–30% of x₁. Smaller, and measurement noise dominates; larger, and you didn't use any data from later cycles.
  • Half-power method is an alternative (use bandwidth of the forced-response peak at √2/2 amplitude). Free decay is simpler when you can excite once and watch.

Worked example — laptop hinge resonance

The measurement

Tap a laptop lid; it rings at f_d ≈ 25 Hz (T_d = 40 ms). Capture ~500 ms of accelerometer data — enough to see 12 cycles cleanly above the noise floor. First peak = 1.0 m/s² (normalized). Tenth subsequent peak (n = 10) = 0.05 m/s².

Compute

δ = (1/10) · ln(1.0 / 0.05) = 0.1 · ln(20) = 0.1 · 2.996 = 0.300 ζ = 0.300 / √(4π² + 0.300²) = 0.300 / √(39.568) = 0.300 / 6.290 = 0.0477 ≈ 4.8% damping — typical for Al hinge with a small plastic bushing

Verify period

ω_d = 2π · 25 = 157 rad/s ω_n = ω_d / √(1 − ζ²) = 157 / √(0.9977) = 157.2 rad/s → only 0.1% adjustment For ζ < 0.1, ω_d ≈ ω_n is a fine approximation.

What it tells you

ζ ≈ 5% means about 10% amplitude decay per cycle. The hinge will ring for ~30 cycles (~1.2 s) before dropping below 1% of initial. That's audible (you hear the resonance). Adding a foam pad or rubber gasket between hinge and chassis raises ζ to 15–20%; the resonance becomes dead-feeling and a tap dies in 2–3 cycles.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

Log decrement
δ = (1/n) ln(x₁/x_{n+1})
From δ to ζ
ζ = δ / √(4π² + δ²)
Approx (small ζ)
δ ≈ 2πζ → ζ ≈ δ/(2π)
Damped period
T_d = 2π / ω_d
Damped freq
ω_d = ω_n √(1 − ζ²)
Q factor
Q = 1 / (2ζ)

RULES OF THUMB

  • x ratio 2 → δ ≈ 0.69, ζ ≈ 11%
  • x ratio 10 → δ ≈ 2.30, ζ ≈ 35%
  • 10 cycles to settle → ζ ≈ 5%
  • 3 cycles to settle → ζ ≈ 15%
  • 2-3% target for stable but quick-settling structures
  • n = 10 cycles is a good measurement length

PITFALLS

  • Reading wrong peak — must be successive positive-going or successive negative-going. Pick consistent.
  • Noise floor — at low amplitude, noise dominates. Don't use peaks below 5× noise.
  • Nonlinear damping — δ shouldn't be constant cycle-to-cycle if there's nonlinear (Coulomb) friction.
  • Coupled modes — if two modes are close in frequency, decay shows beats; log decrement isn't valid.
  • Window / sampling — short sample misses lower-frequency components; aliasing can fake peaks.
  • Confusing ζ and ξ — same thing, different letters. ζ is more common in US, ξ in EU.
