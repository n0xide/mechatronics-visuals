3. Parallel-axis theorem: I = I_cg + m·d²

Shift the rotation axis off the centroid by a distance d. The body's inertia about the new axis grows quadratically with d. This is why a robotic-arm link rotated about its base joint has roughly 4× the inertia of the same link rotated about its center.

0.15 m

What to notice

  • α = T / I is the rotational F = m·a. Doubling torque doubles angular acceleration; doubling mass halves it (with size held fixed); doubling size *quadruples* I and *quarters* α.
  • I scales with size squared. A 2× bigger disk with the same mass has 4× the inertia. Bigger bodies are dramatically harder to spin up.
  • Where the mass sits matters more than how much there is. Hollow sphere (2/3) vs solid sphere (2/5) — same mass, same radius, but the hollow shell has 67% more inertia because all its mass is at maximum r.
  • Rod about end has 4× the inertia of rod about center. mL²/3 vs mL²/12. This is the parallel-axis theorem with d = L/2 in action.
  • Robot-arm consequence (arm-6dof-pa612): J1 carries the moment of inertia of everything outboard, shifted by the parallel-axis term. Each downstream link adds m·d² where d is its centroid distance to J1 — payload at full reach can dwarf the arm's own I.
  • Flywheel design: energy stored E = ½ I ω². To pack more energy at safe RPM, put mass at the rim, not the hub — same kg of steel, much more I.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Units: I in kg·m², ω in rad/s — convert RPM via 2π/60
  • Rolling without slip → split KE between translation and rotation
  • Rod about end = 4× rod about center (parallel-axis with d = L/2)
  • Hollow sphere = ⅔ m r², solid = ⅖ m r² — placement of mass > total mass
