Transmissibility — Vibration Isolation

A vibration isolator sits between a noisy machine (or noisy floor) and what needs to be quiet. The transmissibility T is the ratio of transmitted to source amplitude. Below the natural frequency, the isolator passes vibration through. Above ω_n√2, it attenuates. Past 3·ω_n, attenuation is huge — but only if you don't pass through resonance during startup. Drag the frequency ratio r and damping ζ; the magic point at r = √2 marks the boundary.

r < √2: amplifies (worse than no isolator) r = √2: T = 1 (break-even) r > √2: isolates r > 3: typical design target

Derivation — base excitation

The mass m sits on a spring (stiffness k) and damper (c) on a floor that shakes with y(t) = Y·sin(ωt). What does the mass do?

EoM: m ẍ + c (ẋ − ẏ) + k (x − y) = 0 Let z = x − y (relative displacement). Then: m z̈ + c ż + k z = − m ÿ Steady-state x(t) = X sin(ωt − φ). Apply trig identities and solve for amplitude ratio: T = X / Y = √(1 + (2ζr)²) / √((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²) where r = ω/ω_n and ω_n = √(k/m). Phase: tan φ = (2ζr³) / (1 − r² + (2ζr)²) — phase has a cubic-r term, unlike basic forced response. Limits: r → 0: T → 1 (slow base shake; mass goes with the floor) r → 1: T peaks near 1/(2ζ) (resonance!) r → ∞: T → 0 (mass becomes "frozen" inertia, floor vibrates around it) r = √2: T = 1 exactly, for any ζ. → Cross-over frequency.

Force transmission (same formula, different source)

Replace base excitation with a force on the mass (e.g., rotating machine on the floor; we want to know how much shakes the floor). The math is identical with T = F_floor / F_machine.

For force transmission: T = √(1 + (2ζr)²) / √((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²) — same formula. Design principle: lower the natural frequency until r = ω_excitation / ω_n is >> 1. If your machine spins at 1800 RPM = 30 Hz = 188 rad/s, and you want T = 0.1, you need: T = 0.1 (around 10× isolation) requires r ≈ 3.3 (with light damping). So ω_n ≤ 188/3.3 = 57 rad/s, f_n ≤ 9 Hz. For mass m on spring with deflection δ under static load: f_n = (1/2π)√(g/δ). To get f_n = 9 Hz, need δ ≈ 3 mm. Softer = more isolation but more swing during transients.

Worked example — 3D printer isolation pad

The problem

A Bambu X1C runs head accelerations ~ 20,000 mm/s² and dwells at moderate frequencies (X-Y bed movements 5–50 Hz). The printer body sits on the kitchen counter; counter rings loud below the printer feet. Goal: 90% reduction in transmitted vibration above 30 Hz.

Sizing

Want T ≤ 0.1 above 30 Hz From the curve, for ζ ≈ 0.05 (typical foam/rubber): r ≥ 3.5 → ω_n ≤ 30/3.5 = 8.6 Hz → f_n ≤ 8.6 Hz, ω_n ≤ 54 rad/s Printer mass m ≈ 14 kg → required spring rate k = m·ω_n² = 14 × 54² = 40,800 N/m total Four feet → k_per_foot = 10,200 N/m

Foam pad selection

Sorbothane 50 durometer, 25 mm thick, 50 mm diameter: stiffness ~ 10–15 kN/m → matches Static deflection: δ = m·g / k = 14 × 9.81 / 40800 = 3.4 mm → ¼ of pad thickness, OK

Check resonance

Peak transmissibility at r = 1: T_peak = 1/(2·0.05) = 10 If anything excites the printer at 8.6 Hz, it amplifies 10×. Bed homing or first layer can resonate. Mitigation: choose pad with higher damping. ζ = 0.20 → T_peak = 2.5, much safer.

Outcome

Sorbothane pads (high internal damping ζ ≈ 0.2) are the textbook answer for printer isolation — high enough damping that the resonance peak doesn't ring, soft enough that f_n stays below 10 Hz.

What to notice

  • r < √2 = isolator makes it WORSE — this is the counterintuitive trap. If you put a too-stiff mount under a slow-vibration source, you amplify rather than attenuate. r = 0.7 with ζ = 0.05 gives T ≈ 2 — twice the transmitted amplitude.
  • Damping reduces resonance peak but hurts high-frequency isolation — at r >> 1, increasing ζ raises T (the (2ζr)² term wins in the numerator). High-r isolation prefers low ζ, but you must pass through resonance during startup.
  • r = √2 is a universal point — every transmissibility curve crosses T = 1 at exactly r = √2, regardless of damping. This is the design boundary: above √2, you're isolating; below, you're amplifying.
  • Soft mount = low f_n = good isolation — but also more static deflection and more rocking during transients. The trade-off is acoustic/vibration isolation vs stability of position.
  • Two stages beat one — for ultra-clean optical tables, stack a soft mount under the table and a stiff active mount on it. Each stage T = 0.1 → combined 0.01.
  • Damping needs to be there during startup — a rotating machine starting up sweeps through resonance. Without damping, it hits T_peak = 1/(2ζ) = ∞. Use lockable detuned dampers (e.g., foam donut on top of the isolator) that engage at resonance and release at higher frequency.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

T
√(1+(2ζr)²) / √((1−r²)²+(2ζr)²)
Cross-over
T = 1 at r = √2 (always)
Peak
T_peak ≈ 1/(2ζ) at r ≈ 1
Asymptotic
T → 2ζ/r for r >> 1 with finite ζ   (T → 1/r² only when ζ = 0)
Static deflect
δ = g / ω_n², f_n = (1/2π)√(g/δ)
Isolation %
100·(1 − T)

DESIGN TARGETS

  • Office equipment: r ≥ 3, ζ ~ 0.05
  • Sensitive instruments: r ≥ 5, multi-stage
  • Vehicles: r ~ 2-3, ζ ~ 0.4 (over-damped for ride)
  • Building isolators: r ≥ 4, dual ζ (high near resonance, low past)
  • 3D printer pad: r ≥ 3.5, ζ ~ 0.2

PITFALLS

  • Forgetting r < √2 amplifies — adding a stiff mount can make things worse.
  • Resonance during startup — every rotating machine sweeps through ω_n. Damp it.
  • Soft mount tipping — too-soft mount lets equipment rock/sway. Add lateral stops.
  • Foam compression set — most foams compress over time → f_n drifts up → isolation degrades.
  • Resonance of structure itself — TM analyzes rigid mass; if the equipment has its own bending modes, those resonate independently.
  • Wrong f_excitation — running an analysis at the machine's rated speed; reality includes 2× harmonics and gear-mesh higher-orders.
