Logarithmic decrement

For an underdamped system, measure the ratio of two successive peaks: δ = ln(x₁/x₂). Then ζ = δ / √(4π² + δ²) ≈ δ/(2π) for small ζ. This lets you back out the damping ratio of a real system from a free-decay measurement.

What to notice

  • ωn depends only on m and k. The damper changes how fast oscillation dies, not the natural frequency. ωd = ωn√(1-ζ²) only diverges from ωn for high damping.
  • Critical damping is the speed-record holder for non-overshoot. Anything less overshoots; anything more is slower.
  • Doubling k raises ωn by √2. Frequency scales as the square root of stiffness — twice the spring, only 41% higher frequency.
  • Quadrupling mass halves ωn. Same square-root relationship in reverse: adding mass is a strong frequency-shifter.
  • Logarithmic decrement is the diagnostic trick. Strike a structure, count successive peak ratios, get ζ. You don't need to know m or k.
  • Real systems almost always have ζ in the 0.005–0.1 range unless deliberately damped. PETG, aluminum, steel are all "underdamped" by default.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Use effective mass & stiffness for the mode of interest — not total mass
  • Pendulum ω_n = √(g/L) valid only for small angles (sin θ ≈ θ)
  • Doubling k → ω_n × √2 (not 2×); same for halving m
  • Real materials default to ζ ≈ 0.005–0.1 — assume underdamped unless designed otherwise
