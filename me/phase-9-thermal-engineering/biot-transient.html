Biot Number & Transient Cooling — when is lumped capacitance valid?

A hot solid plunged into a cool fluid loses heat over time. The simple "lumped capacitance" model assumes the entire solid is at uniform temperature — internal conduction is fast compared to surface convection. Whether that assumption holds is decided by one number: the Biot number, Bi = h·L_c / k. Below 0.1, lumped is accurate (≤ 5% error). Above, you need the spatial solution (Heisler charts or first-term series). This visual lets you sweep material, geometry, size, and h to see Bi flip from "lumped OK" to "spatial gradients matter," and plots the cooling curve T(t) live.

The math behind Bi and τ

The Biot number compares internal conduction resistance to external convection resistance: Bi = h·L_c / k_solid Characteristic length L_c (depends on shape): Plane wall (insulated back, length 2L): L_c = L (half-thickness) Long cylinder, radius r: L_c = V/A_s = (πr²·H)/(2πr·H) = r/2 Sphere, radius r: L_c = V/A_s = (4/3·πr³)/(4πr²) = r/3 Generic shape: L_c = V / A_s If Bi < 0.1, lumped capacitance is accurate to ≤ 5%. Energy balance: ρ·V·c_p · dT/dt = −h·A_s · (T − T_∞) Solution: T(t) = T_∞ + (T₀ − T_∞) · exp(−t/τ) Time constant: τ = ρ·V·c_p / (h·A_s) = ρ·c_p·L_c / h After τ: 63% of the way from T₀ to T_∞ After 3τ: 95% of the way After 5τ: 99% (treated as "settled") Thermal diffusivity (intrinsic to material): α = k / (ρ·c_p) [m²/s] Fourier number (dimensionless time): Fo = α·t / L_c² Fo = 1 means heat has diffused one characteristic length. For Bi > 0.1, use one-term series approximation (valid for Fo > 0.2): Plane wall center: θ* = C₁ · exp(−ζ₁² · Fo) where ζ₁ and C₁ are tabulated functions of Bi (Incropera Table 5.1). For full Bi sweep + arbitrary Fo, use the Heisler charts or numerical sim.

Worked example — small aluminum sphere quenched in water

Setup

Aluminum sphere, 20 mm diameter (r = 10 mm), initially at 200 °C. Plunged into a 20 °C water bath. Water-side h ≈ 500 W/m²·K (forced convection over a sphere at moderate Re). How fast does it cool?

Bi check

L_c = r/3 = 0.010/3 = 3.33 mm = 0.00333 m Bi = h·L_c / k = 500 · 0.00333 / 237 = 0.0070 Bi ≪ 0.1 → lumped capacitance valid ✓

Time constant + curve

τ = ρ·c_p·L_c / h = 2702 · 903 · 0.00333 / 500 = 16.3 s 5τ ≈ 81 s for the sphere to reach within 1% of 20 °C At t = τ: T = 20 + 180·e⁻¹ = 20 + 66 = 86 °C At t = 3τ ≈ 49 s: T = 20 + 180·e⁻³ = 20 + 9 = 29 °C

Compare to a steel sphere: same geometry, k = 60.5 W/m·K. Bi = 500·0.00333/60.5 = 0.0275 (still < 0.1, lumped still OK). But τ = 7854 · 434 · 0.00333 / 500 = 22.7 s — slower despite much higher conductivity, because steel's volumetric heat capacity (ρ·c_p = 3.4 MJ/m³·K) exceeds aluminum's (2.4 MJ/m³·K). Conductivity changes Bi; it doesn't change τ for lumped objects.

When lumped fails — Bi > 0.1

What goes wrong

If Bi is large, the surface cools fast but the center stays hot — there's a real spatial gradient. The lumped formula underpredicts the center temperature and overpredicts the average heat removed.

Example: a glass billet (k = 1.4) at 500 °C plunged in oil (h = 800 W/m²K), r = 50 mm. L_c = r/3 = 16.7 mm. Bi = 800 · 0.0167 / 1.4 = 9.5 — completely outside lumped territory.

Solutions for large Bi

  • Heisler charts: graphical lookup of θ_center vs Fo, parameterized by Bi. Used pre-1990s by hand; still in textbooks (Incropera Ch. 5).
  • One-term series approximation for Fo > 0.2: θ* = C₁·exp(−ζ₁²·Fo), with ζ₁(Bi) and C₁(Bi) tabulated.
  • Numerical FD/FEM: explicit Euler on a discretized rod / cylinder / sphere; converges if dt < (Δx)² / (2α).
  • CFD with conjugate heat transfer: full convection-conduction coupling; for production design only.

Sanity rule

If your problem has Bi > 0.1 and you used the lumped τ anyway: center temperature error grows ~Bi/3 in fraction. For Bi = 0.5, expect ~17% under-prediction of center T at moderate Fo. For thermal-cutoff design (where the center reaching some T matters), this is unsafe.

Common pitfalls

  • Wrong L_c for the shape — use V/A_s, not "half-the-thickness" by reflex. Sphere L_c = r/3, cylinder L_c = r/2, plane wall (insulated one side) L_c = L.
  • Properties at the wrong temperature — ρ, c_p, k all drift with T. For 300 °C steel, k drops to ~55 (from 60 at 25 °C). Use mid-range properties for the largest accuracy gain.
  • h is constant? — convective h depends on ΔT (free convection) and surface T (forced air viscosity drift). Iterate if precision matters.
  • Forgetting radiation — for surfaces > 100 °C in still air, radiation can equal or exceed forced convection. Treat h_total = h_conv + h_rad.
  • Initial uniform-T assumption — if you welded the part, it didn't start uniform. Real initial conditions can shift τ by 30%.
  • Using lumped past Bi = 0.1 because "it's close" — error scales with Fo. Long-time behavior is more wrong than short-time.
  • Confusing time constant and thermal-rise time — τ to reach 63%, 3τ for 95%, 5τ for "settled." Don't quote "100% in τ."
