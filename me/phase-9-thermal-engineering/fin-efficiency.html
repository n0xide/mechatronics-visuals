Fin Efficiency

A heat-sink fin sticks out into the air to dump heat. But each point along the fin is cooler than the base — so the fin face nearest the tip rejects less heat than the face near the base. Fin efficiency η_fin is the actual heat rejected vs the maximum possible (if the whole fin were at base temperature). It depends on fin geometry and conductivity through a single parameter m·L. The formulas below assume steady state, 1D conduction along the fin, uniform h on the fin surfaces, and an adiabatic (insulated) tip. Slide fin length, thickness, and material; watch when adding more fin length just adds weight, not cooling.

Where the efficiency formula comes from

Treat the fin as a long thin rod sticking out of a hot base into air at T_∞. Heat conducts along the fin (axially) and convects off the sides.

Energy balance on a slice dx of fin: heat in by conduction − heat out by conduction (axially) − heat off by convection (sides) = 0 (steady). For uniform A_c (cross-section) and P (perimeter): d²θ/dx² − m²θ = 0 where θ = T(x) − T_∞ and m² = h·P/(k·A_c) For long fin with insulated tip (or zero-tip-convection idealization), the temperature profile is θ(x) = θ_b · cosh(m(L − x)) / cosh(mL). Total heat rejected: Q_fin = √(h·P·k·A_c) · θ_b · tanh(mL) Ideal max (whole fin at θ_b): Q_max = h · A_surf · θ_b ≈ h · P · L · θ_b Ratio: η_fin = Q_fin / Q_max = tanh(mL) / (mL)

The mL trade-off

Two extreme regimes, one sweet spot:

Short fin (mL << 1): tanh(mL) ≈ mL → η ≈ 1. Almost the whole fin is at base temp. But A_surf is small → Q is small. The fin works ideally but barely matters. Long fin (mL >> 1): tanh(mL) → 1 → η → 1/(mL). Tip temperature → T_∞; tip is useless. Heat capped by conduction along fin, not by surface area. Adding more length adds weight and pressure drop without proportional cooling. Sweet spot: mL ≈ 1–1.5, η = 75–90%. Most of the fin is "warm enough" to convect; extra length pays diminishing returns past mL = 2. For a given m (fixed material, geometry, h): adding L decreases η but adds A_surf. Combined Q = η · h · A_surf · θ_b peaks somewhere — but slowly. Often "make as long as you can" is bounded by space, weight, or pressure drop, not by efficiency.

Worked example — CPU heat sink fin sizing

The problem

A CPU dissipates 50 W. Allow ΔT_max = 60 K above 25 °C ambient. Forced air at 25 °C provides h ≈ 50 W/m²·K. Heat-sink base is aluminum with 20 fins; design each fin.

Per-fin geometry

Per-fin Q = 50 / 20 = 2.5 W Fin material Al: k = 200 W/m·K Trial: w = 40 mm wide, t = 1.5 mm thick, L = ? m² = h·P/(k·A_c) = 50 · 2(0.040 + 0.0015) / (200 · 0.040 · 0.0015) = 4.15 / 0.012 = 346 → m = 18.6 /m

Required surface area

If η = 0.8: Q = 0.8 · 50 · A_fin · 60 = 2400 · A_fin = 2.5 W → A_fin = 1.04 × 10⁻³ m² ≈ 1040 mm² A_fin ≈ 2 · w · L (top + bottom faces) = 2 · 0.040 · L = 0.08 L → L = 13 mm Check: mL = 18.6 · 0.013 = 0.24 → η = tanh(0.24)/0.24 = 0.98. Refit: If η = 0.98: Q = 0.98 · 50 · 2·0.040·L · 60 = 235 L → L = 2.5/235 = 0.0106 m = 11 mm. Iterate: mL = 0.20, η = 0.99. Converged → L ≈ 11 mm.

Design check

A 40 × 11 mm Al fin at h = 50 is heavily over-fanned: η = 99%, you could make it shorter or thinner. In practice you stack 20–30 fins of 25-50 mm length and run lower h (less fan), or use natural convection (h ~ 5–10) which forces longer fins. Slide h down to 10 W/m²·K to see η drop.

What to notice

  • Copper is silly for natural convection — at low h (~5 W/m²·K), even short Al fins have η > 95%. Copper's higher k buys very little extra. Use Al; save the cost.
  • Stainless and plastic make terrible fins — low k means short conduction length before the fin cools off. mL gets large quickly; most of the fin is dead weight. Steel-frame heat sinks need vapor chambers or heat pipes to compensate.
  • Forced air (high h) hurts efficiency — h appears in m². Stronger fan → bigger m → shorter fin needed for η. Counter-intuitively, fans need shorter fins, not longer ones, to maintain efficiency.
  • Thin fins are better… up to a point — thinning increases P/A_c slowly, but conduction resistance grows fast. Too thin and the fin can't get heat to the surface. Optimum is around t = 1–2 mm for Al.
  • Tip temperature ≈ ΔT_b / cosh(mL) — at mL = 1, tip is at 65% of base; at mL = 2, tip is at 27%; at mL = 3, tip is at 10%. Beyond mL ≈ 3 the tip is just air temperature and you're wasting material.
  • Spacing matters too — fins too close (closer than boundary layer thickness) trap stagnant air and h drops. Typical spacing: 5–10 mm for natural convection, 2–4 mm with fan.

PITFALLS

  • Fin too long — extra tip material does nothing past mL ≈ 3.
  • Plastic fins — k is so low that even tiny fins are inefficient.
  • Tip convection — assumed insulated tip; real tips lose heat. Use "corrected length" L_c = L + t/2.
  • Adjacent fins interfere — boundary-layer collision drops effective h.
  • Forgetting base resistance — heat must reach the fin base. Big spreaders or heat pipes are needed for hot-spot CPUs.
  • Comparing η ratios across designs — η = 90% on a small fin can reject less heat than η = 50% on a big fin. Q = η·A·ΔT is what matters.
