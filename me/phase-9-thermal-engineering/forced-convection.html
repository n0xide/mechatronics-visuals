Forced Convection Correlations — Nu, Re, Pr and how to find h

Conduction is easy: q = k·A·ΔT/L. Convection is harder: the heat-transfer coefficient h depends on fluid properties, flow speed, geometry, and whether the flow is laminar or turbulent. Engineers use empirical Nusselt-number correlations — Nu = h·L/k = C·Re^m·Pr^n — to predict h from dimensionless numbers. This visual lets you pick a geometry, sweep air velocity and characteristic length, and watch h, the flow regime, and the resulting heat-transfer rate update live.

Nu = h·L/k (dimensionless h) Re = V·L/ν (inertia vs viscous) Pr = ν/α (momentum vs thermal diffusivity) — air ≈ 0.707 Flat plate transition: Re ≈ 5 × 105 h (W/m²K): nat-air 5-25 · forced-air 25-500 · forced-water 500-20k · boiling 5k-100k

The math behind it

Three dimensionless numbers govern forced convection: Re = ρ·V·L / µ = V·L / ν — ratio of inertia to viscous forces; tells you laminar vs turbulent Pr = ν / α = µ·c_p / k — ratio of momentum to thermal diffusivity Nu = h·L / k — dimensionless heat-transfer coefficient Pick the right correlation for geometry + regime, compute Nu, solve for h: h = Nu · k / L Then Newton's law of cooling gives the heat transfer rate: Q = h · A · ΔT (A = wetted area, ΔT = T_surface − T_fluid) Fluid properties evaluated at film temperature: T_film = (T_s + T_∞)/2. Air at 25 °C: ν = 1.56×10⁻⁵ m²/s · k = 0.0263 W/m·K · Pr = 0.707 Air at 100 °C: ν = 2.36×10⁻⁵ m²/s · k = 0.0314 W/m·K · Pr = 0.694 Water at 25 °C: ν = 8.93×10⁻⁷ m²/s · k = 0.607 W/m·K · Pr = 6.13

Why correlations and not first principles?

The Navier-Stokes equations describe the velocity + temperature fields exactly, but closed-form solutions exist only for simple geometries (Blasius flat plate, Graetz pipe inlet, etc.). For everything else — heat-sink fins, fans blowing on PCBs, fluid past a wing — you need either:

  • Experimental correlations: fit Nu = f(Re, Pr) to wind-tunnel data. Cheap, fast, accurate enough for design.
  • CFD: numerical solution of Navier-Stokes on a meshed domain. Accurate but slow and needs skill.
  • Lumped thermal model: pretend convection is just a resistance R_conv = 1/(h·A), use SPICE-like networks. Fast but coarse.

For electronics cooling, correlations + lumped models are the workhorses. Spend the CFD budget on the boundary cases that matter (worst hot spot, end-of-life fan curve).

Worked example — 100 W CPU on a heat-sink + 80 mm fan

Setup

An i7 CPU dissipating 100 W under load. Heat-sink: 80 × 80 mm footprint, 60 mm tall, 20 aluminum fins. An 80 mm fan delivers air at ~3 m/s through the fin channel. T_ambient = 25 °C. What's the chip temperature?

Reynolds + Nusselt

Treat the fin channel as flat-plate flow with L = 80 mm = 0.080 m Re = V·L / ν = 3 · 0.080 / 1.56e-5 = 15,400 (laminar — Re < 5×10⁵) Nu = 0.664 · 15400^0.5 · 0.707^(1/3) = 0.664 · 124.1 · 0.891 = 73.4 h = Nu · k / L = 73.4 · 0.0263 / 0.080 = 24.1 W/m²·K

Total wetted area + temperature

Fin area: 20 fins · 2 sides · (80 mm long × 60 mm tall) = 20 · 2 · 0.0048 = 0.192 m² η_fin ≈ 0.85 (typical for Al, aspect ratio considered) → A_eff = 0.163 m² Q = h · A · ΔT → 100 = 24.1 · 0.163 · ΔT → ΔT = 25.5 K T_chip = 25 + 25.5 = 50.5 °C ✓ comfortable

Sanity check via "h ≈ 25 W/m²K for low-speed forced air"

The rule-of-thumb matches the correlation result. If we tripled fan speed to 9 m/s, Re = 46,200, Nu = 127 (laminar still), h = 41.7 W/m²K — 73% more h for 200% more fan speed. Convection scales as ~V0.5; doubling fan rarely doubles cooling.

Mistakes that bite

  • Wrong characteristic length — flat plate uses plate length in flow direction; cylinder uses diameter; fin channel uses hydraulic diameter D_h = 4A/P. Pick correctly or Re is off by an order of magnitude.
  • Property evaluation temperature — use film temperature T_film = (T_s + T_∞)/2, not T_∞. Air's ν changes 50% from 25 °C to 100 °C.
  • Mixing Nu local vs Nu average — local Nu_x predicts h at position x along a plate; average Nu_L predicts the area-averaged h. Use average for design heat-load calcs.
  • Transition region (Re ~ 10⁵) — correlations break down between laminar and turbulent. Mixed correlation: Nu = 0.037 · Re^0.8 · Pr^(1/3) − 871·Pr^(1/3). Worth using for plates that cross the transition.
  • "Just add a bigger fan" diminishing returns — h ∝ V^0.5 for laminar, V^0.8 for turbulent. After turbulent transition, doubling V gives ~75% more h. The first fan upgrade pays back; the third rarely does.
  • Forgetting fin efficiency — h is computed for the fin surface but the fin tip is colder than the base, so effective area is less. Multiply by η_fin (or use fin-array formulas like tanh(mL)/(mL)).
  • Neglecting radiation — for surfaces above ~60 °C with low-speed flow, radiation can add 5-10 W/m²·K of effective h. Surface emissivity (matte black ≈ 0.95, polished Al ≈ 0.05) matters.
