Thermal resistance networks — Tj from datasheet to ambient

Heat moves like current through a string of resistors. Power dissipated by an IC drops a voltage (temperature) across each thermal resistance — junction-to-case (datasheet), case-to-sink (TIM), sink-to-ambient (heat sink rating). In steady state, sum the series resistances, multiply by power, add ambient: Tj = T_a + P · ΣΘ. (Transient response would add thermal capacitances Cth in parallel with each Rth — RC ladder analog.) Slide the inputs; the chain updates and turns red if you blow past Tj_max.

Worked datasheet example

RP2350 in the fold-p4-pico cyberdeck: Tj_max = 85 °C, P = 0.8 W, Θ_jc ≈ 18 °C/W, Θ_cs ≈ 5 °C/W (via array + paste), Θ_sa ≈ 30 °C/W (small PCB pour, sealed enclosure), T_a = 35 °C.

Tj = 35 + 0.8 · (18 + 5 + 30) = 35 + 0.8 · 53 = 77.4 °C — 7.6 °C margin. Tight.

If the user puts the cyberdeck in a hot car (T_a → 55 °C), Tj climbs to 97.4 °C — past Tj_max. Drop Θ_sa to 15 (better pour + vent) and Tj = 55 + 0.8·(18 + 5 + 15) = 55 + 30.4 = 85.4 °C — still 0.4 °C over Tj_max = 85 °C. The pour-and-vent fix isn't enough on its own: you'd also need a lower-Θ_jc package, forced airflow (Θ_sa < 10), or to accept de-rated performance above ambient. That's why "multiply margin in" matters — designing exactly at the limit leaves you no headroom for TIM ageing, dust, or a slightly hotter day.

What to notice

  • Heat = current, ΔT = voltage, Θ = resistance. Once you accept the analogy, every EE intuition transfers. Series resistors add. Parallel resistors combine as reciprocals. KCL holds at every node.
  • Θ_jc is fixed by the package. You can't change it without changing the IC. The only knobs you control after part selection are Θ_cs (TIM choice and pressure) and Θ_sa (heat sink and airflow).
  • Thermal vias work in parallel. A standard 0.3 mm drill / 1.6 mm board / 1 oz plated via has barrel-only Θ ≈ 120 °C/W (from L/(k·A) with A = π·d·wall ≈ 3.3×10⁻⁸ m²). Nine of those in parallel ≈ 13 °C/W — already an 8× reduction over a single via. Add a continuous Cu pour beneath the pad spreading into the ground plane and the effective system Θ drops further (this is where the often-quoted "~3 °C/W per via" actually originates — it's a *system* number, not a per-barrel number). The via array is why every QFN has a thermal pad with a via field beneath it.
  • The biggest Θ wins. Heat piles up at whatever resistance dominates. If Θ_sa = 30 and Θ_jc = 18, the sink limits you — a better sink helps. If Θ_jc were 100, no external fix would matter; you'd need to change the package.
  • Multiply margin in, not out. Don't design to Tj = Tj_max. Aim for Tj ≤ Tj_max − 15 °C at worst-case ambient. TIM ages, dust accumulates, ambient rises.
  • Tj_max is silicon-limited, not package-limited. Most ICs derate performance above ~85 °C and start failing reliability targets above 125 °C. The number on the datasheet is where the manufacturer stops guaranteeing operation.

Cheat sheet

FORMULAS

conduction
R_th = L / (k · A)
convection
R_th = 1 / (h · A)
series
Tj = T_amb + P · ΣR_th
parallel
1/R = Σ 1/R_i

MEMORIZE

  • Electrical analogy — heat ≡ current, ΔT ≡ V, R_th ≡ R.
  • Series adds, parallel reduces — vias in parallel are big wins.
  • Tj_max ≈ 85–125 °C typical IC silicon limit.

COMMON PITFALLS

  • Miss one stage → wildly wrong Tj — every interface counts.
  • TIM ages — pump-out and dry-out degrade R over years.
  • Don't design to Tj_max — leave 15–20 °C margin.
